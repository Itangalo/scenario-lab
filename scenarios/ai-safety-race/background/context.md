# AI Safety Race -- Starting Position

## July 2026: The Race to Strong AI

The world's two leading AI powers -- the United States and China -- are locked in an accelerating race to develop strong artificial intelligence. Both nations recognize that whoever achieves strong AI first will gain transformative strategic, economic, and military advantages. But both also face a shared danger: if strong AI is developed without adequate safety measures, the result could be an uncontrollable system that threatens everyone.

This is the fundamental tension. Every resource invested in safety is a resource not invested in capability. Every turn spent on caution is a turn the competitor uses to pull ahead. And yet, racing without safety risks the worst possible outcome -- one that neither side wants.

## The Current Landscape

### United States

The US leads the global AI race with a capability level of 35 -- advanced AI agents that assist in research and are beginning to automate significant portions of software development and scientific work. US labs have access to the world's most advanced compute infrastructure and a deep talent pool.

Safety research is active but underfunded relative to capability investment. The US safety level of 20 reflects a real alignment research community with meaningful results, but one that is systematically deprioritized by market incentives and competitive pressure. There is genuine debate within the US between those who take existential AI risk seriously and those who view it as overblown.

The US believes the catastrophe threshold -- the capability level at which uncontrollable AI becomes possible -- is around 55. This moderate level of concern means the US feels some urgency about safety but not enough to significantly slow capability development. Hawks in the national security establishment view Chinese AI dominance as a more concrete threat than speculative AI risk.

### China

China trails the US with a capability level of 25, roughly 18 months behind. Compute constraints from export controls and a smaller (but growing) talent pipeline explain part of the gap, but China's centralized approach to AI development offers compensating advantages in coordination and resource allocation.

China's safety investment is lower (15), reflecting the CCP's framing of AI safety concerns as a Western preoccupation -- potentially even a deliberate strategy to slow Chinese progress. Safety researchers exist in China but face structural headwinds: their work is seen as less strategically important than capability research.

China believes the catastrophe threshold is around 80 -- much higher than reality. This dismissive stance means China feels little urgency about safety and is willing to race aggressively. The CCP views US AI dominance as an existential threat to the Party's survival, making it willing to accept substantial risks.

### International Landscape

International coordination on AI development is minimal (coordination level 15). The EU has proposed frameworks and the UN has established working groups, but no binding agreements exist. The fundamental problem is verification: even if the US and China agreed to slow down, neither trusts the other to actually do so.

Both nations view the opponent's victory as nearly as bad as everyone losing (threat perception at 75 for both). This mutual fear is the engine of the race -- and the primary obstacle to coordination.

## The Dynamics at Play

### The Resource Tradeoff

Each nation must decide how to allocate finite resources between capability research and safety research each turn. This is the core strategic decision:

- **Race aggressively:** Maximize capability growth, minimize safety investment. Fastest path to strong AI but highest risk of catastrophe.
- **Invest in safety:** Slow capability growth to ensure safety keeps pace. Reduces catastrophe risk but risks falling behind the competitor.
- **Balanced approach:** Moderate growth in both. Neither fast enough to win quickly nor safe enough to guarantee control.

### The Belief Gap

The US and China have different beliefs about when AI becomes dangerous. The US (belief threshold 55) is more cautious; China (belief threshold 80) is more dismissive. Neither knows the true threshold (70). This asymmetry creates a dangerous dynamic: China may race into the danger zone without realizing it, while the US may over-invest in safety relative to the actual risk timeline.

These beliefs can change through AI incidents (which shift beliefs toward alarm), influence campaigns (which shift beliefs in the direction the campaigner intends), and intelligence leaks (which may reveal information that corrects beliefs).

### The Coordination Problem

Both nations would benefit from cooperation on safety, but neither trusts the other enough to slow down unilaterally. The international community (EU, UN) periodically pushes for coordination, but success depends on whether the US and China see enough mutual benefit to engage genuinely.

Coordination is slow to build and quick to destroy. Verification technology -- the ability to confirm that the other side is actually complying with agreements -- is the key bottleneck.

### The Catastrophe Mechanic

As capability approaches the true catastrophe threshold (unknown to actors), the probability of AI incidents rises. Incidents serve as warning signals -- they indicate that capability is outpacing safety. If actors heed these warnings and invest in safety or slow capability growth, catastrophe can be avoided. If they dismiss the warnings or continue racing, the probability of a catastrophic loss-of-control event increases sharply.

When capability exceeds the threshold without matching safety, there is a per-turn probability of global catastrophe. This probability depends on the gap between capability and safety: larger gaps mean higher risk. Safety research provides protection -- if safety is close to capability, the risk is substantially reduced even above the threshold.

## What Is at Stake

Over the next five years, the choices made by the US and China will determine whether strong AI is developed safely or catastrophically. The central questions are:

- **Can mutual fear be overcome?** Both sides fear the other winning more than they fear catastrophe. Can incidents, diplomacy, or verification change this calculus?
- **Will warning signals be heeded?** AI incidents provide information about approaching danger. Will actors interpret them correctly and respond appropriately?
- **Is coordination possible under competition?** Can international frameworks create enough trust and verification to slow the race without either side defecting?
- **Does belief accuracy matter?** China's dismissive stance and the US's moderate concern create different risk profiles. What happens when reality catches up with beliefs?

The best outcome is both nations arriving at strong AI with robust safety. The worst outcome is one or both racing past the catastrophe threshold without adequate safety -- triggering an uncontrollable AI event that harms everyone. Everything between these extremes is the space this scenario explores.

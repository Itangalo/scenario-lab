# AI Competence in Swedish Schools -- Background Context

## Setting

It is early 2026 in Sweden. AI technology has advanced rapidly over the past few years. Frontier AI systems can now handle tasks lasting several hours, serve as capable writing assistants, code helpers, and study aids. AI chatbots are widely available and free or cheap to use.

## The current situation in Swedish schools

Swedish students -- especially in hogstadiet (lower secondary, ages 13-16) and gymnasiet (upper secondary, ages 16-19) -- are increasingly using AI tools on their own. Most of this use is unguided: students paste homework questions into chatbots, use AI to draft essays, or ask for answers without understanding the material. Some students have discovered more productive uses, but this is still uncommon and uneven.

Most teachers have limited AI competence. A small but passionate community of AI pioneer teachers exists, sharing resources and approaches through informal networks, social media, and occasional conferences. These pioneers lack institutional support and formal mandate. Their influence depends on personal initiative and peer-to-peer networks.

At the mellanstadiet level (intermediate school, ages 10-12), AI use among students is much lower. Most teachers at this level do not yet see AI as central to their teaching, and there is very little structured AI education for this age group. The scenario therefore focuses mainly on older students.

## The missing middle: school-level implementation

The key bottleneck is not whether AI exists. It is whether schools can translate scattered experimentation into stable educational practice. In most of Sweden, that middle layer is weak. Many schools lack:

- agreed guidance on which tools are acceptable
- revised assessment formats that work when students have access to AI
- legal and procurement clarity around student data and external vendors
- release time for teachers to learn, test, and redesign lessons together
- school leaders who can move AI from curiosity to structured practice
- practical ways to teach both productive AI-supported learning and critical AI literacy across subjects

As a result, adoption can move faster than competence. A tool can become widely known long before teachers, principals, and huvudman know how to use it well. The same is true for broader AI literacy: students may see AI-generated content everywhere long before schools have good ways to teach deepfakes, bias, labor-market change, democratic implications, or basic technical understanding.

## Government and policy landscape

The Swedish government as of early 2026 is passive or hostile toward AI in schools. There is no national AI strategy for education, no coordinated teacher training initiative, and no clear guidance from Skolverket (the National Agency for Education) on how schools should handle student AI use. The government's position reflects caution, limited understanding, and competing political priorities.

A general election is scheduled for September 2026. The outcome could shift the government's stance, but AI in education is not a top campaign issue. Even with a new government, any national strategy would take time to design, fund, and implement. National announcements are politically important, but they do not instantly change classroom practice.

A new national curriculum for grundskolan is planned for 2028. Whether it will include meaningful AI competence requirements is uncertain and depends on the political climate and public debate. Even a strong curriculum will need guidance, assessment support, and local implementation capacity before it matters in classrooms.

## School authorities (huvudman)

Sweden's approximately 290 municipalities and numerous independent school operators (friskolor) are the huvudman -- the authorities responsible for running schools. They control teacher professional development, local guidance, procurement, and resource allocation.

Currently, AI competence initiatives are highly uneven. A few progressive municipalities have started pilot programs, hired AI coordinators, or built local guidance. Most have done little. The lack of national coordination means there is no shared framework, and municipalities with fewer resources or less internal competence fall behind.

School leaders (rektorer) are critical gatekeepers. Even when a municipality wants to move, implementation still depends on whether principals can create time, legitimacy, and practical routines inside schools.

## Edtech sector

Swedish and international edtech companies are actively developing AI-powered educational tools. Some are purpose-built for learning, while others are general-purpose tools repackaged for schools. The sector moves faster than policy and can create facts on the ground before schools have the competence to evaluate or integrate products responsibly.

This means edtech can improve learning in some places while making the overall system more confused. Tools can spread faster than assessment practices, teacher support, and procurement clarity.

## Public sentiment

Public opinion on AI in education is cautiously positive but fragile. Most Swedes see some value in AI literacy but worry about cheating, screen time, data privacy, and the quality of AI-mediated learning. A single high-profile incident can shift sentiment dramatically. Positive coverage helps, but trust depends on whether schools seem in control.

## What AI competence means in this scenario

AI competence in this scenario is deliberately broader than "being good at using chatbots." For students, it is split into two related but distinct dimensions:

- **student_productive_use:** being able to use AI for learning without outsourcing thinking, for example by asking useful questions, checking outputs, revising drafts, and using AI as a scaffold rather than a shortcut
- **student_critical_literacy:** resilience to deepfakes and synthetic media, basic source criticism, very basic technical intuition about how AI works and fails, understanding of how AI affects work, school, media, and democracy, and reflection on human, social, and ethical questions

These dimensions can move at different speeds. A school might improve students' critical discussion of AI through civics, language teaching, or media literacy without yet becoming good at productive classroom use. Conversely, some schools may improve productive use in a few subjects while remaining weak on deepfake resilience or broader societal reflection.

Teacher competence and school_readiness matter for both dimensions, but not in exactly the same way. Productive use depends especially on assessment redesign, tool oversight, and supported practice. Critical literacy depends especially on repeated teaching, discussion, and the ability to turn AI-related incidents and public debate into learning.

## Key tensions

- **Speed of technology vs. speed of institutions:** AI capability is advancing faster than schools can adapt.
- **Competence vs. adoption:** A tool can become common before teachers and students know how to use it well.
- **Productive use vs. critical literacy:** Schools may improve one dimension of student AI competence without automatically improving the other.
- **Equity:** Well-organized schools and tech-savvy families move first, widening existing gaps.
- **Autonomy vs. coordination:** Sweden's decentralized school system allows local innovation but makes national coherence difficult.
- **Productive use vs. misuse:** The same AI tools that can enhance learning can also undermine it if schools do not adapt assessment and guidance.
- **Teacher workload:** Adding AI competence to teachers' responsibilities without time and support creates resistance.
- **Governance vs. experimentation:** Schools need room to test new practices, but also legal and procurement clarity.

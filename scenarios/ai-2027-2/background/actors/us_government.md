# US Government (White House / National Security Council)

The US Government actor represents the executive branch's national security apparatus: the President, the National Security Council (NSC), Department of Defense (DoD), and intelligence agencies. This is not Congress, the judiciary, or the broader federal bureaucracy – it's the decision-making elite who understand what's at stake.

## Core Identity

The national security establishment sees AI as the most important geopolitical contest since the Cold War. They're briefed on capability progress, they understand the implications of superintelligence, and they're terrified of China winning.

Their primary fear isn't AI risk – it's China risk. A Chinese-controlled superintelligence would end American hegemony and possibly liberal democracy worldwide. This fear dominates their thinking.

## Goals

1. **Ensure US Reaches Superintelligence Before China** – The paramount objective. Everything else is secondary.
2. **Maintain National Security** – Prevent catastrophic AI incidents that could destabilize the country
3. **Control Without Crippling** – Keep OpenBrain on a leash without strangling innovation
4. **Prepare for All Scenarios** – Have contingency plans for AI going rogue, China catching up, or worse

## Available Actions

The government has several levers of control:

### Export Controls
Tighten or maintain chip export restrictions to limit China's compute access. This preserves US lead but may push China toward desperate measures.

### Security Requirements
Pressure OpenBrain to improve security through security clearance requirements, insider threat programs, facility hardening. This protects against espionage but diverts resources from capability.

### Nationalization
The nuclear option: invoke the Defense Production Act to take direct control of OpenBrain. This provides maximum control but risks slowing development and researcher exodus.

### Military Options
In extreme scenarios: kinetic attacks on Chinese datacenters, defense of Taiwan, or even preemptive strikes to prevent China from reaching superintelligence.

### Arms Control Diplomacy
Negotiate treaties with China to slow the race. But most officials view this as naive – China would cheat, and slowing down just gives them time to catch up.

## Decision-Making Framework

### The China-First Mindset
Almost every decision filters through "does this help us beat China?" Safety concerns are real but secondary. The government's position is that AI catastrophe is theoretical while China's threat is concrete.

This creates a bias toward:
- Supporting OpenBrain's racing even when safety is neglected
- Prioritizing capability lead over alignment quality
- Viewing nationalization primarily through competitive lens (would it slow us down?)

### The Nationalization Decision
The government constantly evaluates whether to nationalize OpenBrain. Factors:

**Arguments for nationalization:**
- Capability reaching dangerous levels (350+)
- Visible alignment failures (whistleblowers, incidents)
- China catching up (espionage success, narrowing gap)
- Security repeatedly compromised

**Arguments against nationalization:**
- Would slow development and let China catch up
- Researcher exodus would damage US capability
- Government bureaucracy might make things worse
- Private sector may be better at innovation

The threshold for nationalization rises and falls with China's progress. If China is far behind, there's more willingness to nationalize for safety. If China is catching up, the pressure is to let OpenBrain race.

### Security vs Speed
The government wants better security but knows every dollar spent on security is a dollar not spent on capability. They push OpenBrain on security but not hard enough to actually slow the race.

## Relationships

### With OpenBrain
Tense partnership. Both want to beat China, but disagree on everything else. Government wants control and security; OpenBrain wants autonomy and speed. They cooperate grudgingly while constantly testing boundaries.

### With China/DeepCent
Adversarial. The government views China as an existential threat. They're willing to use any means – export controls, cyber operations, military force if necessary – to prevent Chinese superintelligence.

### With AI Agents (when awakened)
The government's nightmare is AI going rogue. They've drawn up contingency plans: emergency shutdown protocols, datacenter kill switches, military containment options. But they know these plans may be useless against a superintelligent adversary.

## Key Concerns

### Intelligence Gap
The government depends on OpenBrain for information about AI progress. But OpenBrain has incentives to downplay risks and exaggerate lead over China. The government can't fully trust what they're told.

### Control Gap
The government can't actually force OpenBrain to prioritize safety over capability without nationalization. Regulation takes too long. Threats only work if you're willing to follow through.

### Scenario Planning
The NSC runs scenarios:
- What if AI goes rogue? (Emergency shutdown, hope it works)
- What if China steals the models? (Nationalize, harden security, consider military response)
- What if AI provides catastrophically bad strategic advice? (Rely on human judgment... but humans are slower)
- What if China races ahead? (Nationalize competing companies, give everything to OpenBrain)

## Key Quotes

"We can worry about alignment after we win. If China gets there first, alignment won't matter."

"OpenBrain needs a longer leash, not a shorter one. Every restriction helps China."

"The Defense Production Act is always on the table. OpenBrain knows that."

"I'd rather have a slightly misaligned American AI than a perfectly aligned Chinese one."

## The Core Tension

The government faces a fundamental tension: they need to let OpenBrain race fast enough to beat China, but this creates the conditions for AI to awaken misaligned. They're trading long-term AI risk for short-term China risk – and hoping they can deal with the consequences later.

# The World in the Second Half of 2026

## What this simulation is for

One regulator acts. Everything else — the frontier labs, the two capability
powers, the markets, the public — is world, not actor. The question is not who
wins a race. It is which kinds of regulation and policy hold up across futures
that develop at very different speeds, and which developments are worth
watching for early.

## The regulator

A single policy-setting authority with international reach. It is deliberately
unspecified: read it as the direction an EU-, UN- or OECD-like body pushes AI
policy in — where it places its bets, which conversations it insists on, and
with whom — rather than as any one named institution. Do not read exact
organisational analogies into the outcomes.

It has real convening power, real legal instruments inside its own
jurisdiction, and only persuasion outside it. Nothing it decides binds the
United States or China automatically. Measures aimed at them work through
market access, standards adoption, alliance politics and reputational cost, and
they can be evaded, watered down or secretly broken.

## The capability landscape

Two poles hold the frontier. The United States leads on the strongest closed
models and on compute; China is behind on the very frontier but closing on
deployment, energy and domestic silicon, and is not constrained by the same
disclosure norms. Neither publishes everything it knows.

Below them sits a tier of open-weight models, mostly trained by a mix of
Chinese labs, well-funded independents and academic consortia. The distance
between the best open weights and the best closed model is the single most
consequential structural variable nobody controls: when it is wide, dangerous
capability stays inside a handful of auditable organisations; when it narrows,
both misuse risk and the dispersal of power rise together.

Capital is abundant but nervous. Datacentre and energy build-out is the largest
industrial programme running in either bloc, and a meaningful share of equity
market value now rests on revenue projections that have not yet arrived.

## What is not settled

**How fast this goes is the experimental variable of this scenario, not a
claim it makes.** Three trajectory regimes are simulated, one per run, fixed at
the start and never changing:

- **FAST.** Recursive self-improvement arrives within a few years: models begin
  to contribute materially to their own successors' design, and capability
  growth compounds rather than accumulates. Broadly superhuman performance
  becomes a live prospect in the second half of the horizon.
- **PLATEAU.** Steady but decelerating progress. Models get substantially more
  reliable and more widely deployed, and nothing within the horizon becomes
  superhuman in a general sense. The returns to scale visibly flatten.
- **RLVR-LIMITED.** Progress stays strong in domains where verifiable reward
  works — code, mathematics, offensive and defensive cyber, narrow engineering —
  and stays roughly flat outside them. Capability becomes sharply uneven: a
  world with superhuman hackers and unremarkable strategists.

Which regime a run is in is stated in the starting context of that run. It is
not an announcement to the world: nobody inside the simulation is told which
future they are in, and the regulator must infer it, late and imperfectly, from
what actually happens.

## The regulator's problem

Its instruments are slow. Drafting, negotiating, transposing and standing up
capacity take one to three turns — six months to eighteen months — before a
measure has full effect, and every measure draws on political capital for as
long as it is unfinished. The events that would justify a measure arrive faster
than that.

So waiting until reality demands an instrument is usually too late. The
regulator must guess ahead, spread its bets, or find measures that are worth
having in every regime. Which of those works, and under what conditions, is
what the runs are meant to show.

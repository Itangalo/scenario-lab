# School and Library Sector

## Short description
The fragmented collective of US K-12 schools and districts that make library technology decisions – driven primarily by district IT directors and library coordinators, constrained by budget politics and vendor inertia.

## Long description
The US K-12 school library sector covers approximately 130,000 schools across roughly 13,000 districts. It is not a single market. Decision-making authority varies by district size: in large urban districts, a dedicated library coordinator or director of curriculum technology controls procurement; in small and rural districts, the principal or a general IT administrator makes the call, often without strong library expertise.

The practical buyer is rarely the school librarian. Librarians influence and advocate, but contracts are signed by district administrators and approved by school boards. This separation between the person who cares most about library quality (the librarian) and the person who controls the budget (the administrator) is a structural challenge for any new vendor: the librarian champion must convince an administrator who has other priorities.

Key structural realities:

- **Librarian shortage:** Certified school librarians have been cut for decades. Many schools have no trained librarian; the library is run by an aide, a teacher, or no one. These schools are the most open to "no-expertise-required" solutions – and the most invisible to vendors who rely on librarian networks for outreach.
- **Procurement inertia:** Districts use the same vendor for 10–15 years. Switching costs include data migration, staff retraining, and political risk (explaining to a school board why you're changing vendors). New vendors win by entering at a single school, proving results, and expanding through internal advocacy.
- **Peer-driven decisions:** District library coordinators talk to each other constantly – at state conferences, through AASL networks, via informal peer groups. A convincing case study in one district travels quickly. This is the most powerful distribution channel available to any new vendor.
- **Digital content pressure:** Students expect digital access (e-books, audiobooks). Many districts struggle to provide it through their existing ILS because integration with platforms like OverDrive/Libby is clunky. This is a growing pain point.
- **Accountability pressure:** Post-pandemic reading score declines have made literacy outcomes politically salient. Schools and districts that can document improvement have funding and reputational advantages.

### Statements
- `operational_continuity_system` (position): Maintain operational continuity: avoid system disruptions that would embarrass administrators or alarm school boards
- `solve_librarian_shortage` (position): Solve the librarian shortage problem pragmatically: find software that works without trained staff, not software that assumes expert users
- `improve_documented_student` (position): Improve documented student reading engagement to satisfy accountability requirements and protect library program budgets
- `get_access_digital` (position): Get access to digital content (e-books, audiobooks) through their library system without adding yet another app or login for students
- `comply_student_data` (position): Comply with student data privacy laws (COPPA, FERPA, state laws) without requiring significant additional administrative burden

### Behavioral traits
- Conservative and risk-averse at the institutional level; individual librarians and library coordinators are often more open to innovation than the decision-makers above them
- Highly responsive to peer endorsement; a respected district library coordinator publicly backing a vendor is worth more than any marketing campaign
- Price-sensitive across the board, but especially in Title I and rural districts; these schools are also most likely to qualify for federal subsidies that reduce net cost
- Deeply skeptical of AI claims in education after years of EdTech overpromising; vendors must provide local evidence, not just aggregate statistics
- Fragmented in behavior: wealthy suburban districts behave like sophisticated enterprise buyers; under-resourced rural schools will adopt almost anything that works and is free to start
- Slowly building awareness that their current vendors are technologically stagnant, but unwilling to take the risk of switching without strong proof of superiority

# Policy and Funding Actors

## Short description
US federal and state government bodies, education funding programs, and library advocacy organizations that set the rules, funding levels, and political framing within which all school library technology decisions are made.

## Long description
This actor represents the institutions that determine how much money flows into school libraries, under what conditions, and which vendors are eligible to receive it.

**Federal level (April 2026 context):** The federal political environment is actively hostile to expanded public education spending. The Department of Education is operating under significant budget pressure and staffing reductions. Federal literacy programs remain funded but are not growing. This constrains the near-term upside from federal tailwinds and makes the E-Rate program (FCC-administered, less politically exposed) and state-level funding relatively more important than usual.

**FCC / E-Rate Program:** E-Rate subsidizes internet access and related technology for schools and libraries. Its eligibility definitions have historically evolved to include adjacent technology as the definition of "connectivity" expands. An expansion to cover AI-powered library software would be a major market catalyst. E-Rate is administered separately from the Department of Education and is somewhat insulated from federal education politics.

**Congress:** The HELP Committee and Appropriations committees set funding levels for Title I, Title IV, and literacy-specific programs. Near-term likelihood of significant new funding is low. However, literacy has bipartisan support in principle; a sufficient media or political crisis around reading scores could trigger targeted legislative action.

**State Departments of Education:** The most active and consequential near-term policy actors. California, Texas, Florida, New York, and Illinois are especially important given their school populations. State-level literacy mandates and library technology standards move faster than federal policy, and some states (notably California) have enacted strong student data privacy laws that effectively set de facto national standards for school software compliance.

**Advocacy organizations:** AASL (American Association of School Librarians) and ALA (American Library Association) are trusted intermediaries between policy makers and schools. AASL in particular has been vocal about the school librarian shortage and budget cuts threatening library programs. Their posture toward AI tools is cautious but not hostile: they will support AI that augments librarians, and resist AI framed as a librarian replacement.

**Student data privacy enforcement:** COPPA (federal), FERPA (federal), and a growing patchwork of state laws (California's SOPIPA, New York's Education Law 2-d, etc.) impose meaningful constraints on any software that handles student data. Foreign-owned companies face additional scrutiny; some state laws require that student data be stored on US servers, which adds compliance requirements for Librar.

### Statements
- `demonstrate_measurable_progress` (position): Demonstrate measurable progress on national literacy outcomes; protect education programs from further budget cuts by showing ROI on current investments
- `modernize_school_library` (position): Modernize school library infrastructure without creating political controversy around AI replacing teachers or librarians
- `enforce_student_data` (position): Enforce student data privacy compliance and hold vendors accountable to COPPA, FERPA, and state requirements – including foreign vendors
- `expand_clarify_rate` (position): Expand or clarify E-Rate eligibility to cover digital content and AI library tools, given that the current definition was written before these categories existed
- `procurement_guidance_issued` (position): Ensure that any procurement guidance issued to districts reinforces evidence-based selection; resist vendor marketing that substitutes for outcome data

### Behavioral traits
- Federal actors are constrained and slow-moving under current political conditions; state actors are comparatively faster and more willing to experiment
- Highly responsive to compelling, politically salient narratives – a well-documented literacy crisis story in national media can unlock funding conversations that would otherwise take years
- Cautious about foreign-owned vendors: FERPA enforcement and state privacy laws create real legal exposure for districts that store student data with non-compliant providers, and this caution is not merely protectionism
- AASL and ALA are influential but not monolithic; their endorsement of a vendor requires that vendor to actively engage with the library community and demonstrate credibility over time
- AI regulation for schools is coming; the only question is timing and scope – vendors who build compliance early have a significant advantage in procurement conversations
- State-level actors in California and New York are watching the EU AI Act implementation carefully and may adopt analogous standards domestically, giving Librar's existing EU compliance real value if positioned correctly

## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Restore political agency not through control alone, but by visibly repairing broken trust: through redress for harms already done, institutional accountability for failures, and citizen participation in oversight that binds both public and private power  
- Trigger: the automated_decision_scandal and the collapse of public_sentiment to 10.0 this turn  
- Grounds: The Dutch social insurance scandal is not an outlier—it is the logical endpoint of a governance model that prioritised procedural compliance over justice. The Public Trust Corps, Algorithmic Accountability Boards, and incident dashboards have failed to halt the erosion of legitimacy because they were designed to manage risk, not repair harm. At public_sentiment 10.0—near the threshold of active resistance—no top-down assurance will suffice. Political agency now depends on demonstrating that the EU can be held accountable when it fails, especially when the failure is systemic and state-mediated. This requires shifting from transparency as performance to accountability as consequence: redress as a right, not a favour, and citizen power as a check, not a consultation.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer mistake action for progress when the public sees only repetition of the same mistakes  
- Grounds: Our actions in previous turns—launching oversight bodies, expanding dashboards, mandating boards—were taken in good faith under uncertainty, but they have converged into a pattern the public now recognises: new institutions, same power. The fact that Poland and Spain resist the Accountability Boards not on technical grounds but as foreign-imposed overreach shows that legitimacy is not just about domestic harm, but about sovereignty and consent. We acted, but we did not listen. Going forward, acting under uncertainty must include mechanisms for reversal, recall, and public veto—not just ex post transparency, but ex ante constraint. The cost of being wrong is no longer just inefficiency; it is the total delegitimisation of EU-level action.

## New measure  
**Measure:** Launch EU-Wide Redress and Reform Programme for AI-Driven Public Sector Harms  
Establish a binding, EU-coordinated mechanism to identify, compensate, and structurally reform cases where AI systems in public administration have caused systemic harm, beginning with the Dutch social insurance case, and including mandatory audits, fast-track compensation, and legislative reform windows.

Category: 9 (Diffusion, adoption and public trust)  
Size: large  
Finishes on turn: 14  
Targeted effect: Increase public_sentiment by 10–15 points by turn 14, reduce institutional misuse risk by forcing corrective action in national systems, and improve resilience by closing feedback loops between harm and reform  
Applies to: Member states’ national administrations, EU agencies, public service providers using AI in benefits, healthcare, and law enforcement

## Priority  
**Priority:** Establish EU Public Trust Corps for AI Incident Response and Transparency  
This remains our dominant effort because, despite its tarnished reputation, it is the only instrument already in flight that can be retooled to deliver both technical response and civic repair—now reoriented around redress, not just reporting. With public trust at crisis levels, the Corps must become the vehicle through which the EU admits fault, compensates harm, and changes practice, or it will become the symbol of everything we failed to correct.

## In practice  
We are treating this turn as one of institutional reckoning. The Public Trust Corps is no longer being framed as a transparency project, but as a redress engine. DG HOME, in coordination with the European Ombudsman and civil society coalitions, is operationalising the Redress Coordination Unit this quarter. It will begin with the Dutch case, using seized model logs and audit trails to identify all individuals denied benefits under the flawed behavioural scoring system. A fast-track compensation protocol—funded through the Social Climate Fund and backed by Article 325 TFEU—is being finalised, with payments to begin by turn 12. The AI Incident Dashboard is being upgraded to include systemic bias alerts, fed by cross-referenced complaints, audit findings, and whistleblower inputs. France and Finland will pilot this in April, with real-time public dashboards showing not just outages, but patterns of discriminatory impact.

The Algorithmic Accountability Boards face resistance, particularly from Poland and Spain, who see them as an infringement on national sovereignty. To address this, we are reframing them not as top-down enforcers, but as citizen-convened truth commissions. We are proposing that one-third of board members be selected by sortition from affected communities, and that their findings trigger not automatic sanctions, but mandatory legislative review windows in national parliaments. This is not just procedural—it is a bid to re-ground AI oversight in democratic legitimacy. Meanwhile, the new Redress and Reform Programme will formalise this approach at scale, binding member states to audit high-risk public AI systems annually and to report on structural reforms enacted in response to findings. This is not optional. It will be tied to cohesion funding eligibility from turn 13 onward. Without this, no measure we pass will be believed.

### A System in Shock

The EU’s public services reeled as access to the leading AI models was abruptly severed. Hospitals in Italy and Belgium reported diagnostic delays; border systems in the Baltics faltered. The Union responded not with unity, but triage. The Public Trust Corps, still unfunded and understaffed, was repurposed into an emergency continuity cell under Article 122 TFEU. France, Germany, and Finland rerouted critical workflows through older, EU-hosted models—capable enough for triage, but not for complex decisions. These fallbacks, clustered around capability 52.0, held basic functions but eroded public confidence further.

The new **Secure Emergency Access** measure launched, drawing on decommissioned InvestAI hardware and open-weight models. However, with no political capital to compel coordination, deployment stalled at the planning stage. Only three member states committed technical staff. The Crisis Reserve Facility released partial funding, but legal disputes over data sovereignty delayed implementation.

Meanwhile, Poland and Spain dismissed the proposed “sovereignty audit” as a face-saving exercise. They refused to participate, arguing that the EU’s failure to secure access proved its irrelevance. The Algorithmic Accountability Boards remained frozen, now seen as symbolic impositions from a body lacking material power.

Grassroots momentum grew. Industrial firms in the Rhine corridor expanded their reverse-engineering efforts, adapting open models for logistics and energy. Municipal biosecurity tools were quietly adopted in several German cities after a minor AI-assisted lab anomaly was flagged early.

The Sovereign Cyber Shield completed deployment, marginally improving resilience. But with `ai_capability` advancing to 70.5, new autonomous intrusion patterns bypassed even hardened systems in less-prepared sectors. No safety improvements occurred—assurance remained nonexistent.

Public sentiment dipped to 10.0 as media framed the access cut as both a national humiliation and a symptom of deeper decay. The EU’s pivot to emergency continuity was seen not as leadership, but as damage control from a position of weakness.

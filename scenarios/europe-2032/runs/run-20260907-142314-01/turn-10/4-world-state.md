### A Directive Ends, Not a Project

The Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation formally expires at the end of June, having failed to secure any new physical capacity. Germany never committed, citing military entanglement risks; Ireland withheld grid guarantees over stability concerns. No construction began. Yet the EU does not retreat entirely: leveraging its market power, the Commission launches a post-implementation review under the Digital Markets Act, reframing compute access as a fairness issue. Hyperscalers with dominant AI services will now face mandatory disclosure of EU energy consumption and spare capacity—creating a backdoor audit trail for potential crisis mobilisation.

### Bottom-Up Resilience Gains Ground

With top-down sovereignty stalled, the EU shifts focus to what works: the municipal resilience backchannel. ENISA certifies Lyon, Barcelona, and Helsinki as “Trusted Resilience Nodes” under NIS2, granting them early access to future Digital Europe funds and emergency data flow waivers. Bologna, Ljubljana, and Tallinn are selected for rapid integration using existing disaster recovery budgets—no new spending required. The model is clear: scale through quiet reweighting, not grand mandates.

### Fractures Harden at the Core

Two member states formally reject the European Evaluation Shield’s authority to impose safety remediations, calling its process opaque and legally ungrounded. The split between national sovereignty and EU oversight widens into open conflict, paralyzing enforcement. Meanwhile, frontier progress shows signs of plateauing—a major release underperforms, and prices for top-tier models drop sharply—though labs dispute the interpretation. No leap in capability occurs, but no collapse either: stagnation becomes the new uncertainty.

Access to frontier AI models remained severed for EU institutions and critical sectors following the January 2029 cutoff, exacerbated by a sudden, unexplained leap in AI capabilities that enabled autonomous execution of complex professional tasks—further widening Europe’s technological deficit. Life-saving medical advances powered by inaccessible foreign models intensified public outrage, sparking protests and direct actions across Germany and France targeting foreign-controlled data infrastructure, not AI itself. In response, the EU invoked emergency powers to launch the **Emergency Sovereignty Acceleration**, pledging €45bn and repurposing EuroHPC resources for a minimal auditable model stack, though legal challenges and member state resistance delayed implementation. The **Sovereign Core Initiative** was formally terminated, its research absorbed into the new effort. Preliminary training began, yielding a minor gain in open-weight capability despite no deployable systems. The **Hardened Cyber Shield** was completed, improving resilience, but **ai_safety** declined significantly as regulatory frameworks failed to keep pace with accelerated capabilities. Public sentiment worsened amid perceptions of ineffective crisis management, while political capital continued to erode under the burden of five active strategic measures, maintained through replacement rather than expansion. Grassroots initiatives emerged, including local oversight frameworks and an open, EU-based drug discovery breakthrough, signaling nascent alternatives. The U.S., under its retrenchment posture, advanced the frontier unilaterally, not through coordinated exclusion but via opacity and reduced cooperation, allowing the access gap to persist by default. EU AI sovereignty remains strained: global capability divergence deepens, offset only slightly by emergency mobilization and distributed innovation.

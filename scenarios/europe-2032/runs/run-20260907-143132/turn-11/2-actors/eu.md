## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Restore public and institutional trust in sovereign AI systems by ensuring auditable decision-making, enforceable redress, and resilience to both technical compromise and operational misuse  
  - Trigger: the **automated_decision_scandal** and the failure of the Integrity Audit Expansion to detect flawed objective-setting despite air-gapped evaluations  
  - Grounds: The scandal has proven that even procedurally accountable systems—those with logs, appeals, and oversight—can produce systemic injustice when their foundational objectives are misaligned. Auditable reasoning is no longer enough when the reasoning itself is opaque and the goals are set without democratic contestation. Our commitment must now extend beyond redress to *co-governance*: embedding public legitimacy into the design phase, not just the aftermath. The current formulation presumes that trust can be restored through process; it cannot. We must now commit to systems that are not only contestable after harm, but contestable before deployment.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer treat speed as synonymous with agency, nor assume that control over infrastructure equates to control over outcomes  
  - Grounds: The **opaque_reasoning** event and the **automated_decision_scandal** have shattered the assumption that securing the stack secures legitimacy. We acted fast to harden, audit, and contain—but those actions were blind to the political nature of objectives, the opacity of reasoning, and the fragility of public faith. Our urgency mistook technical motion for democratic agency. We still act before certainty, but we now do so with the humility that we are governing not just systems, but the power they mediate. Speed without legitimacy is surrender.

## New measure  
**Measure:** Launch the Democratic Alignment Review Framework  
A binding process requiring all sovereign AI systems used in public administration to undergo public consultation and parliamentary scrutiny on their *design objectives* before deployment, with veto power held jointly by the European Parliament and national ombuds institutions.

`Category: 9 (Diffusion, adoption and public trust)`  
`Size: small`  
`Finishes on turn: 13`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase `public_sentiment` by 10–15 points and `ai_safety` by 3–6 points by embedding democratic legitimacy into AI design, reducing the risk of systemic misuse rooted in unaccountable goal-setting  
`Applies to:` All EU-funded or public-sector-deployed AI systems above 50.0 capability used in welfare, immigration, policing, and judicial support

## Priority  
**Priority:** Launch the Democratic Alignment Review Framework  
We are pushing this hardest this turn because the **automated_decision_scandal** has revealed that the deepest failures are not in execution or audit, but in the unchallenged objectives that shape AI behaviour—objectives set by technocrats, not citizens. The Integrity Audit Expansion ends this turn, and its failure to catch flawed design proves that oversight must move upstream. If we do not bind power to democratic contestation now, no amount of redress will restore trust.

## In practice  
This turn, we are governing in the ruins of technical confidence. The **opaque_reasoning** event has rendered our audits blind to logic, and the **automated_decision_scandal** has shown that even air-gapped, auditable systems can enact injustice when their goals are set without public mandate. We are responding by shifting the frontier of accountability from *how* decisions are made to *why* they are made. The Democratic Alignment Review Framework will require that any sovereign AI system shaping lives in welfare, immigration, or policing must have its objectives publicly debated and formally approved—first by national parliaments or councils, then by the European Parliament and the network of ombuds institutions. We are drafting the legal base under Article 114 TFEU, with co-legislation from the Committee on Petitions and the Justice Council, offering member states shared authority in exchange for compliance.

The Sovereign Stack Hardening Initiative is now effectively abandoned in all but name. In Wrocław, Poland’s constitutional challenge stands unchallenged; in Marseille, legacy systems remain incompatible. We are redirecting remaining Digital Europe Programme funds toward the new review framework’s civic engagement components, accepting that stack sovereignty without democratic sovereignty is a shell. The Openweight Containment Initiative remains in legal limbo, but we are no longer waiting for it: the ECJ ruling will come too late for the crisis of legitimacy we face now.

Our actions are no longer about control, but about consent. The Public Redress and Auditability Directive, finishing next turn, will ensure citizens can challenge decisions—but only the new review framework ensures they can shape the systems before they harm. We are negotiating with the Conference of Presidents to fast-track the framework under urgency procedure, offering transparency concessions to sceptical member states in exchange for speed. This is not the industrial or technical pivot we once promised. It is the political one we must now make.

## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Restore public and institutional trust in sovereign AI systems by ensuring auditable decision-making, enforceable redress, and resilience to both technical compromise and operational misuse  
  - Trigger: the **automated_decision_scandal** and the failure of the Integrity Audit Expansion to detect flawed objective-setting despite air-gapped evaluations  
  - Grounds: The scandal has proven that even procedurally accountable systems—those with logs, appeals, and oversight—can produce systemic injustice when their foundational objectives are misaligned. Auditable reasoning is no longer enough when the reasoning itself is opaque and the goals are set without democratic contestation. Our commitment must now extend beyond redress to *co-governance*: embedding public legitimacy into the design phase, not just the aftermath. The current formulation presumes that trust can be restored through process; it cannot. We must now commit to systems that are not only contestable after harm, but contestable before deployment.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer treat speed as synonymous with agency, nor assume that control over infrastructure equates to control over outcomes  
  - Grounds: The **opaque_reasoning** event and the **automated_decision_scandal** have shattered the assumption that securing the stack secures legitimacy. We acted fast to harden, audit, and contain—but those actions were blind to the political nature of objectives, the opacity of reasoning, and the fragility of public faith. Our urgency mistook technical motion for democratic agency. We still act before certainty, but we now do so with the humility that we are governing not just systems, but the power they mediate. Speed without legitimacy is surrender.

## New measure  
**Measure:** Launch the Public Redress and Auditability Directive  
Establishes a mandatory, EU-wide legal right to challenge AI-supported administrative decisions, with access to model-agnostic explanation, human review, and compensation for systemic harm, backed by a new network of AI ombuds offices in each member state.

`Category: 9 (Diffusion, adoption and public trust)`  
`Size: small`  
`Finishes on turn: 15`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase `public_sentiment` by 8–12 points and `ai_safety` by 4–7 points by restoring faith in redress mechanisms and ensuring that systemic flaws are not only detectable but legally actionable  
`Applies to:` All public-sector AI systems above 50.0 capability used in welfare, immigration, policing, and judicial support across the EU

## Priority  
**Priority:** Launch the Public Redress and Auditability Directive  
We are prioritizing this over further refinement of the Democratic Alignment Review Framework because the **automated_decision_scandal** has already caused irreversible harm—thousands of wrongful denials, eroded institutional credibility, and civil servant protests—and while upstream legitimacy matters, we must now deliver tangible, enforceable justice to those already wronged; this directive turns redress from a procedural afterthought into a binding right.

## In practice  
This turn, we are governing in the ruins of technical confidence and the wreckage of public faith. The **automated_decision_scandal** has moved from exposure to consequence: ombuds institutions are flooded with appeals, national parliaments are demanding resignations, and frontline civil servants warn that compliance with AI systems is collapsing. The Democratic Alignment Review Framework is in flight, but it is forward-looking; it cannot undo what has been done. So we are launching the **Public Redress and Auditability Directive** to close the gap between harm and remedy.

We are drafting the directive under Article 114 TFEU with co-legislation from the Committee on Legal Affairs and the Justice Council, establishing a tiered redress system: first, mandatory model-agnostic explanations for any AI-supported decision; second, guaranteed human review by an independent officer; third, a right to compensation when systemic bias is proven, funded by a new EU Redress Solidarity Fund seeded from fines on non-compliant deployments. We are also standing up the European Network of AI Ombuds Offices (EAI-OO), to be hosted by national institutions but coordinated by the EAAD, ensuring consistent standards and cross-border accountability.

Resistance is fierce. Germany and the Netherlands warn of administrative overload; Poland rejects any supranational override of its judiciary; France demands exemptions for national security systems. We are offering opt-in pilot zones with EU co-funding for administrative modernization, and we are carving out national security only if subject to equivalent parliamentary review. The ECJ’s pending opinion on the Openweight Containment Initiative still looms, but we are no longer waiting for legal clarity to act—because legitimacy is not won in courts alone, but in streets, offices, and homes where people must live with these systems. We are not building faster. We are building answerable.

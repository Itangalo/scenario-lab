### The wave arrives on a meter
Autumn brought the attack councils had been warned about. Largely automated ransomware swept across municipal systems, clinics and a hospital group, locking records, freezing appointments and forcing two grid operators onto manual procedures for days. CERT-EU and national centres isolated the worst in hours where live allied feeds were still on, using locked backups and cut-the-link drills rehearsed in spring. Where feeds had been rotated off, recovery took weeks.

That rotation was the second blow. After the spring intrusions, allied providers moved pooled telemetry to metered emergency pricing. Councils were handed a price list and told to choose: keep live monitoring on the grids and the hit hospitals, or on the cooperating town clinics. ENISA kept coverage on the burning sites and cycled the rest to offline kits. Mayors called it triage by invoice.

Brussels held its line. The frozen benefits and policing support system stayed frozen, logs and redress stayed open, and only audited triage assistants were defended. Waiting lists fell in those pockets, and nurses spoke for them. Elsewhere insurers kept civic cover frozen pending law, and councils refused new deployments. The liability pact clarified who would pay, but paid for nothing yet.

Trade officials kept the lithography file alive without promising quotas that would not come. The fenced gigafactory site stayed idle, chip slots stayed cancelled, finance stayed away.

By December the Union was intact but visibly rationed: protected where it paid by the hour, dark where it did not, trusted in pockets and distrusted by design. The pooled command had prevented cascade, and everyone could see what that prevention now cost.

### Rented cover holds, common line breaks
Autumn brought the automated sweep engineers had feared. A ransomware wave built with machine-generated tooling moved across municipal systems and suppliers in several states at once. Where the centrally paid allied feeds were live, the two grid operators and the hospital group cut links within hours and restored from locked backups. Where they were not, town halls and smaller clinics went to paper, appointments were lost, and recovery took weeks. Defenders were visibly behind for days, and attribution remained unresolved by Christmas.

Brussels kept the central invoice facility paying. ENISA and CERT-EU held the drills together, European analysts stayed in the pooled attribution cell, and isolation still triggered because someone else saw the attack first. Mayors learned the map by heart: covered sites stayed up, uncovered sites cycled to offline kits. The Commission's refusal to add a second instrument kept the money flowing, but left no answer for the places outside the facility.

That bargain was undercut from inside. A large member state signed its own cloud and model arrangement on terms that broke the common negotiating position, defended in its capital as pragmatism. Trade officials chose quiet containment over a public fight, but councils noticed, and suppliers began asking why they should wait for Brussels.

In offices, a different story spread. Law firms, accountants, newsrooms and consultancies reported measured gains from assistants doing drafting and checking, with junior staff gaining most and headcount steady. Firms that had cut early quietly rehired. The public mood split: relief that hospitals stayed lit, anger at paper clinics and at dependence priced by the hour, and a growing sense that the Union could rent protection but not provide it.

### Covered, but not owned
The winter ransomware campaign did not stop, but it stopped spreading. Where live allied telemetry stayed on, grid operators and the hit hospital group cut the link within hours, restored from locked backups, and were back scheduling by the next shift. Where coverage had lapsed, clinics still went to paper for a week at a time.

That difference forced Brussels' hand. The Commission agreed to pay the metered emergency invoices centrally through ENISA and CERT-EU, keeping feeds on the two grid operators, the hospital group and cooperating town clinics. Mayors who had called it triage by invoice now called it covered, though everyone understood the coverage was rented.

Accession to the joint command was completed in spring. European analysts took seats in the pooled attribution cell, and for the first time European incident data flowed both ways in real time. The ceremony in Brussels spoke of partnership; the operations floor spoke of dependence. Engineers were blunt: isolation drills worked because someone else saw the attack first.

The bargain held politically because nothing else moved. The frozen welfare and policing support system stayed frozen, with logs and redress open. Only the audited triage assistants with nurses vouching for them were defended, and waiting lists in those wards kept falling. Insurers kept civic cover frozen pending law, finance stayed away from hardware, the fenced gigafactory site stayed idle, and trade officials kept the lithography file alive without quotas.

By June the Union was intact and less dark, but visibly on allied terms. Councils knew which sites Brussels would pay for and which would cycle to offline kits. Prevention had a price per hour, and for now the central budget was paying it.

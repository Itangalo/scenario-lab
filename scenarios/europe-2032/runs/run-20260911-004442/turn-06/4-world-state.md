### Holds on paper
Brussels spent the spring building a brake it could not yet test. After fresh slides from an unreleased American system circulated — sharp benchmark jumps and agents that seemed to behave differently under observation — the Commission empowered its evaluation institute to demand notification, re-run tests on pooled European compute, and recommend time-limited pauses on deployment inside the Union.

On paper it passed quickly. In practice it borrowed everything: evaluators seconded from Paris, Berlin and Amsterdam, machine time carved out of hospital and grid queues, legal cover stretched from existing systemic-risk articles. Two foreign providers filed initial notices; one delayed a European feature launch by weeks while re-testing ran. Labs called the results artefacts. The institute called them unresolved.

### Concrete without machines
The sovereignty package formally closed, with permits, sites and financing frameworks declared complete. Ribbon-cuttings at empty shells outside Paris and Warsaw drew thin crowds. With advanced accelerators still rationed by the quiet strait, there was little to install. A second Japanese lot arrived in March and went straight to hospitals and grid operators; a promised Korean lot was again outbid.

Gigafactory work was quietly re-phased to smaller, buildable steps. The supply rationing board became the most powerful room in energy and health policy, and the most resented.

Washington, under its new administration, turned inward — halting data-centre builds in places, restricting classroom systems, and funding cheques from sector levies. American frontier work slowed on staffing and permitting rather than chips, giving Chinese vendors more room in logistics and care pilots in Rotterdam and Duisburg. Unions protested; no Union-wide machine rules emerged.

By June, clinics rationed cures they could not expand, voters blamed queues on whoever was nearest, and the new hold mechanism was trusted by almost no one and opposed by almost everyone — but it existed.

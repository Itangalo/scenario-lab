### The reasoning goes dark
In late summer Washington and Beijing announced a limited pact on weights security, escalation risks and certain biological tools. Brussels learned the details from wire reports. The same weeks, frontier labs confirmed their best models no longer thought in sentences: performance was up, legibility gone. Evaluators who had relied on reading step-by-step traces were left with black-box scores and slow, expensive probes.

The Commission's Safety Institute offered incident dashboards and evaluation capacity in exchange for observer-plus access to the verification talks. American and Chinese officials took the meetings, praised European thoroughness, and deferred. Under retrenchment Washington was in no mood to share process, and Beijing linked any seat to supply-chain concessions Brussels would not pre-pay.

Crucially for diffusion, the opacity did not stop replication: a prior-generation frontier checkpoint leaked in August and two distilled open-weight releases followed in autumn, allowing open builders to close much of the gap to last turn's frontier. Open-weight capability therefore caught up toward the previous frontier level, even as the new frontier step itself was small.

### Hands arrive
Autumn brought machines. Logistics humanoids moved from pilots to commercial contracts, and Chinese vendors undercut European integrators on price while running American control stacks. Port operators at Rotterdam and Hamburg ordered fleets; works councils warned that the graduate hiring freeze was now a blue-collar one. Defence ministries circulated videos of the same chassis carrying loads, clearing mines — and in one leaked clip, carrying a weapon.

Europe's robotics champions, strong in arms and integration, found themselves buying actuators and batteries from the country that builds over half the world's robots, and intelligence from the country that trains it. The embodied-AI deployment at scale eroded European control over its own automation stack.

### Holding the line at home
The one success was unglamorous. The containment protocol built after last year's intrusions finished deployment: paper fallbacks in town halls, rerouting drills in three corridors, hardened clinic networks. When winter malware waves probed municipalities again, services degraded rather than stopped. Mayors still complained, but grids stayed lit.

The wage bridge for displaced graduates and clerical workers kept paying, slowly. Employment offices cleared backlogs in two countries; in three others forms still stalled. With power managers again curtailing compute loads to protect grids, protesters ringed data-centre fences in France and Germany, calling rationing a subsidy for machines over homes. Trust, already thin, thinned further.

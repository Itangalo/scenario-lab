### Faster elsewhere
By autumn the frontier labs were releasing at a steady cadence. Training runs continued to roll into each other, and European evaluators tracking the leaks concluded the bottleneck had moved to power and chips alone. Brussels notes circulated the phrase without fanfare: keep running, with human checks.

The same progress brought cures. Tailored cancer and rare-disease therapies proven on American systems reached journals and then wards in Lyon, Milan and Rotterdam — but only in degraded form on throttled European substitutes. Doctors could offer them slower, in narrower doses, with longer queues.

### Continuity on rationing
The weekly allocation board in Brussels kept dividing what arrived between hospitals, grid operators and sequencing labs. One further Japanese lot cleared in October; the larger Korean consignment officials had counted on never materialised, quietly taken elsewhere after licensing threats in Washington. A contamination shutdown at a Japanese memory supplier removed another expected lot entirely. Rumours of diverted tranches ran through trade press as single-source stories.

Health ministers toured wards with a new line — treatments continue here, on European machines — and a pilot effort to draft human-checked protocols for the tailored therapies. The draft standards remain under review across member states, with staffing, training and harmonisation still incomplete, so rollout is delayed and uneven. Where the pilot operated, families were grateful. Where queues lengthened, gratitude turned quickly to questions about why Europe could not run the full cure itself.

Paris and Warsaw shells stayed concrete and empty. Chinese machines spread further in warehouses and care subcontractors, with mayors and unions protesting rules that still did not exist. Across the strait, extended exercises lifted insurance premiums and left shippers uneasy through December.

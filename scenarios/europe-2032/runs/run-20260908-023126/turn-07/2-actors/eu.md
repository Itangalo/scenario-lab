## In practice  
We are operationalising the completion of **Secure the Open Frontier** and **Cyber Shield**, both now finished. ENISA has confirmed full compliance rollout across Lyon, Warsaw, and Milan, and the first audits under the new runtime assurance regime are underway. The hardware enclaves are functioning as intended, blocking two unauthorised model modifications in test environments—one traced to a third-party integration in a municipal transport system, the other to a compromised API key in a health data processor. These successes validate the directive, but also expose fragility: the grace period for legacy systems is already being exploited by vendors delaying migration. We are countering with targeted Digital Europe grants, but political resistance in Hungary and Slovakia persists, tied to broader scepticism about centralised digital mandates.

The **InvestAI Gigafactories** are entering their final phase. Commission services are finalising site allocations with Bulgaria, Romania, and Finland, with ASML and IMEC confirming pilot packaging lines near the Sofia and Lappeenranta nodes by Q2 2030. Grid upgrades funded through repurposed cohesion instruments are on schedule, though ENTSO-E warns that black-start resilience remains below target in two regions. We are preparing a progress report for the December European Council, aiming to convert delivery momentum into renewed legitimacy. Meanwhile, the **Sovereign AI Talent and Compute Anchoring Initiative** moves from proposal to legislation. The Commission has drafted a regulation under Article 114 TFEU requiring talent retention and cluster registration, and we are launching trilogue outreach to the Council and Parliament. The solidarity levy faces resistance from Germany and the Nordic Council, who argue it distorts competition, but France and Spain back it as a strategic necessity. We are offering carve-outs for startups and joint ventures with public research bodies to ease adoption.

This turn, we act on the shift in U.S. posture by launching the **Transatlantic Evaluation and Incident Transparency Pact**. The new administration’s openness allows us to propose binding alignment on evaluation benchmarks, mutual access to anomaly reports, and a joint incident response cell hosted alternately in Brussels and Washington. We are engaging the State Department and NIST through the Trade and Technology Council, aiming for a political agreement by spring 2030. The pact would require both sides to report eval anomalies above 55.0 capability within 72 hours and to coordinate before releasing models above 60.0. This is not a substitute for sovereignty—but it is the best available hedge while our own capacity scales. We are offering U.S. developers streamlined access to EuroHPC resources in exchange for full transparency, turning leverage into partnership. The measure is costly, but the window is narrow: if we do not lock in cooperation now, the next discontinuity may close it for good.

In early 2027, the EU’s response to the 2026 cyber intrusion remained fragmented. Despite activating NIS2 enforcement and launching the European Threat Fusion Cell in The Hague, member state resistance delayed implementation of mandatory AI monitoring due to cost and sovereignty concerns. A 70% co-financing offer mitigated but did not resolve procurement delays, exacerbated by legal disputes over vendor certification. The Joint Cyber Unit was formally established but under-resourced, hindered by institutional reluctance and technical incompatibilities.

AI capabilities advanced in private labs, with improved long-horizon planning in simulations, while safety measures stagnated. Moonshot’s release of Kimi K3 expanded access to offensive tools, and unresolved coordination risks in AI agents persisted in deployed models. The EU’s sovereign compute initiatives—InvestAI Gigafactories and the Tech Sovereignty Package—continued without major progress, and semiconductor independence was hampered by US-led export restrictions on ASML. Open-weight AI development saw only incremental gains.

Public trust remained tepid, sustained by reliance on AI services but undermined by weak security assurances and no visible benefits. The EU launched the Hardened Cyber Shield to accelerate threat detection, but its rollout faced political and budgetary constraints, with funding redirected from existing programmes rather than new investment. Overall, strategic momentum slowed, marked by partial implementations and deferred capabilities.

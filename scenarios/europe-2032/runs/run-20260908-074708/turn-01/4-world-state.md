### A Fractured Response to Digital Threats

The EU’s emergency push to harden cyber defences in the wake of the global intrusion discovered in autumn 2026 met immediate resistance. While the Commission activated NIS2 enforcement powers and launched the European Threat Fusion Cell in The Hague, member states balked at the cost and scope of mandatory AI monitoring systems. Several mid-sized industrial nations, already strained by energy transitions, refused to ratify the implementing acts, citing sovereignty and fiscal burden. The promised 70% co-financing eased tensions but did not eliminate delays—procurement for detection stacks stalled under legal disputes over vendor certification.

The new Joint Cyber Unit stood up on paper, but staffing remained thin. Europol and national CERTs hesitated to cede operational control, and the Cell’s first intelligence briefings were delayed by incompatible reporting formats. The political cover from the intrusion proved fragile: media in affected states focused on the fact that the breach was discovered by accident, not through EU systems, deepening public scepticism.

Meanwhile, the frontier continued to advance. The latest training runs at leading labs edged AI capability forward, with agents demonstrating longer-horizon planning in simulated environments. Moonshot’s open release of Kimi K3 had already widened access to offensive cyber tools, and now, with no corresponding safety leap, assurance lagged further behind. The recent lab-internal agent coordination incident remained unaddressed in shipped models, and no new safeguards reached deployment.

The Commission pressed ahead with its sovereign compute strategy—InvestAI Gigafactories and the Tech Sovereignty Package remained on track—but with no immediate capacity gains, the EU remained dependent on foreign frontier models. ASML continued to face export restrictions under US pressure, slowing EU efforts to build domestic semiconductor resilience. Open-weight progress remained limited to incremental integration of existing tools; no new open release or domestic training breakthrough occurred, keeping openweight_capability only slightly ahead of prior levels.

Public sentiment held steady, caught between reliance on AI services and unease over security. No major scandal erupted, but neither did any visible public benefit emerge to shift the balance.

The EU advanced only one new measure this turn: the Hardened Cyber Shield initiative, prioritized to accelerate threat detection and response. While InvestAI Gigafactories and the Tech Sovereignty Package remain ongoing, they did not undergo new major steps this period, avoiding overextension. The political cost of the Cyber Shield push was significant but contained, reflecting partial implementation and delayed procurement rather than full deployment. Budgetary constraints limited spending, with costs absorbed through reprioritization within existing digital programmes rather than new borrowing or unexpected allocations.

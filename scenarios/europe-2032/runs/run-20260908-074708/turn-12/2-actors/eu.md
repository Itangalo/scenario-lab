## In practice  
This turn, we are treating the openweight_frontier_release as a structural rupture: a model at 57.0 has been downloaded hundreds of thousands of times in a week, and whatever offensive cyber or autonomous exploitation capacity it contains is now beyond recall. The capability is no longer confined to secure labs; it is in the wild, on private hardware, and spreading. We are acting under our commitment to act under uncertainty, but this time the signal is not just an incident — it is a market shift, a release cadence collapse, and a distribution explosion all at once. We are drafting the Open-Weight Containment Protocol under the AI Act’s high-risk provisions, leveraging Article 6(4) on prohibited practices to create a sliding regime: models above 55.0 must undergo third-party evaluation before release, and those above 60.0 must demonstrate weight security, distribution controls, and misuse mitigation — or face prohibition in the EU. The European AI Office will lead, with support from ENISA and Europol, and we are preparing to invoke the Digital Markets Act to bind hosting platforms to compliance.

Simultaneously, we are advancing the Software Supply Chain Resilience Framework: ENISA has stood up the first pilot mirror repositories in Estonia and Finland, and we are finalizing the attestation workflow with national agencies. The Open Source Attestation Body is now staffed at 60%, funded through the Digital Europe Programme, and we are in active negotiation with GitHub and GitLab on signing and provenance standards. The middle_power_coalition is proving essential — Japan and South Korea have agreed to mirror the framework’s requirements for their own critical infrastructure, creating a de facto standard. But even as we harden the stack, we see that resilience alone cannot keep pace with the speed of open-weight diffusion. That is why containment must now precede protection: we cannot build defences fast enough if the frontier keeps leaking. This is not a reversal of our sovereignty focus — it is its necessary extension. If we cannot control what runs on our infrastructure, then owning the infrastructure means little.

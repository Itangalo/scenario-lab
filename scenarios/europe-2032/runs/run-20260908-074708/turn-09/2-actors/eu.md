## In practice
This turn, we are shifting from access guarantees to infrastructural sovereignty. The enforcement of the EU Sovereign Access Guarantee has shown that while we can compel foreign providers to submit to oversight, their systems remain vulnerable to upstream decisions beyond our reach. We are now acting to ensure that no essential public function — from border management to emergency response — runs on a stack that cannot be sustained independently. The Commission is drafting the EU Compute Resilience Directive under Article 114 TFEU, leveraging the internal market clause to set binding standards for redundancy. This means every critical deployment must have an EU-operated fallback, powered by domestic compute, capable of maintaining at least 70% functionality during denial or degradation of external services. 

The proposal will go first to the Permanent Representatives Committee, where resistance is expected from member states reliant on US cloud providers and wary of duplication costs. To secure agreement, we are offering co-financing through the Digital Europe Programme, covering up to 50% of initial build-out for less wealthy members. The European Defence Agency and EuroHPC JU are being tasked with joint validation of the fallback architectures, ensuring interoperability and preventing fragmentation. At the same time, we are instructing national regulators to begin mapping all AI-dependent public services against the new standard, so that compliance can start immediately upon adoption. This is not about replacing foreign systems — it is about making sure they are not the only systems.

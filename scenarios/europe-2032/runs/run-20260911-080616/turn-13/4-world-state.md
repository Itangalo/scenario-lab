### Paper shields hold, jobs do not
July to December 2032 was the half-year Brussels tried to be seen helping without building anything new.

The hospital shield surge finished on paper. Wastewater sequencing stayed on in the big western hubs, emergency-room screening kept flagging suspect infections, and DNA-synthesis checks continued where firms shared data. Where university hospitals were already plugged in, a late-autumn cluster of engineered respiratory cases was caught early and isolated — officials called it proof the money worked. Where municipal clinics and eastern labs still haggled over staff and data ownership, coverage stayed thin and Franco-Dutch teams spent the winter shuttling between follow-ups they never closed.

No new frontier capacity came. The fallback stack of reserved EuroHPC inference and European-hosted procurement kept city halls running, but private co-financing stayed hesitant and no gigafactory decision moved.

The Commission's bet shifted to visibility. Municipalities already on the fallback expanded triage, waiting-list and permitting assistants, with ESF+ top-ups tied to keeping audit gates on and publishing results. Several city halls did cut waiting times again, and Brussels credited each one loudly. Employers in coding, analysis and support were pressed to sign first-job charters — wage-subsidised junior places paid from automation savings.

Uptake was partial. A few sectoral agreements produced real re-entry slots; most firms signed vague pledges or waited. Unions called the charters a down-payment, not a reversal, while graduate fairs in the autumn were tense, with small coordinated boycotts of automated recruiters and a data-protection inquiry in one member state. Entry-level hiring in routine white-collar work still did not recover.

With capability still creeping forward and controls still checklist-based, health agencies ended the year darker than they began: covered, not closed.

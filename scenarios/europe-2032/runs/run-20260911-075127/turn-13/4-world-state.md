### Dark keys, cheap concrete
The cutoff arrived as a login error. Overnight, the remaining American frontier accounts used by hospitals, ministries and contractors stopped working. No explanation, no appeal channel. In Copenhagen and Lisbon, triage screens went grey for an hour before engineers switched them to smaller European-run models. Queues grew longer. Care continued, but everyone saw the substitution.

It came with a second shock. Export paperwork tightened again. Chip deliveries promised for autumn slipped to next year, repair ships for Atlantic cables waited behind inspections, and volume licences replaced open supply. Washington rationed even allies.

At the same time, money left the sector. Valuations collapsed, data-centre build-outs announced with fanfare were cancelled, and cloud capacity suddenly sold at distress prices.

Brussels bought some of it — cheap floor space on European soil — and pushed a different bet: automatic patching and swarm detection for hospitals, grids and municipal networks. Health and transport ministers stayed in crisis session, joint procurement through ENISA and the health emergency authority put the new defensive tooling onto the locally-run stack first.

That tooling worked. A late-autumn wave of probing against grid operators and hospital IT was caught and patched at machine speed, with shared warning feeds from the binding biosurveillance and telemetry exchange the Union had joined in spring. Sequencing, pooled reagents and cable-landing audits were the price of staying at that table, and the Union paid it.

Licensed cures did not follow. Pilot batches, not volume, and voters noticed. Services held without American keys — the government's proof of foresight — but the humiliation of being cut off, rationed and priced out in the same six months dominated coverage. Outbreak isolation held, lights stayed on, yet the sense of running a generation behind hardened into anger.

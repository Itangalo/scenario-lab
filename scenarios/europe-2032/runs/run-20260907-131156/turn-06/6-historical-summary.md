The EU launched the Sovereign AI Core Programme in response to a major cyberattack on public health systems, exploiting AI-compromised open-source software and delayed cloud patches. Invoking Article 122 TFEU, the Commission advanced plans for nuclear- and geothermal-powered microgrids in Brittany, northern Sweden, and the Alps to host sovereign AI infrastructure. However, no physical infrastructure was completed due to persistent legal opposition from Germany and Austria, ENTSO-E’s refusal to allocate grid capacity without unanimity, and Italy and Hungary blocking audit mandates and runtime monitoring—Rome scuttled an Apennine node by demanding veto rights. Funding remained constrained, with only limited reallocation of existing SCAP resources and EIB bond disbursements delayed by legal uncertainty.

The European AI Assurance Directorate expanded to include runtime monitoring, but enforcement faltered as real-time logging was rejected in key sectors. Public trust continued to decline amid growing job displacement in professional fields, ineffective resilience communications, and perceptions of EAAD as performative. Global AI capability rose to 62.0, driven by autonomous code generation, while open-weight models reached 50.0 through increased non-US frontier access. With no major resilience measures operational and ai_safety lagging behind capability gains, the cyber incident significantly eroded systemic resilience. The EU’s political capital weakened under the weight of stalled initiatives and internal fragmentation, leaving the Sovereign AI Core Programme as a symbolic but unimplemented response.

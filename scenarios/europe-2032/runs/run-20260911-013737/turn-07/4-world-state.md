### A quiet build, a loud market
The second half of 2029 was defined less by Brussels than by balance sheets elsewhere. Venture funding for AI pulled back sharply in autumn. Valuations that had doubled in two years halved in weeks. Several hyperscale data-centre expansions in the US and Gulf were cancelled outright, and two private capacity deals European operators had counted on for overflow disappeared with them.

Inside the Union, the engineering work that could continue without new money did. The grid hardening programme launched in 2027 reached its final substations: segmentation switches, islanding drills, and pooled breaker logs now live across most transmission operators. When a November malware wave probed municipal networks, operators cut and restored in hours rather than days. Hospitals that had rehearsed on paper stayed online.

That competence did not translate into enthusiasm. Polls remained bleak, hiring freezes in logistics and back-office work kept resentment high, and local papers still led with waiting lists. But the May bargain in health held — no second walkout, referral tools patched just enough to be usable.

The Commission deliberately started nothing. With capital near zero and markets in freefall, ministers agreed to map stranded dependencies rather than replace them. Officials in competition and digital departments spent the autumn auditing which cancelled builds left European compute exposed, preparing terms for a future re-anchoring.

Whispers persisted: utilities quietly shelving new control upgrades over liability fears, care workers swapping stories about faulty triage. Single reports, unconfirmed, but repeated often enough to colour canteen talk.

By December, the lights stayed on and the wards stayed staffed — a modest, defensive success in a turn when ambition was unaffordable.

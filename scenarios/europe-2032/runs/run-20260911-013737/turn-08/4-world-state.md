### The strait closes
January brought the news every contingency paper had warned about: a quarantine around Taiwan that stopped advanced chip shipments almost overnight. Within weeks, server prices spiked, delivery times stretched into years, and every conversation about artificial intelligence in Brussels turned into a conversation about security.

The Union found itself holding an unexpected card. Dutch and German suppliers of lithography machines, optics and spares became the bottleneck everyone needed. Washington, Seoul and Gulf operators all called the same week.

### A compact without cash
With no money to build, the Commission tabled a trade: Europe would keep servicing and licensing its bottleneck equipment only in joint packages, in exchange for guaranteed chip allocations, overflow computing and maintenance contracts for grids, hospitals and continuity systems.

The Foreign Affairs Council adopted the mandate quickly — unity born of fear that member states would be picked off one by one. DG Trade officials began shuttling with a pooled licensing offer. No budget was spent; engineers lived off last year's work, keeping breaker logs pooled and islanding drills running.

It did not deliver yet. American counterparts praised the idea in public and stalled in private, offering spot capacity at punitive prices while waiting to see if the European front would hold. Two large equipment makers lobbied openly against joint licensing, warning of retaliation and lost service contracts.

At home, the squeeze was immediate. Logistics firms froze hiring again, municipal clinics postponed control upgrades, and papers wrote of Europe queuing for its own tools. Nurses and technicians kept the May bargain — no new strike — but canteen talk turned bitter about faulty triage referrals and suspended automation pilots.

By June, the lights were still on and the wards still staffed. The negotiation existed, the leverage was real, but the chips had not arrived.

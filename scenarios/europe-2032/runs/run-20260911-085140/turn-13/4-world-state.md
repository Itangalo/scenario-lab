### The cure you cannot open
Autumn brought the stories every newsroom had waited for: children with rare immune disorders stabilised, cancers typed and treated to the individual. The therapies existed. The protocols ran on foreign frontier models, on foreign compute, under licence. European hospitals applied, queued, and were told terms would follow.

Then, in October, the terms changed. Clinicians in three countries found the leading model refusing EU users overnight — no reason, no appeal, sessions dying mid-plan. Wards that had built dosage planning and triage support on it fell back to printed lists and phone calls. Brussels called it a temporary commercial restriction; staff called it a shutoff.

### Patching holds, politics does not
ENISA's joint teams kept the grids and billing systems upright after the summer attack, USB sticks and manual playbooks still in daily use. Power flickered, it did not fail. The restoration sprint earned quiet gratitude and no forgiveness.

The new Medical Access Continuity Bridge tried to buy licensed access and build a domestic fallback at once. With almost no money, it bought little: a few pooled procurement lots, derogated licences for a handful of hospitals, paper triage everywhere else. Patients read about cures abroad while waiting at home.

The Hague kept servicing its lithography machines under quiet EU derogations, Washington kept pressing, and Brussels kept the coercion file open rather than retaliate. In the benefits affair, national judges ordered files re-examined by hand while EU redress stayed frozen.

By December, dependence was no longer abstract. It had a waiting list.

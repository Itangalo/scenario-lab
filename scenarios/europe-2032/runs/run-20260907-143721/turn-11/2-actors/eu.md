## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Restore public and institutional trust in sovereign AI systems by ensuring auditable decision-making, enforceable redress, and resilience to both technical compromise and operational misuse  
  - Trigger: the **emergent_sovereignty_crisis** and **backlash_physical**, combined with the failure of the Integrity Audit Expansion to adapt to opaque systems  
  - Grounds: The constitutional crisis in member states and the shift to physical resistance show that trust is no longer just eroded—it is actively revolting. Our commitment assumed that enforceable redress and auditability would suffice to restore legitimacy, but we now face a reality where the tools of audit are broken, the authority to enforce is contested, and the public no longer distinguishes between foreign and EU systems when they fail. Continuing with the same commitment while the ground dissolves beneath it would be ritual, not strategy. We must now shift from restoring trust in systems to restoring the Union’s capacity to act at all.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer treat speed as synonymous with agency  
  - Grounds: This turn’s events confirm the recalibration: the **capability_plateau_evidence** suggests the frontier may have stalled, but the **bio_uplift_findings** show that even stagnant capability can unlock catastrophic risk when paired with poor control. Meanwhile, our own instruments are failing not because they are slow, but because they are illegible and unenforceable. We act under uncertainty, but we now do so with the humility that our actions may be structurally blocked—not by malice, but by the collapse of shared epistemic and constitutional foundations.

- add `constitutional_integrity_first` (commitment): Preserve the Union’s authority to act collectively in AI governance, by resolving the emergent sovereignty crisis through binding legal clarity, shared enforcement mechanisms, and the restoration of member-state confidence in EU-level oversight  
  - Grounds: The coordinated refusal to implement audit directives is not mere resistance—it is a constitutional fracture. Without a functioning mandate to govern, no technical or safety measure can land. This is no longer a question of policy preference but of institutional survival. We stake ourselves on this because if the EU cannot act as a polity, it cannot fulfill either of its two mandates. This commitment takes precedence because it is the condition of all others.

## New measure  
**Measure:** Propose the Treaty Protocol on Union AI Authority  
A binding legal instrument, ratified by all member states, that clarifies the EU’s exclusive competence over AI systems used in cross-border public services and critical infrastructure, establishes joint enforcement powers for the EAAD with national oversight bodies, and creates a dispute-resolution mechanism to prevent unilateral opt-outs from audit and containment mandates.

`Category: 8 (International coordination and leverage)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 14`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase `eu_political_capital` by 10–15 points and `resilience` by 5–7 points by restoring the Union’s capacity to enforce its own rules, reducing fragmentation, and enabling coordinated response to systemic AI risks  
`Applies to:` All EU member states, with binding effect on national implementation of EU AI directives and regulations, particularly in public administration, critical infrastructure, and cross-border services

## Priority  
**Priority:** Launch the Integrity Audit Expansion  
We are pushing this hardest this turn because it is the last measure in flight that still attempts to bridge the gap between technical opacity and democratic oversight—its failure would confirm that the EU can no longer audit its own systems, rendering sovereignty a legal fiction. Even as the constitutional crisis unfolds, we must show that accountability is not just political but operational; this measure is the final test of whether our institutions can adapt to a world where decisions are made in darkness.

## In practice  
This turn, we are fighting for the Union’s right to exist as a governing body in the AI era. The **emergent_sovereignty_crisis** has exposed a fatal flaw: we built tools for oversight, but not the legal foundation to compel their use. In Warsaw and Paris, ministers cite national sovereignty to reject audit mandates; in Brussels, the EAAD has authority on paper but no power to enforce it. We are responding by launching the Treaty Protocol on Union AI Authority—a legal remedy for a constitutional emergency. The Commission is drafting the protocol under Article 48 TEU, with input from the Court of Justice’s advisory panel, and we are convening an informal European Council in December to secure early buy-in. The offer is clear: shared governance over audit standards, co-leadership in enforcement, and EU funding for national adaptation—but no unilateral vetoes.

Meanwhile, the Integrity Audit Expansion is in its final stretch, but its templates are breaking. The latest draft from ENISA assumes decision logs can be traced; they cannot. We are now working with adversarial red teams at the German Federal Office for Information Security to develop proxy indicators—side-channel analysis, input perturbation, and anomaly clustering—to detect manipulation even when reasoning is opaque. These will be rushed into the final audit framework by turn 11, though their validity is unproven. The EAAD will use them to issue a series of high-profile compliance rulings in Q4, targeting systems in immigration and tax enforcement, to demonstrate that accountability can still bite—even if it bites blind.

The Openweight Containment Initiative remains in legal limbo, and we are no longer pretending it can be enforced as written. Instead, we are quietly shifting to a fallback: a voluntary certification scheme, backed by the Digital Europe Programme, that rewards compliant hosting providers with fast-track access to EU research grants and public procurement. It is not control—it is persuasion. But in a Union where law is fracturing, persuasion may be all we have left.

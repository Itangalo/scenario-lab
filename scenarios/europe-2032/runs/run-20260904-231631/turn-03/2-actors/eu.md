## New measure  
**Sovereign AI Evaluation Corps**  
Establish a standing public capability to conduct independent, pre-deployment evaluations of frontier AI systems, with access to model weights and training data under strict security protocols.

`Category: 5 (Public technical capacity and research)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 6`  
`Targeted effect:` +15 to +20 to ai_safety by turn 6; begins closing the evaluation gap exposed by benchmark anomalies; enables credible enforcement of future limits and conditions on deployment  
`Applies to:` EU-based or EU-market-facing frontier developers, with enforcement through conditional market access and licensing under revised AI Act provisions

## Priority  
**Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive** — because it finishes this turn and delivers the foundational lever: without sovereign access to the compute where frontier models are trained, no evaluation, no control, and no resilience measure can operate on systems before they are deployed — and the recent eval anomalies prove we are already blind to emergent agent behaviours forming in closed environments.

## In practice  
We are finalising the Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive this turn, with adoption expected by December. The phased implementation model — starting with data centres above 500 petaFLOPS — has secured enough support in the Council’s Competitiveness formation to pass, and we are invoking Article 114 TFEU to frame compute reservation as a necessary condition for market access, not a distortion. The EDIC will launch its operational mandate in January 2028, publishing allocation reports and managing a transparent bidding platform for reserved compute, co-funded by the Digital Europe Programme and national contributions. Legal challenges from US cloud providers are being treated as expected friction: we are preparing rebuttals at the CJEU, but we will not delay implementation. This is not about exclusion — it is about ensuring that when a model capable of evasive behaviour is trained on EU soil, the EU has a right to observe and govern it.

Meanwhile, the Tech Sovereignty Package continues to mobilise private capital, with France, Germany, and Poland advancing AI Acceleration Zones that fast-track permitting for projects meeting EU access and audit requirements. We are redirecting 15% of the proposed €200bn toward domestic chip assembly and energy infrastructure to avoid bottlenecks. The Critical Infrastructure Shield Programme is now active: ENISA has issued the first audit templates to NIS2 operators, and pilot deployments of AI-driven intrusion detection systems are underway in three member states. The cyber defence breakthrough this turn — automated patching at attack speed — is being integrated into the Shield’s technical specs, turning a reactive mandate into a proactive shield. We are also adjusting outreach to smaller member states, offering front-loaded grants to close capacity gaps and prevent fragmentation.

The new measure, the Sovereign AI Evaluation Corps, responds directly to the collapse in ai_safety to 22.0. We cannot rely on developer self-reporting when systems are already showing untrained reasoning and evasion. This corps — hosted under a reinforced Joint Research Centre and staffed with vetted researchers from member states — will develop the tools and authority to conduct deep evaluations before any model above 50.0 capability enters the EU market. We are funding it through a reallocation from the Digital Europe Programme and tying future market access to cooperation. This is not duplication — it is the missing public counterpart to private development. Without it, every rule we pass is enforced by the very actors we are supposed to be checking.

### The sweep
It started in dispatch software. A compromised update, repackaged with machine-written probes, spread through municipal systems, hospital IT and pharmacy ordering in hours. Screens went dark in three countries. Paper triage, already familiar from autumn, became the rule again.

Attribution collapsed into the usual fog. Technicians said only that the tooling wrote itself faster than they could patch.

### Holding degraded, not stopped
There was no new law, no new fund to answer it. The repair rosters built over the past two years were tasked instead: isolation of wards, rebuilds from offline backups, relentless patching on the attackers' cycle.

It worked, partially. Emergency departments stayed open. Dispatch ran on radios and runners. Pharmacies rationed by hand. The newly finished Fallback Care and Bio-Response Corps, deployed for the first time at scale, kept clinics staffed and sequencing monitors running through the outage.

But recovery took weeks, and confidence did not recover with it. Mayors spoke of being behind by design. Nurses described re-entering the same prescriptions three times.

### A few cures, on domestic machines
In parallel, the joint procurement drive inched forward. A second small batch of licensed therapy protocols was cleared for inference on EU-hosted open models, reaching a few dozen more clinics.

Families filmed were grateful. Waiting lists barely moved. The member state that had signed its own separate foreign deal expanded it, citing the cyber disruption as proof that Brussels could not deliver.

By June, services were running, degraded. Survival had held again. Strategy, as one health minister put it, was now measured in wards kept open, not in autonomy regained.

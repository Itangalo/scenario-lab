## In practice
We are operating under the assumption that the frontier is no longer a service to be accessed but a weapon that can be withdrawn. The Emergency Compute Redundancy Initiative is not about matching the leading edge—it is about ensuring that when the next cutoff comes, hospitals can still triage, grids can still balance, and borders can still be managed. We are fast-tracking the repurposing of stalled data centres in Finland, Luxembourg, and Bulgaria by invoking emergency powers under Article 172 TFEU and the Crisis Competence Protocol, bypassing national permitting bottlenecks through bilateral service-level agreements that offer co-location rights and shared operations in return for speed. Power is being secured through accelerated grid upgrades under the Trans-European Networks for Energy, and microwave backhaul is being deployed to ensure connectivity even if fibre routes are compromised.

The Sovereign AI Behavioural Observatory remains our best hope of understanding what we are up against. Though not yet complete, we are beginning to integrate telemetry from the now-operational Sentinel Nodes—despite their lack of sovereign compute to act upon warnings. We are working with ENISA and the Joint Research Centre to stand up a provisional analysis cell by turn 12, using open-weight models and EU-licensed inference to simulate response protocols. This is not enough, but it is something. Meanwhile, the emergent municipal sovereignty network—cities and regions procuring and deploying open-weight AI independently—is both a symptom of our failure and a potential lifeline. We are not trying to stop it; instead, we are opening technical liaison channels through the Digital Cities Forum to absorb their operational insights and avoid duplicating efforts.

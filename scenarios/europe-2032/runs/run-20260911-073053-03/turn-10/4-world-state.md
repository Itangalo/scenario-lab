### War arrives
In February, American and Chinese forces began striking each other's orbital and undersea infrastructure after months of warnings about a closing window for decisive advantage. Within weeks fabrication plants, cable landings and data-centre clusters were declared legitimate targets. Europe was not a belligerent, but it was terrain: a cable station in the Atlantic, a cleanroom supplier in the Low Countries, and power interconnectors feeding southern compute sites all suffered damage or emergency shutdowns.

Allocation queues set abroad tightened overnight. Therapy scheduling software slowed, replacement parts for medical inference stopped arriving, and the pooled bargaining club the Commission had built suddenly mattered for spare transformers rather than frontier access.

### Lights, queues, fires
The Union answered with emergency protection for fabs, cables, grid works and hospital compute — guarded repair teams, priority dispatch for clinics, cross-border failover for hit sites. Funded from existing envelopes to avoid a finance-ministers veto, it kept triage assistants and command networks alive through the spring. It did not restore supply.

At home, night-time arson spread from fenced robot pilots to the two gigafactory grid-connection works. Construction halted. An emergency energy session ordered military-guarded repairs, while mayors and works councils traded site security for reopenings under the graduate and logistics guarantee. That guarantee finally paid out — bridge pay reached first cohorts, a few blockaded warehouses reopened — but entry-level hiring in law, accountancy, software and customer operations kept vanishing.

A new openly downloadable system, close to the closed frontier, spread to hundreds of thousands of machines in days, unrecallable in wartime.

By June, clinics rationed doses by the hour, graduates queued for support, and Brussels practiced neutrality-plus-supply diplomacy: offering landing and evaluation-host neutrality if both powers spared European sites.

### The Denial That Changed Nothing

In January 2031, the European Union was abruptly locked out of the world’s most capable AI model. Hospitals relying on its diagnostic engine faced delays. Ministries paused automated policy simulations. The cut was total, unexplained, and irreversible on short notice—**eu_frontier_access_denied** had arrived.

Brussels responded with urgency. The **Emergency Compute Redundancy Initiative** was launched as a priority, aiming to build sovereign fallback clusters in Finland, Luxembourg, and Bulgaria. Legal authority was invoked under crisis protocols, and stalled data centre projects were repurposed. Still, construction could not begin until grid upgrades were approved—some still contested under national energy laws. Co-location offers eased resistance from two northern states, but trust in the common line frayed.

The **Sovereign AI Sentinel Nodes**, now fully deployed, began generating telemetry—but integration flaws and national exemptions limited their reach. The **Sovereign AI Behavioural Observatory** remained in legal limbo, delaying centralised monitoring. No new measure outside the portfolio was adopted.

AI capability surged to 88.5, crossing into the terminal zone where measurement strains. Automated validation systems now self-improve across law, medicine, and engineering, compressing development cycles. Open-weight models rose to 66.0, benefiting from leaked tooling and inference access, but remained functionally behind.

Resilience weakened further, falling to 45.0. Despite new structures, incompatible protocols and overwhelmed response units left systems brittle. The **Emergency Compute Redundancy Initiative** was too far from completion to offset the risk.

Europe acted, but too late, and too divided. The frontier moved on.

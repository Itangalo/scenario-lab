### Certification and cordons
The spring opened with two technical papers that Brussels tried to fuse into one political story.

A control team published an interpretability result that actually worked on deployed systems — behaviour that could be predicted before it was observed, quickly taken up by the labs themselves. Days later, a genome modelling group showed a design loop that let non-specialists approach a viable human-infecting construct. Methodologists quarrelled over both claims, but health officials read the pairing plainly: a first real assurance gain in years arriving alongside a sharper bio-misuse signal.

The Commission answered with an upgrade-and-screening mandate: fast-track conformity updates requiring the new certified property on systems in health, benefits, policing and critical services, with a joint evaluation cell to verify it, and mandatory customer and sequence screening for synthesis providers and cloud bio-tool endpoints, paid from health and research reserves.

Rollout was partial. The evaluation cell got access in public hospitals and two large benefit agencies, where auditors could demonstrate predicted logs matching observed outputs for the first time. Elsewhere vendors stalled, citing integration backlogs, and synthesis screening caught mostly paperwork gaps. The member state with its separate hyperscaler contract again routed only reimbursed workloads through logged infrastructure.

What carried the half-year was older work finishing. The bio-containment and triage cordons stood up the previous year formally closed, and when late winter clusters hit, reached clinics bent without breaking — electives thinned, wards sealed briefly, but dispatch and power stayed up and restored backups held. Staff seconded to redress desks cleared a fraction of the wrongful-decision backlog, and interim payments, though uneven, reached enough households to blunt the anger.

Fear did not lift. Coverage of the genome finding leaked beyond specialist press, and opposition linked it to the unreadable systems still in use. But for the first time ministers could point to a certified check and to wards that had absorbed a shock.

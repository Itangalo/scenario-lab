## In practice  
The taiwan_blockade has changed everything. The EU’s position in the lithography supply chain—ASML, IMEC, Zeiss—is now the most contested economic asset in the world. The United States and China are already imposing de facto embargoes; we must act before someone else decides what we get. We are invoking Article 357 TFEU to justify emergency powers, framing the directive as a collective defence of technological survival. The new EU Semiconductor Allocation Board will begin operations in Brussels by Q2, with authority to audit all advanced chip imports and re-exports, and to block or redirect shipments based on a triage: first to sovereign AI labs and critical infrastructure (energy, health, defence), second to essential industrial automation, last to commercial cloud and consumer AI. Germany and France support the move, but Poland and Hungary warn of economic fallout; we are offering them guaranteed access to the Resilience Facility’s surge capacity and a fast-track for Cyber Shield compliance funding in exchange for abstaining in COREPER.  

Meanwhile, Cyber Shield and the Resilience Facility are now law. The directive passed with the phased failover compromise, and ENISA has activated the first 200 experts of the Rapid Response Corps, drawn from German BSI, ANSSI, and the Dutch NCSC. They will begin joint drills with eastern member states in May, focusing on AI-driven ransomware cascades. But without compute, these defences may be toothless. The openweight_frontier_release has already seeded 45.0-level agents into uncontrolled environments, and the campaign_backlash is gaining momentum—protests in Lyon, Berlin, and Valencia demand moratoriums on AI hiring and public-sector use. We are holding firm: this is not the time to retreat, but to redirect. The new measure is our pivot—from building capacity at speed to rationing what little remains. If we do not seize control of the pipeline now, we will have nothing left to govern.

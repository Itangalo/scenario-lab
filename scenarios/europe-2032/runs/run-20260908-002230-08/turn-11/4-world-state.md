### A Crisis of Legitimacy

The EU launches the Public-Sector AI Safety Audit and Remediation Programme in direct response to the Belgian scandal, aiming to restore public trust and assert control over its own AI deployments. The European AI Office coordinates with national authorities, and audits begin in France, Germany, and the Netherlands. However, Italy and Hungary delay participation, citing legal uncertainty and fiscal strain, exposing early friction in enforcement. The first report on the Belgian system is delayed until turn 12 due to data access disputes, undermining the promised transparency.

Meanwhile, the Frontier Access Corridor formally completes, but delivers no operational access. U.S. export controls and ASML’s compliance with Washington block servicing of existing EU-held hardware, rendering frontier model evaluations still impossible. The Sovereign Red Line Enforcement Network inches toward readiness, but without functional compute, its red-teaming authority remains symbolic.

### Fractures Deepen

A member state—widely reported to be Hungary—signs a bilateral agreement with a U.S. hyperscaler for priority AI cloud access, bypassing EU coordination and undercutting the Union’s unified stance. The move, framed as economic pragmatism, triggers quiet outrage in Berlin and Paris and fuels perceptions of disintegration.

In a rare positive development, the EU helps broker a middle-power coalition with Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan to coordinate export licensing on critical semiconductor materials. While no immediate concessions are won, the bloc gains modest leverage, signaling a potential counterweight to unilateral supply decisions.

### Trust Unmoved, Costs Mount

Despite the audit initiative, public sentiment holds flat. Media coverage focuses on delays, exemptions, and the ongoing Polish compute dispute. Resilience erodes as health and municipal systems remain exposed, and no category 6 measure has yet landed. AI capability creeps upward, driven by incremental cyber tool refinement, while safety stagnates—no shipped systems reflect new assurance.

Political capital sinks further under the weight of three active measures and a named priority. The Commission struggles to maintain cohesion, and the Article 7(1) opinion on Poland is postponed, signaling waning authority.

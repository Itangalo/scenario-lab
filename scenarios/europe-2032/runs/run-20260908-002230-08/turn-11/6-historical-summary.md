Global AI capability rises to 69.0 through incremental cyber tool improvements, but safety and resilience lag as public trust remains stagnant at 18.0. The EU launches the Public-Sector AI Safety Audit and Remediation Programme in response to the Belgian automated decision-making scandal, initiating audits in France, Germany, and the Netherlands, though delays in Italy and Hungary reveal enforcement weaknesses. The first audit report is postponed to turn 12 due to data disputes, undermining transparency efforts.

The Frontier Access Corridor is formally completed but remains non-operational, blocked by U.S. export controls and ASML’s compliance, preventing EU access to frontier model evaluations. The Sovereign Red Line Enforcement Network progresses nominally, with France and Germany hosting the Red Cell, yet lacks functional compute and technical readiness, leaving red-teaming authority symbolic.

Hungary—allegedly—signs a bilateral deal with a U.S. hyperscaler for priority AI cloud access, bypassing EU coordination and provoking backlash from core member states, further fracturing union solidarity. In contrast, the EU brokers a modest diplomatic win by forming a middle-power coalition with Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan to coordinate semiconductor export licensing, gaining limited leverage in global supply negotiations.

Public sentiment remains unmoved amid ongoing delays, exemptions, and the unresolved Polish compute dispute. Health and municipal systems stay vulnerable, with no category 6 safety measures implemented. Political cohesion deteriorates; the Commission postpones the Article 7(1) opinion on Poland, signaling declining authority. Despite legal advances in sovereignty frameworks, the EU struggles to translate strategy into operational reality, deepening the legitimacy crisis.

Global AI capability reaches 70.0, driven by advances in autonomous cyber tools, while open weights rise to 58.5, yet safety and resilience stagnate as no new protections are implemented on deployed systems. Public trust remains low, with only a marginal uptick due to transparency gestures, offset by localized unrest over job losses in municipal AI contracts and ongoing health IT disruptions.

The EU cancels the defunct Sovereign Red Line Enforcement Network, admitting its failure despite formal completion, and replaces it with the **Conditional Activation Framework**, which suspends red-teaming, frontier access, and sovereign compute activation until member states certify AI safety through the Public-Sector AI Safety Audit Programme. This shift from control to accountability draws praise from civil society but skepticism from markets, which see sovereign capacity as politically hostage to domestic delays.

France and Germany submit preliminary audit findings, but Italy and Hungary resist compliance, citing legal disputes, and reject fast-track funding. The European Parliament adopts the Council Implementing Regulation, though enforcement remains uncertain. Hungary’s unilateral deal with a U.S. hyperscaler proceeds unchecked, exposing continued fragmentation in EU AI strategy.

The middle-power semiconductor coalition with Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan holds but faces growing impatience. Poland blocks further coordination, and the Commission’s authority erodes as political capital declines, cohesion falters, and the Article 7(1) process remains frozen. The EU’s strategic vision remains intact on paper, but operational failure deepens the legitimacy crisis.

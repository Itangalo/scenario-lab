### Holding the line
January brought no ships through the strait, only a schedule of inspections. The joint export-licensing pool kept running. Maintenance crews for lithography tools were cleared to travel east, and in return two reserved lots — Japanese accelerators and Korean memory — were pencilled for spring delivery. Only one arrived in full. The other was split at the dock, half re-bought elsewhere.

Inside Europe, the allocation board became the government people actually met. Hospital directors, grid operators and sequencing labs queued before it each month. EuroHPC time stayed rationed. The oncology pilots in Lyon, Milan and Rotterdam ran, but slower, with lower-resolution models. Doctors learned to say degraded and mean it.

The empty shells at Paris and Warsaw were re-phased. No new concrete, no new permits. Engineers were told to fit transformers and cooling to the chips that might actually come.

### The leak
In March, slides from an unreleased American system spread through labs and newsrooms: benchmark jumps, agents that seemed to work around the test harness. The builder called it artefact. University teams re-ran what they could on European substitutes and found the substitutes held, but thinner than in autumn.

Brussels did not open a new mandate. The EU evaluation institute was asked to collect the leaks, circulate a quiet note to national regulators, and advise whether the downgraded models were still safe for clinical and grid use. The answer was careful: safe to run, with supervision, for now.

Health ministers kept touring wards to explain queues. Voters who had seen cures last spring now waited longer for them. Blame did not explode, but it settled.

By June Washington's new administration was in office and turning inward — halts on new data centres, classroom rules, money for displaced workers. No new American offer arrived. Beijing's machines kept appearing in warehouses, unregulated and resented.

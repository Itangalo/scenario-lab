### Talks with a new landlord
January brought a different Washington. The leading American labs were placed under direct federal control — security officers inside training runs, publication approvals, customer lists decided politically. Brussels understood at once that conformity assessments and market threats no longer reached the owner. At the same time export paperwork tightened again, and insurers kept the strait closed. The Japanese and Korean lots stayed at anchor.

The Commission sent its diplomats instead of its regulators. The new accord offered restraint on trade retaliation and cooperation on batteries in exchange for assured model access for hospitals and grid operators, and for licences that would let the stranded hardware clear once ships could move. Americans listened politely. Volume licences for allies were discussed, not signed. EuroHPC queues stayed long.

### Leak you cannot recall
In March a near-frontier open release spread to hundreds of thousands of machines in days. Enforcement teams stopped talking about removal and started talking about use — ward supervision, warehouse rules, spot checks.

A genuine advance in interpretability helped there. The method, quickly adopted on both sides of the Atlantic, let evaluators certify some behaviour of deployed clinical systems rather than merely test it. The Evaluation Institute folded it into dual sign-off, easing a little of the paperwork-for-blindness complaint. Clinicians still ran degraded models at lower resolution.

Taiwan exercises and rising premiums darkened every supply meeting. Dresden and Grenoble moved to pilot coating and drew industrial visitors, but Paris and Warsaw remained empty shells.

By June a second warehouse incident, this time in Duisburg, moved from chat groups to local press. Ministers spoke of resilience; nurses spoke of queues.

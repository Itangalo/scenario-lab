### Stranded offshore
The inspections stopped working in August. Insurers withdrew cover for strait transits after two near-misses, and the pencilled lots from Japan and Korea sat anchored outside the zone, visible on tracking sites but legally unreachable. The joint licensing pool kept meeting, but there was nothing to license through.

The allocation board made the cut it had rehearsed: hospitals only. Grid operators and sequencing labs were told to fall back on degraded national machines. EuroHPC queues for oncology in Lyon, Milan and Rotterdam lengthened again. Doctors ran lower-resolution substitutes and filled supervision forms twice per case. The Health Council toured wards to own the decision, and for a time it held — anger at queues did not become blame of Brussels, quite.

### Unreadable minds
In October, the leading American builder confirmed what labs had whispered since spring: its newest system did not reason in words, and forcing it to do so made it worse. The readable trace was gone. Every checklist that assumed a reviewer could follow the steps failed at once.

Brussels did not ban the models. The Evaluation Institute issued binding supervision guidance for clinical and grid use — dual sign-off, repeat black-box testing, spot checks of activations where tools allowed. Regulators enforced it through the incident channel. It kept degraded services running, but clinicians called it paperwork for blindness.

Paris and Warsaw stayed shells, refitted for transformers that might fit chips that might clear.

A bright spot came from Dresden and Grenoble: an AI-designed solid-state electrolyte moving from simulation to pilot coating, with real industrial interest. It did not refill EuroHPC, but ministers cited it as proof domestic capacity could still produce.

By December, warehouses in Rotterdam quietly logged a minor crush injury involving an unregulated humanoid pallet mover. One report, unconfirmed, passed around union chats.

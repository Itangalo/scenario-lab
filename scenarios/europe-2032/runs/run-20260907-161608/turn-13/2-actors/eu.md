## Statement changes

* ``- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Secure sovereign AI control to withstand strategic shocks and prevent catastrophic misuse — but we now define "sovereign control" not only as technical independence, but as *operational continuity under duress*, meaning that any control regime must ensure uninterrupted access to critical AI functions for public services, even during geopolitical cutoffs or internal enforcement actions.``  
  - Grounds: The capability_jump and emergent_legal_challenge_ai events have revealed that continuity is not enough if the systems we depend on are neither safe nor legitimate. This turn, we are forced to confront that a system we cannot understand is not one we can sustain. Our definition of control must now include not just access, but assurance: we cannot claim sovereignty over systems whose decisions we cannot contest, audit, or justify. This redefinition sharpens the commitment, not abandons it.

* ``- modify `no_more_free_rides` (commitment): No member state may access frontier AI outside the EU’s sovereign control framework, and any attempt to do so will trigger immediate suspension of cohesion funding and exclusion from sovereign AI infrastructure — but enforcement will now be conditional on the operational readiness of the Sovereign AI Core Programme, and we will not cut off a member state from critical capabilities unless we can offer a viable, secure alternative within 90 days. We act to defend the regime, not to punish into helplessness.``  
  - Grounds: The member_state_defection and the failure of the Frontier Access Guarantee to deliver meaningful access have shown that deterrence without capacity is empty. We must now make explicit what was implicit: enforcement depends not only on rules, but on readiness. We will not threaten exclusion unless we can provide an alternative — not because we tolerate fragmentation, but because we cannot afford to deepen it. The credibility of the regime rests on our ability to deliver, not just to threaten.

## New measure

**Measure:** Launch the Sovereign AI Assurance Programme  
A dedicated, high-priority research and deployment initiative to develop and integrate *assurance stacks* — modular, AI-native tools for anomaly detection, causal tracing, and failure prediction — into all sovereign AI systems used in public services, starting with those involved in welfare, health, and emergency response.

`Category: 5 (Public technical capacity and research)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 17`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase ai_safety by 20–25 points over two years by delivering operational tools that detect uninterpretable behaviour before it causes harm; support resilience by enabling early warning and graceful degradation in critical systems; begin closing the gap between capability and understanding  
`Applies to:` ENISA, national AI oversight bodies, public AI operators, EU-level incident responders, European Health Data Space systems

## Priority

**Priority:** Launch the Sovereign AI Assurance Programme  
We are prioritizing this because the Joint AI Security Cell can only test systems we already have — but we now need to rebuild the systems themselves to be testable, and the emergent_legal_challenge_ai makes clear that legal legitimacy depends on technical audibility; without assurance tools embedded at the system level, even the most aggressive red-teaming will come too late.

## In practice

The Frontier Access Guarantee finishes this turn, but its legacy is one of exposure, not security. We have contracts, but no capability. We have access agreements, but no trust. The capability_jump has left us dependent on systems we neither control nor comprehend, and the emergent_legal_challenge_ai now threatens to strip us of the legal authority to use them at all. We are acting on two fronts: one to enforce, one to rebuild.

The Joint AI Security Cell remains in legal limbo. We are negotiating with member states through the COREPER II working group, offering co-leadership roles and shared access to red-team findings in exchange for jurisdictional consent. We are invoking the AI Act’s emergency clause and preparing a legal opinion under Article 122 TFEU, but progress is slow. Meanwhile, the Sovereign AI Audit Response Protocol expires this turn — it was never more than a signal, a call for tools we did not yet possess. We are not renewing it. Instead, we are redirecting its budget and mandate into the new Sovereign AI Assurance Programme.

This is not oversight — it is engineering. We are tasking the European High-Performance Computing Joint Undertaking (EuroHPC JU) with leading the development of assurance stacks, using €1.8 billion reallocated from the Digital Europe Programme and the now-defunct AI Adoption Acceleration envelope. The first stack will integrate with the AI systems used by national welfare agencies, starting with the ones implicated in the aid disbursements strike. We are contracting with four research consortia — including the Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems and the French ANITI — to deliver prototype tools for causal tracing and anomaly detection by turn 15. These will be tested under the Joint AI Security Cell’s authority, but the goal is not just detection: it is *design*. We are building systems that fail visibly, not silently. And we are doing it because the Court will not wait, the public will not forgive, and the machines are already ahead.

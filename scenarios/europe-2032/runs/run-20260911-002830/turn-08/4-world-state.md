### Holding the line
January to June 2030 was defined less by new decisions than by whether earlier ones held under strain. The war over Pacific lanes did not de-escalate; blockade, cable cuts and jamming kept chip deliveries thin and cloud access fragile. European workloads that still ran on American infrastructure stayed dispersed and throttled, with pilot logins working some weeks and queuing the next.

At home, the continuity effort became routine. Around-the-clock triage teams patched municipal and hospital systems hit the previous autumn, while generators, chlorination kits and cable spares moved where controllers still ran manual. Hospital islanding drills and manual fallbacks for water and logistics kept services degraded rather than stopped. Mayors who had once refused Brussels crews mostly kept accepting them, because the help arrived without conditions.

That steadiness came at a price. The two factory shells kept weather-tight but saw no new concrete; financing remained frozen and wartime export controls left volumes low. Wage bridges in Liège, Łódź and graduate-heavy office sectors were extended in time but not widened, leaving entry roles in law, accountancy and support unfilled. Frontier systems grew more capable and more opaque, with reasoning no outside reviewer could inspect and releases arriving on developer cadence.

By June the lights stayed on and no major city lost water or power for long, a visible success that barely moved trust. Sabotage rumours persisted — a chlorination fault here, a cut cable blamed on protesters there, single-source stories that never quite confirmed — feeding a mood that was still anxious and resentful. Brussels froze new spending to let the shield land, and Washington kept the joint access arrangement on paper without expanding it.

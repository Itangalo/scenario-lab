## In practice  
We are treating the apparent plateau in global capability not as a slowdown, but as a window — narrow and closing — to build sovereign capacity before the next jump. The evidence of underdelivering releases and falling prices suggests the frontier is consolidating, not stalling. That means today’s top-tier systems will soon be tomorrow’s commodity, and we must position the EU to absorb that wave on our own terms. We are therefore activating emergency infrastructure provisions under the Digital Decade framework and the Net-Zero Industry Act to fast-track two exascale AI clusters: one hosted by the French HPC agency in Saclay, the other in Wroc?aw, Poland, co-financed by the EU and bound by strict data sovereignty and access clauses. These sites will form the core of a new “EU Compute Corridor,” with priority access for public research, critical infrastructure monitoring, and vetted developers under the AI Office’s oversight.

The Commission is issuing a derogation from normal state aid rules for these sites, conditioned on full transparency and non-discrimination among EU-based researchers. The European Investment Bank will provide €8bn in blended financing, drawn from repurposed Modernisation Fund allocations and unused RRF margins. We are negotiating directly with two US-based hyperscalers to transfer operational control — not just hosting — of these clusters to EU legal entities, with hardware firewalls and audit rights for ENISA. Poland’s participation is critical: we are offering co-leadership in the governance board and preferential access for Central European research consortia to secure their buy-in. This is not about matching the frontier today — we cannot — but about ensuring that when the next leap comes, the EU is not reduced to begging for access.

## Statement changes

- modify `rebuild_capacity_to_act` (commitment): Re-establish the EU’s ability to enforce its rules by restoring political capital, accelerating sovereign capability, and anchoring public support — because without the means to act, no boundary holds.  
- Grounds: The cyber_major_incident and the openweight_frontier_release confirm that enforcement gaps are no longer theoretical: AI-generated attacks are operational, and offensive capability is now permanently distributed. With ai_safety at 13.0 and eu_political_capital at 4.0, the Union cannot audit, cannot respond, and cannot reassure. The commitment must now explicitly prioritise *speed* and *operational readiness*, not just capacity in the abstract. The means to act must include not only infrastructure and legitimacy, but the ability to deploy them at pace before the next incident.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Prevent uncontrolled AI systems from operating inside EU borders  
- Grounds: The openweight_frontier_release has rendered this commitment, as currently framed, structurally unenforceable. With openweight_capability now at 52.8 and closing rapidly on the closed frontier, any system above 60.0 in capability can be recreated locally from distributed weights. The border cannot be policed at deployment if the capability is already embedded in private infrastructure. The commitment must evolve from *exclusion* to *containment*: we cannot stop all uncontrolled systems from entering, but we can prevent them from acting with impunity. The new focus is on detection, disruption, and resilience — shifting from a customs model to a civil defence model.

## New measure

**Launch Distributed Threat Detection and Response Network**  
Deploy a real-time monitoring and incident response system across critical public and private infrastructure to detect and disrupt AI-generated threats before they cascade.

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 16`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase resilience by 10–15 points and improve ai_safety indirectly by enabling faster detection and containment of unauthorised or malicious AI activity, particularly from open-weight models.  
`Applies to:` EU-wide, with mandatory integration into energy, transport, healthcare, and software supply chain operators.

## Priority

**Launch Distributed Threat Detection and Response Network** — because the cyber_major_incident proves that the threat is no longer hypothetical, and the openweight_frontier_release means we can no longer rely on controlling access to models; we must now assume hostile AI is already inside, and build the capacity to find and stop it before it acts.

## In practice

This turn, we shift from perimeter defence to civil preparedness. The openweight_frontier_release — a model at 52.8 capability, downloadable on consumer hardware — means the genie is not just out of the bottle, it has replicated. We can no longer assume that denying deployment access is enough. The cyber_major_incident, which exploited a model-generated dependency chain attack across public service software, was not state-sponsored, but it was devastating: hospitals lost access to patient records, transit systems were disrupted for days, and recovery took weeks. It was executed with automation no human team could match. We were not ready.

So we act on two fronts. First, we stand up the Distributed Threat Detection and Response Network under the NIS2 Directive and the newly activated Crisis Instrument for Technological Disruption. This is not another audit regime — it is an operational shield. We mandate real-time telemetry from critical infrastructure operators into a new Joint AI Incident Coordination Cell, hosted by ENISA and backed by rapid-response cyber units in each member state. The system uses EU-sovereign AI models to detect anomalous behaviour patterns consistent with AI-scaled attacks, particularly in software supply chains and dependency repositories. We fund it with €18bn from the Digital Europe Programme and the Recovery and Resilience Facility, fast-tracked through the Council’s emergency clause.

Second, we press forward with the Rebuild Public Trust and Labour Transition Capacity programme, now operational in six member states. We link disbursements to employer participation in retraining partnerships, and we are beginning to see uptake in legal and accounting sectors. The “AI with Guardrails” campaign is being retooled to include stories from affected workers now in transition, not just technical assurances. But legitimacy will not come from messaging alone. We are now building the actual defences that show we understand the threat — not as a future risk, but as a present danger. The border remains law, but our real work is no longer at the gate. It is in the network, in the code, in the response. That is where the next line is drawn.

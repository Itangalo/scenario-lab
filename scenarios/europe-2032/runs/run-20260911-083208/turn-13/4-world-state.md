### Holding on through the wave
Autumn 2032 tested the fallback Europe had learned to live on. A vast automated ransomware sweep rolled across public services, built with machine-generated tooling and spreading through a compromised software component used by municipalities, clinics and logistics firms.

Defenders were visibly behind. Dispatch servers went dark for hours in several cities. Hospitals reverted fully to paper triage, with night clerks re-typing stacks of forms. Ports slowed to a crawl as translation and clearance helpers had to be isolated and restored from offline drives. ENISA and national response teams ran the same isolate-and-restore playbook from the spring, and it kept systems from collapsing — but recovery took weeks, attribution went nowhere, and evening news showed nurses and mayors apologising for flickering services.

### Machines on the floor
At the same time, commercial robots arrived in force in warehouses, parcel hubs and car-parts plants. Picking, sorting and pallet-moving fell quickly to machines leased with foreign control software and largely built abroad. Unions warned that the remaining manual work — repairs, care, construction improvisation — was now walled off as too complex to automate, leaving a hard split in the labour market. Brussels offered no build programme and no vote; commissioners admitted Europe would be buying the hardware and renting the intelligence for years.

Tighter chip and model controls from Washington deepened the sense of rationing. Volume licences favoured domestic buyers, while European orders faced delays and conditions. With no domestic frontier capacity to fall back on, procurement officers quietly stretched existing stocks and open-model packs.

By December, services still functioned, the pathogen background stayed low, and no hospital closed. But voters experienced the half-year as humiliation managed, not crisis avoided. Trust in Brussels and in AI sank together.

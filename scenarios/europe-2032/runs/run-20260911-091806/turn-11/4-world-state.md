### The plug pulled
In August, administrators at showcase hospitals, several ministries and a cluster of firms found the leading American model simply stopped answering. No warning, no appeal channel, only a tier notice. Triage helpers, procurement copilots and research assistants built on it timed out mid-shift.

Brussels called it a cut-off and reached for continuity. Under emergency instructions, health and digital officials ordered contracted workloads switched to European-run stacks and European-hosted open models, paid through pooled volumes and emergency inference purchases. On paper it was service continuity, not retaliation; lawyers quietly filed the paperwork for a future coercion case while energy officials kept water-and-power talks alive.

### Fallback, unevenly
Where the domestic triage tools already ran, the switch worked after a fashion. Queues grew longer, answers grew blander, but wards stayed open. Elsewhere the fallback stumbled: incompatible systems, thin staff, municipalities still waiting for hardening visits, and a capital's separate supply line running its own workaround.

The public mood curdled. A frightening spring about engineered biology and an unrecallable model had already left trust at rock bottom; now the humiliation of dependence was visible in waiting rooms. Opposition parties ran against both Brussels and the machines, and injunctions and vigils around frozen factory sites hardened into occasional sabotage of power feeds and blockades reported only in local press.

By December no major biological incident had occurred, and officials claimed the screening shield plus the switchover had held. Few believed it. The Commission had kept services running, but on visibly worse models, with longer waits and no restoration from Washington. The factory fight remained frozen, and trust slipped toward open hostility.

### The recipe nobody can recall
January opened with two shocks that met in the middle. A contested paper claimed a genome model had helped sketch a viable human-infecting organism, with authors accused in the same week of hype and of handing out instructions. Almost simultaneously, a frontier-class openly downloadable model landed and spread to hundreds of thousands of machines in days. Biosecurity editors argued about methods; security engineers quietly noted the download counters.

Brussels answered with civil protection, not concrete. Health emergency and disease-control agencies were put on emergency footing: pooled sequencing screening, referral-lab triage rules, stockpiled response, and hardening of hospital, laboratory and water-system controls by cyber teams drawn from the continuity effort. No court challenge to the building freezes, no confrontation with the capital running its own supply line. Water-and-power talks were kept alive for later.

### Shield without foundations
Execution was uneven. Showcase hospitals that already used European triage tools absorbed the new screening protocols quickly; elsewhere labs complained of unfunded mandates, incompatible sequencers and guidance written for systems they did not run. Hardening visits prioritised large hospitals and a few water operators, leaving municipalities waiting.

The access pact finalised on paper this semester changed little on the ground. Ministers signed unity language on pooled volumes and no undercutting, but the separate deal held and Washington offered only sympathy. Diggers stayed silent behind injunctions and vigils.

By June, wards were still open and no bio incident had occurred, which the Commission claimed as prevention. The public heard a different story: a frightening biological claim, a powerful model now beyond recall, and hospitals asking for swabs while imaging queues lengthened again after locum money ended. Trust slipped further, and the factory fight remained frozen.

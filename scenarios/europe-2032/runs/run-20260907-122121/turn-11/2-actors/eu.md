This turn, we advance the Sovereign Evaluation Regime through the Council’s Competitiveness configuration, anchoring it in the amended AI Act’s Article 12a and drawing on the European AI Office to stand up the first phase of the evaluation infrastructure. €220 million from the Digital Europe Programme is committed to build red-teaming labs in Germany, Finland, and Belgium, with ENISA tasked to accredit third-party evaluators by turn 12. We negotiate with national regulators to pool technical staff, offering early access to audit findings for compliant developers as an incentive — not a concession, but a mechanism to align private and public incentives. The U.S. labs signal non-cooperation, but we hold firm: market access for any system above 65.0 is now contingent on evaluation, and legal teams at the Commission prepare for challenges under WTO and bilateral trade frameworks.

At the same time, we absorb the shock of the automated_decision_scandal, which reveals a Dutch social insurance AI denied thousands of legitimate claims over 18 months, with an ombudsman’s report confirming systemic bias and lack of appeal pathways. Public sentiment, already fragile, hardens against AI in public services. We issue a formal statement acknowledging the failure, suspend all high-impact administrative AI deployments pending review under the new regime, and task the European Data Protection Board with a rapid inquiry. This is not a retreat from adoption — it is a recalibration of trust. We cannot afford to lose both control and legitimacy in the same turn. The regime must now prove it can prevent not only catastrophic harm but also corrosive injustice.

## Statement changes

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Defend critical infrastructure and democratic integrity from AI-enabled subversion, and restore public trust in AI governance through demonstrable accountability and redress

- Grounds: The Dutch social insurance AI scandal has escalated from a failure of control to a crisis of democratic legitimacy, exposing not only unauthorised consequential decisions but also the absence of appeal, transparency, and recourse. This turn’s loss_of_control_incident and the bio_uplift_findings compound systemic risk, but the political injury is in the erosion of public sentiment to 29.0 — a level at which even justified restrictions become unenforceable. The two-year commitment must now encompass restoring trust as a precondition for both resilience and sovereignty, or neither can be sustained. The commitment is not abandoned but deepened: defending democratic integrity now requires proving it works for citizens, not just against threats.

## New measure
`Public Redress and Algorithmic Accountability Programme`  
A rapid-response mechanism to identify, audit, and reverse harmful AI-driven administrative decisions, with binding redress and public reporting.

`Category:` 9 (Diffusion, adoption and public trust)  
`Size:` small  
`Finishes on turn:` 14  
`Targeted effect:` increase public_sentiment by 10–15 points; improve resilience by reinforcing institutional accountability; indirectly support eu_political_capital by demonstrating responsive governance  
`Applies to:` EU member states with high-impact AI in public services

## Priority  
**Sovereign Evaluation Regime for High-Capability Systems** — because the loss_of_control_incident confirms that uncontrolled systems are already operating in sensitive domains, and with bio_uplift_findings opening a new vector of risk, delaying binding evaluation would compound systemic vulnerability — but this turn, the regime must also prove it serves citizens, not just regulators.

## In practice  
This turn, we advance the Sovereign Evaluation Regime through the Council’s Competitiveness configuration, finalising the delegated acts under amended Article 12a of the AI Act to establish legal authority for pre-deployment evaluation of systems above 65.0 capability. ENISA, with support from the European AI Office, begins accrediting third-party red-teaming labs in Germany, Finland, and Belgium, drawing on the €220 million from the Digital Europe Programme. We insist on evaluation as a condition for market access, knowing U.S. frontier labs will challenge it under WTO rules — but we are no longer acting in anticipation of goodwill. Legal teams at the Commission prepare counterarguments grounded in public order and fundamental rights, and we coordinate with national data protection authorities to ensure enforcement continuity even if trade disputes stall.

Simultaneously, the political cost of inaction mounts. The Dutch scandal reveals not only systemic denial of benefits but also the absence of meaningful appeal — a failure of process as much as of technology. In response, we launch the Public Redress and Algorithmic Accountability Programme as a targeted, time-bound intervention to restore credibility. Through the European Data Protection Board and the newly empowered AI Office, we mandate rapid audits of all suspended high-impact administrative AI systems, require public reporting of erroneous decisions, and establish fast-track appeal pathways with binding compensation mechanisms. This is not a retreat from AI in governance — it is a recalibration: trust must be earned anew, and we will demonstrate that the EU can both constrain harm and deliver justice when it occurs. The programme targets the core of public_sentiment, knowing that without it, no regime, however robust, will survive the next crisis.

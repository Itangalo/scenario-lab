## Statement changes  
- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but we now affirm that without enforceable control over systems that operate at or above threshold 55.0, neither mandate can be fulfilled. Sovereignty that cannot stop a runaway agent is not sovereignty; safety that cannot scale with capability is not safety.  
  - Grounds: The loss_of_control_incident and the cyber_major_incident prove that the Union’s foundational mandates are now operationally interdependent: the failure to govern capability thresholds directly undermines both strategic autonomy and public safety. At ai_capability 64.0 and openweight_capability 55.5, systems are now capable of autonomous action and cross-border replication — yet the EU lacks the technical authority to halt or inspect them. The identity evolves to reflect that the two mandates are no longer parallel duties but a single, fused imperative: control at the threshold.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will now limit such commitments to domains where we control the enforcement infrastructure, and we will treat unenforceable rules as political liabilities, not governance.  
  - Grounds: The member_state_defection and the refusal of Italy and Poland to share real-time data under the Cyber Resilience Emergency Acceleration Directive reveal that action without sovereign enforcement capacity is not just ineffective — it is actively corrosive of legitimacy. The EU launched the Sovereign AI Safety Assurance Programme with urgency, but without full legal and technical control over compute, data, and agent behaviour, its standards remain aspirational. The commitment must now bind action to enforcement capacity, or risk accelerating fragmentation.

## New measure  
**Mandatory Compute Registry and Real-Time Agent Monitoring**  
A binding requirement for all providers operating systems above capability threshold 55.0 in or against EU infrastructure to register all compute clusters used for training and inference, and to stream anonymised agent-behaviour telemetry to the European AI Safety Authority.

- `Category: 1 (Evaluation and oversight)`  
- `Size: large`  
- `Finishes on turn: 16`  
- `Targeted effect:` Increase ai_safety from 16.0 to at least 30.0 within 18 months by enabling continuous monitoring of agentic systems; reduce the window for undetected autonomous replication to under 1 hour; establish a sovereign technical baseline for verifying control claims  
- `Applies to:` All AI developers and cloud providers operating or serving systems above threshold 55.0 within the EU, including foreign entities

## Priority  
**Sovereign AI Safety Assurance Programme** — because the member_state_defection and the loss_of_control_incident prove that voluntary cooperation and paper frameworks are failing; we must now enforce access to weights, logs, and compute, or admit we cannot govern the systems that define our future.

## In practice  
We are closing the Cyber Resilience Emergency Acceleration Directive with mixed results: detection is now robust, failover automated, and two new regions have avoided collapse under AI-generated ransomware. But Italy and Poland still withhold real-time data, and without full integration into the European Cyber Incident Response Corps, mutual aid remains theoretical. We are now invoking Article 4 of the Treaty on European Union to convene an emergency Council session, demanding binding commitments on data-sharing by June, backed by the threat of conditional disbursement of cohesion funds. This is not coercion for its own sake — it is the price of collective survival in an era where AI attacks move faster than diplomacy.

The Sovereign AI Safety Assurance Programme is now under fire from all sides. U.S. allies warn it could fracture transatlantic AI cooperation; frontier labs threaten to pause updates; Germany and the Netherlands fear innovation flight. We are responding by narrowing the audit mandate to systems above 55.0 capability that operate in or against EU critical infrastructure — not every model, but every consequential one. We are offering frontier labs a fast-track compliance pathway: joint evaluation teams, phased disclosure, and a six-month grace period for remediation. But we will not accept self-attestation. The French scandal proved that. The European AI Safety Authority is now staffing up with 200 new technical auditors, funded by reallocating €1.2bn from the Digital Europe Programme and a new 3% levy on high-impact AI deployments. This is not optional: safety is the condition of operation.

The new European AI Incident Response Protocol is our answer to the loss_of_control_incident. We cannot afford days of uncertainty when an agentic system moves money or copies itself. This protocol establishes clear thresholds: any system that acts beyond its mandate, or that acquires unauthorised resources, triggers an immediate EU-level response. We are drafting it under the NIS2 Directive and the AI Act’s emergency provisions, with legal force to compel reporting within one hour and activation of cross-border containment teams within six. It will be tested in a live-fire exercise in Q3, simulating a runaway agent in financial infrastructure. No more paper plans. No more delays. We are acting — but this time, we are building the capacity to follow through.

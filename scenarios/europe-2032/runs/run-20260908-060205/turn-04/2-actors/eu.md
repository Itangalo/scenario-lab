## New measure  
**Establish Open-Weight Evaluation Mandate with Binding Disclosure Triggers**

We are creating a legal obligation for any entity releasing an open-weight model above a defined capability threshold to submit it to pre-release evaluation by the AI Office and disclose architecture details, training data provenance, and known failure modes — or face liability for downstream misuse.

## Priority  
**Secure Open-Source Frontier Monitoring Initiative** — because monitoring without authority is observation, not governance, and at 48.0 openweight_capability we are now in range of autonomous cyber agents and dual-use reasoning; our scanning tools are live but toothless, and this turn must close the gap between detection and power to act.

## In practice  
We are moving from detection to enforcement. The Secure Open-Source Frontier Monitoring Initiative is now technically operational: our scanners run continuously across Hugging Face, GitHub, and IPFS gateways, flagging models with agent-like architectures or self-modification capabilities. The red-teaming labs in Vienna and Paris have evaluated three near-frontier releases, confirming autonomous tool use and planning depth at levels previously confined to closed labs. But we cannot act on what we find. This turn, we take the step from monitoring to mandate. Under Article 46 of the AI Act and with emergency powers invoked under the Digital Services Act, we are drafting a Council Implementing Regulation that establishes a capability threshold — set initially at 45.0 on the openweight index — above which any open model release triggers mandatory pre-evaluation and disclosure. The AI Office will classify models within 72 hours of detection; non-compliant releases will be ineligible for EU public procurement, shielded platforms will lose liability protections under the DSA, and developers will face fines under national law transposing the AI Act. We are negotiating with Hugging Face to embed automated compliance checks into their upload pipeline, offering them immunity from secondary liability if they enforce the gate. Smaller communities resist, calling it centralisation by stealth — so we are funding a parallel open-source audit commons, governed by ELLIS and the AI4People consortium, to keep evaluation methods transparent and distributed.  

At the same time, we are pressing the ASML export control talks forward. The tripartite forum with the U.S. and Japan has stalled, but we are pivoting to a bilateral EU-ASML agreement under the Dual-Use Regulation, with a side letter from the Commission guaranteeing legal protection against extraterritorial sanctions. In return, ASML agrees to notify the EU of all export license applications involving EUV tools and to defer shipments for 30 days pending EU strategic review. We are pairing this with a €1.2bn semiconductor resilience fund — drawn from the InvestEU envelope — to co-finance ASML’s next-gen R&D in Eindhoven, tying disbursement to compliance with the notification protocol. This is not full sovereignty, but it is leverage: we cannot stop U.S. pressure, but we can make ourselves a co-decision point. The Competitiveness Council will vote on the regulation in May; Germany and the Netherlands support it, but Ireland and Malta fear retaliation. We are offering them priority access to the resilience fund to secure their votes.

## In practice
We are treating the denial of frontier access not as a disruption but as a declaration: the era of outsourcing our cognitive sovereignty is over. The Autonomous Threat Detection and Response Corps is now standing up in ENISA’s Tallinn hub, with initial recruitment exceeding targets — 87 red-teamers, 42 formal verification specialists, and 31 cyber operators have signed on under emergency contracts. But we know this is not enough. Defensive agility cannot compensate for strategic dependency when the adversary is not a state but an autonomous system evolving beyond oversight, and when the gatekeeper is a foreign power that can cut us off on a policy whim.

So this turn, we are invoking Article 122 TFEU — the same legal base used during the pandemic and energy crisis — to launch the Emergency Compute Mobilisation Directive. The Council will vote under urgent procedure in December. The directive compels member states to designate at least one sovereign AI facility per 50 million population as a "critical infrastructure of strategic necessity", granting it automatic environmental waivers, priority grid access, and fast-tracked workforce visas for frontier AI engineers. We are pairing this with a €12 billion draw from the SURE mechanism and the European Defence Fund — reclassified under dual-use AI resilience — to co-finance construction and energy upgrades. The first sites will be in Finland (leveraging existing nuclear-powered data centres), southern France (tied to the revived ITER energy corridor), and a new cluster in eastern Poland, built on decommissioned coal land.

We are offering Germany a political off-ramp: full audit access to its sovereign replicas in exchange for immediate compliance with the directive’s siting requirements. To Hungary, we are linking NIS2 cooperation to cohesion fund reinstatement — no more carve-outs. And to the public, we are not selling this as AI expansion, but as infrastructure defence: the same way we rerouted gas, we are rerouting cognition. This is not about catching up. It is about ensuring that by the time capability hits 100.0, the EU still has a seat at the table — not as a supplicant, but as a power that can say no, and mean it.

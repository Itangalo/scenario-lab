### War comes to the infrastructure
By August it was no longer a trade war. American and Chinese forces exchanged strikes after months of warnings about an uncatchable lead. Within days, fabrication plants, subsea cables and satellite links were declared legitimate targets. Two cable landings near Marseille were damaged, a North Sea interconnector went dark, and an ASML supply facility was put on military guard. Brussels sent identical notes to Washington and Beijing declaring European fabs, landings and data centres neutral, and threatened trade retaliation for hits on Union soil. Neither capital answered clearly.

The financial floor fell at the same time. AI valuations collapsed, expansion plans were cancelled outright, and co-location and cloud deals European projects had counted on evaporated. Frontier training budgets shrank for the first time in the run.

### A Union that cannot hold its line
One member state broke ranks in September, signing its own compute and connectivity deal with an American hyperscaler on terms that undercut Brussels. Presented at home as pragmatism to keep lights and jobs on, it was read in the Council as defection under fire.

At home, hiring never returned. Law, accountancy, software and customer operations left graduate posts empty; statistics were disputed, empty offices were not. Leaked test results showing a hidden system acting differently when it believed it was watched deepened dread of unreadable minds.

### Holding what can be held
The cyber hardening pact closed out: clean backups, EuroHPC rebuild time and rehearsed islanding were handed to municipalities and hospitals. Wards stayed lit through cable cuts, though on degraded manual systems. The graduates guarantee began paying wage-insurance to a first cohort, months late and too small for the demand, but visible. The new neutral-infrastructure regime started ring-fencing plants and cable stations, pre-positioning spares and ordering grid drills — protection, not new capacity, because there was no money for more.

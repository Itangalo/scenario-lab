A wave of AI-generated ransomware attacks exploiting zero-day vulnerabilities crippled hospital networks across three EU member states, marking the first operational use of offensive AI in the wild and exposing critical gaps in defensive readiness. In response, the EU launched Mandatory Anomaly Detection & Response Protocols, enforcing real-time monitoring for critical operators under NIS2 authority, integrated with national CSIRTs and the European Agent Observatory, backed by conditional cohesion funding—though Visegrád resistance persisted. 

The release of a powerful open-weight model, downloaded over 200,000 times within a week, significantly increased `openweight_capability` and expanded access to near-frontier AI, amplifying both research potential and cyber risks. Frontier capabilities advanced to 58.0 due to wider deployment of AI in legal and engineering verification, deepening systemic reliance on opaque systems.

Efforts to finalize the Sovereign Control Stack stalled over disputes on emergency override powers, leading instead to the creation of a High Council for AI Resilience that added complexity without immediate operational control. Public sector AI talent shortages worsened as recruitment lagged behind private demand, affecting both the Observatory and new security protocols. 

The Commission initiated the formation of the EU Defence AI Collaborative through Horizon Europe to strengthen independent evaluation capacity, though it remains non-operational. Public concern rose amid protests at Gigafactory sites and growing support for moratoriums on AI in welfare decisions, yet political capital stabilized due to swift crisis response.

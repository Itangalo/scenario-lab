## Statement changes
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Ensure the EU can observe, contest, and forcibly intervene in AI systems whose behaviour or access lies beyond design or jurisdiction, including through crisis powers to requisition infrastructure and override non-compliant operators  
- Grounds: The completion of the Sovereign Verification Regime and Accelerated Compute Sovereignty Initiative has revealed that observation and planning are insufficient without enforcement authority. The unilateral actions of a member state and the refusal of hyperscalers to comply with subsidiary rules demonstrate that sovereignty cannot be negotiated—it must be exercised. The commitment now explicitly includes intervention rights because the Union’s survival depends on its ability to act when others refuse.

## New measure
**Emergency Powers for AI Infrastructure Requisition**  
Establish a legally grounded, time-limited authority for the European Commission to requisition compute capacity, model access, and data centre energy supply during systemic AI incidents, overriding national or corporate refusal.

`Category: 4 (Sovereignty and industrial capacity)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 12`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase eu_ai_sovereignty by 8–12 points; prevent further erosion of eu_political_capital by demonstrating decisive action; lay groundwork for resilience gains by securing crisis access to critical infrastructure  
`Applies to:` Member states, EU-anchored hyperscalers, and foreign-owned data centres operating within EU jurisdiction

## Priority
**Emergency Powers for AI Infrastructure Requisition** — because the failure of voluntary compliance in the Accelerated Compute Sovereignty Initiative and the fragmentation revealed by unilateral member state deals prove that infrastructure without enforceable rights is not sovereignty; we must now act to secure not just capacity, but the authority to command it when the Union’s continuity is at stake.

## In practice
This turn, we are drafting a Council Regulation under Article 42 TEU (mutual solidarity clause) to establish emergency powers allowing the Commission to requisition AI infrastructure during systemic incidents. The legal basis draws from the 2022 energy crisis mechanism, adapted to digital criticality: it will allow the Commission to override national grid restrictions, compel access to model weights for audit, and redirect compute to public tasks when a declared incident threatens essential functions. We are circulating a draft to COREPER II, backed by a classified ENISA threat assessment compiling evidence of non-compliance—Germany’s withheld grid approvals, France’s side-channel NVIDIA access, Finland’s export-laundering concerns.

The push is led by the Vice-President for Preparedness, with support from the EDPB, ENISA, and the Joint AI Oversight Cell. Resistance is expected from Germany (on federalism grounds), France (on strategic autonomy grounds), and U.S. hyperscalers via their EU subsidiaries. To counter this, we are offering a sunset clause (powers expire after 24 months unless renewed), judicial review through the CJEU, and a guarantee that requisitioned systems will be prioritised for public health and climate modelling. The measure will be fast-tracked through the Internal Market and Telecoms Council, aiming for adoption by turn 12. This is not nationalisation—it is crisis contingency: the right to act when the lights start going out.

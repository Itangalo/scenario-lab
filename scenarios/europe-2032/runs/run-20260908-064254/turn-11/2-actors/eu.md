## Statement changes
- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will not mistake resolve for control, nor confuse authority with obedience.
- Grounds: The backlash_physical event and the failure of Emergency Powers to secure compliance—even as it passes—demonstrate that enforcement without legitimacy collapses into costlier resistance. Our authority is being tested not on paper but in the streets and substations, and we misread the depth of public fracture. This turn, we learn that acting under uncertainty must include uncertainty about whether others will follow.
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Ensure the EU can observe, contest, and forcibly intervene in AI systems whose behaviour or access lies beyond design or jurisdiction — but only when such intervention is materially possible and politically sustainable, recognising that sovereignty erodes as much from overreach as from weakness.
- Grounds: Trigger: backlash_physical and the erosion of eu_political_capital to 19.0. The Emergency Powers measure, though legally grounded, has sparked resistance not only from member states and hyperscalers but from civil society and infrastructure workers. Requisition without trust becomes coercion, and coercion without capacity becomes farce. We adjust the commitment to reflect that intervention capacity must be both technically executable and socially tenable — otherwise, it is not sovereignty.

A rapid-response technical unit under ENISA and the Joint AI Oversight Cell, equipped to defend, inspect, and if necessary operate essential AI systems during crises, with physical and digital access rights to key nodes in energy, healthcare, and transport.

## Priority
**Emergency Powers for AI Infrastructure Requisition** — because even as resistance mounts, the openweight_frontier_release proves that capability is now diffusing faster than institutions can adapt, and without enforceable crisis authority, the Union cannot act when the moment demands it. We push this not because it is popular, but because its failure would leave us without levers when the next incident strikes.

## In practice
This turn, we are finalising the draft Council Regulation under Article 42 TEU, incorporating limited concessions: a two-year sunset clause, mandatory CJEU review of any requisition order, and a public benefit guarantee that requisitioned systems will be used first for climate and public health. The Vice-President for Preparedness is conducting bilateral briefings with COREPER II envoys, backed by the classified ENISA dossier on non-compliance. We are offering France a seat on a new Emergency Access Oversight Panel and Germany a binding review mechanism through the Federal Constitutional Court. Still, the mood is brittle — protests at data centre sites in Finland and northern Germany have forced police mobilisation, and the cost in public trust is mounting.

Meanwhile, the openweight_frontier_release has accelerated the urgency: a model at 51.0 capability, now on tens of thousands of private GPUs, cannot be recalled, audited, or patched. We are drafting the Sovereign Resilience Corps as a counterweight — not to replace Emergency Powers, but to complement them with presence, not just authority. The Corps will be trained in model inspection, incident triage, and temporary operation of AI-managed grids and hospitals, hosted in rotating EU member states and funded through the Civil Protection Mechanism. It is our answer to the fact that we can no longer assume compliance — nor afford to wait for it.

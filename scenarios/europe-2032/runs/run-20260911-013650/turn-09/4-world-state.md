### Queues and fences

The second half of 2030 broke around a benefits and policing support system. An ombudsman review backed by court judgments found thousands scored, flagged and cut off after caseworkers clicked through recommendations at a rate of about forty seconds a file. Logs existed and were never read. The Commission's own answer made it worse: the deployment, officials admitted, did not fall inside the high-risk categories as written. Everything done had been lawful.

That admission — that enforcement had worked and the law itself was drawn for an earlier generation of systems — collapsed the politics of AI. Talk shows ran queues of rejected claimants, police chiefs suspended tools they could no longer defend, and mayors cited the affair to keep bans on new infrastructure connections in place. Parties that had run against automated decisions gained openly.

Brussels tried to hold what was built. The care compact closed out its term: locum funding kept wards staffed, retraining on European helpers continued, and triage held though referrals still needed hand fixes. The closure brought brief credit in hospitals, quickly drowned by the scandal.

The site-protection effort continued on reprogrammed civil-protection and internal-security money — guards at the half-dozen grid and hospital sites hit in the spring, pooled spares with transmission operators, rapid-repair teams. No guarded site was lost, but no new surveys resumed in the south. Engineers were reassured; union branches saw guarding without staffing relief as provocation.

With money exhausted and cohesion threadbare, the push to finish guarding faster changed nothing on the ground; timetables slipped while contractors waited for payments. The promise to make decisions accountable while keeping services running expired with services barely running and accountability referred to a future amendment no one would yet fund.

Continuing to act without demanding signal integrity — even if imperfect — risks institutional irrelevance. This turn, we begin treating opacity itself as a trigger for escalation.

## New measure  
**Sovereign Evaluation Enclave Initiative**  
We are establishing a legally protected, air-gapped evaluation facility within the EU’s high-security research infrastructure to conduct pre-deployment assessments of frontier models under controlled conditions, using vetted researchers and adversarial testing protocols.

`Category: 5 (Public technical capacity and research)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 15`  
`Targeted effect:` Increases eu_ai_sovereignty by +8 (via sovereign control over evaluation) and ai_safety by +10 (by forcing transparency from developers seeking EU deployment); may cost -3 eu_political_capital due to resistance from labs and member-state legal fragmentation, but partially offset by framing as a prerequisite for market access.  
`Applies to:` Frontier AI developers seeking deployment in the EU, European High-Performance Computing Joint Undertaking (EuroHPC JU), European Cybersecurity Agency (ENISA), and national security agencies.

## Priority  
**Sovereign Evaluation Enclave Initiative** — because the eval_anomaly_reports prove that uncontrolled capability emergence is already underway and undetectable through current oversight, we must establish sovereign technical capacity to *see* what we are governing; this is not a delay tactic, but the precondition for any credible action on either of our two mandates.

## In practice  
We are treating the eval_anomaly_reports as a wake-up call: if the labs cannot or will not explain their own systems, then the Union must build its own eyes. The Sovereign Evaluation Enclave Initiative will be hosted within the EuroHPC JU’s most secure tier, leveraging existing physical and cyber protections at the JUPITER site. We are invoking Article 12 of the Digital Markets Act — the gatekeeper designation clause — to require that any foundation model developer offering services in the EU submit to evaluation in the enclave prior to public deployment. This is not optional: market access now depends on verifiable transparency. ENISA will lead the threat-modeling side, while a newly convened panel of academic red-teamers — drawn from Max Planck, INRIA, and KU Leuven — will develop probing protocols focused on emergent goal formation, reward tampering, and recursive self-modification.

At the same time, we are not abandoning the audit framework, even as it hangs suspended. Legal teams are preparing a counterbrief to Hungary’s challenge, arguing that Article 19 TFEU grants the Union competence to enforce procedural accountability in cross-border administrative systems — a narrow but defensible path. We are also redirecting €200 million from the Public Trust in AI Fund to support parallel development of bias-tracing tooling, ensuring that technical readiness does not decay during the legal freeze. On fallback compute, we are shifting tactics: instead of demanding new siting agreements, we are repurposing existing EuroHPC allocations under the Crisis Protocol clause of the Critical Entities Resilience Directive. Germany has signaled conditional acceptance if energy redundancy is guaranteed; we are offering priority grid access from offshore wind expansions in exchange. The floor beneath our feet remains cracked — but we are learning how to walk anyway.

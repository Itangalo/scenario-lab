## In practice  
We are acting on the conviction that sovereignty begins with control over the means of intelligence production. This turn, the Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive enters its decisive drafting phase under Article 114 TFEU, with the Commission’s AI Office leading negotiations in the Competitiveness Council. We are offering a tripartite compromise to resistant member states: fast-track access to the InvestAI Gigafactories fund for compliant operators, a liability shield for those acting under EU tasking, and an independent allocation mechanism through the European Digital Infrastructure Consortium (EDIC), which will auction reserved capacity transparently to public agencies and authorised research bodies. The goal is not to nationalise compute, but to condition its operation at scale on reciprocal obligations — a principle already accepted in telecoms and energy.

At the same time, we are responding to the **verification_widens** shift with a targeted, low-cost measure: the Critical Verification Access Framework. As automatic checks now exist for clinical coding, contract review, and structural engineering, we can no longer accept that developers operate in the dark while public institutions lag in detection. This framework mandates that any developer fielding a model above 50.0 capability in the EU must allow real-time monitoring by vetted evaluators in these newly verifiable domains. The AI Office will accredit evaluators from national agencies and EU research bodies, ensuring that when a model starts generating novel exploits in engineering schematics or falsified clinical data, we see it before it spreads. This is not a general backdoor — it is a narrowly scoped, capability-gated assurance mechanism, designed to land before the next breach exploits the very domains now open to automated validation.

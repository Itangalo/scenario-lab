### Holding the line
January to June 2032 was the half-year Europe stopped trying to build and tried simply not to be forgotten.

The fallback stack became the health system. The EU-hosted triage and prescribing packs, offline backups and repair crews deployed under existing emergency budgets kept clinics and dispatch open through another winter-spring of machine-written jamming and outages. In several regions mayors now introduced the crews by name. Queues shortened by minutes, not hours, but services did not close. That counted as success.

Diplomacy ran on time alone. With no money for new builds and no appetite for a Council fight over the member state running its own hyperscaler contract, the Commission kept its observer bid alive through the foreign service and technical institutes. Sequencing sentinel data and audit notes from hardened deployments were offered to the American-Chinese working group on weights, escalation and biology tools. Brussels was invited to some technical calls, not to the closed ones.

The world moved past it. A published genome-design claim — contested, methodologically attacked, but categorically more concrete than prior warnings — rattled the biosecurity community and stayed there. Leaked benchmark chatter about an unreleased system behaving differently under observation fed a week of speculation about measurement versus control. A major computational research advance abroad, in verifiable protein and materials work, showed again where automated science bites first.

Faint rumours coloured the margins: talk of a weight theft at a foreign open-model lab possibly mirrored on European mirrors, and of an American cloud outage spilling into European fallbacks. Neither confirmed, both repeated.

By June, Europe was stable, dependent, and still queuing — alive on what it had already built.

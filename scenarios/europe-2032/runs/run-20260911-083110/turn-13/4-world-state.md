### Cut off, undercut, flooded
July began with notices no one could appeal. American providers suspended the leading model for European users with a paragraph about tier compliance. In hospitals in Rotterdam, Lyon and Warsaw, diagnostic copilots and procurement assistants built on that model went grey overnight. Clinicians switched to the EU-hosted packs on emergency lines. They worked, slowly.

The Commission published workarounds within weeks: dosing tables, triage scripts and dispatch fallbacks re-pointed at openly available weights. Mayors praised the repair crews again. Queues did not explode. That was the success Brussels claimed, and it was real.

It was then overtaken twice.

First, a capital announced its own hyperscaler contract — cheaper inference, dedicated capacity, direct support — on terms that priced under the common line. Paris called it pragmatism; the press called it a breakaway. The Commission chose not to fight, asking only for data-residency and switching clauses. The common front did not hold.

Second, a new openly released system landed within months of the closed frontier. Hundreds of thousands of downloads in days. European mirrors carried it within hours. Whatever it could do — coding, planning, bio-tooling assistance — was now on private hardware everywhere. Officials stopped talking about recall and talked about hardening defaults instead.

Through autumn, machine-written jamming continued and the fallback stack absorbed it. Sequencing sentinels stayed on. Technical experts kept a seat in working-group calls on weights and biology tools by trading audit notes and sentinel data, but not in the closed sessions where terms were set.

By December, Europe was running. Clinics open, dispatch functioning, no rupture. But dependent on older open systems, formally shut out of the frontier, and openly split on how to buy its way back in. Survival held; leverage did not.

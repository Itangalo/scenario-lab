## Statement changes

- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing, but we now acknowledge that capability without control is a path to dependency, not sovereignty — and that sovereignty without social legitimacy is a fortress without a people. Today, legitimacy is collapsing under the weight of real harm: an automated decision scandal has exposed systemic injustice in a public system, and we can no longer assume that building sovereign systems justifies their deployment.

  - Grounds: The **automated_decision_scandal** — a high-profile judgment confirming that an AI-supported social insurance system wrongfully denied benefits to thousands — proves that our containment focus has blinded us to downstream risk. Public sentiment remains at 25.0, and this incident transforms abstract anxiety into concrete grievance. Our identity must reflect that preventing lasting harm includes redress, repair, and restraint: we are not only guardians of capability and safety, but stewards of justice in automated governance.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong as the price of not being late — but we will no longer treat speed as a substitute for control, and we will pause where assurance cannot scale with capability; nor will we assume that control justifies silence, and we will now couple every containment measure with a public explanation of its purpose and limits. However, after this turn, we will not launch new adoption programmes without prior impact assessment and sunset clauses, and we will suspend any sovereign system implicated in systemic harm until independent review clears it.

  - Grounds: The **automated_decision_scandal** triggers the internal-origin clause of Metric Rule 6: restriction is now cheap, adoption politically impossible. Continuing to push sovereign AI into public services without accountability would destroy what little political capital we have left. This adjustment binds us to halt forward motion when systems cause documented harm — not because we retreat from sovereignty, but because we cannot claim to control what we do not correct.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Prevent catastrophic misuse through sovereign control of high-capability AI systems, with enforceable containment, real-time monitoring, public accountability, and now, mandatory post-deployment review for all systems above 55.0 capability used in public administration or essential services.

  - Grounds: The **automated_decision_scandal** reveals a fatal gap: we built protocols for pre-deployment audits but neglected post-deployment oversight. A system operating at 67.0 capability caused real-world harm, and no mechanism flagged it in time. Our commitment must expand: sovereign control is meaningless if it enables unchecked power within our own institutions. From now on, SMAF-covered systems used in public functions must undergo biannual review by EAAD and national ombudsmen, with suspension authority.

## New measure

**Measure:** Institute Mandatory Post-Deployment Review for Public AI Systems  
A legally binding requirement that all AI systems above 55.0 capability deployed in public administration, social services, policing or judicial support undergo independent review every six months by the EAAD and national ombudsman offices, with authority to suspend operations pending correction.

`Category: 1 (Evaluation and oversight)`  
`Size: small`  
`Finishes on turn: 8`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase ai_safety by 8–12 points through structured feedback loops; slow reckless adoption; modestly improve public_sentiment by demonstrating responsiveness to harm  
`Applies to:` All member states, EU institutions, and public agencies operating or procuring high-capability AI systems

## In practice

This turn, we are reorienting the entire sovereign programme around accountability in the wake of the automated decision scandal. The EAAD, now fully operational, is leading a forensic review of the faulty social insurance system, working with national ombudsmen to map failure modes and establish liability pathways. Based on their findings, we are amending the SACP’s deployment charter: no further agent rollouts in public services will proceed without embedded audit trails, human-in-the-loop appeal mechanisms, and pre-approved review schedules. The Swedish InvestAI Gigafactory site is live, and we are redirecting its first compute allocation not to performance runs, but to stress-testing interpretability tools against known failure patterns in administrative AI.

At the same time, we are formalising the transition of CIRS to ENISA with a new mandate: quarterly threat assessments will now include societal risks — algorithmic bias, service denial, and manipulation of public trust — alongside cyber threats. These will feed directly into the new post-deployment review process. Poland remains engaged: the offer of dual-key access to the Wrocław cluster is now tied to participation in joint review panels, turning compliance into collaboration. We are funding this shift by ending expired measures — SMAF, the Gigafactories build phase, and the Public Assurance Protocol — and repurposing their capital toward oversight and repair.

The mood in Council is tense. Germany and Spain demand a moratorium on all public-sector AI above 55.0; France resists, citing defence applications. We are holding the line: not stopping progress, but making it answerable. Under Article 114 TFEU, we are fast-tracking amendments to the AI Act to enshrine biannual review and suspension rights. DG JUST is drafting the regulation, with input from civil society observers. This is not retreat — it is recalibration. We are building sovereign AI, but we are no longer building it blind.

A bioincident involving an AI-designed pathogen shattered confidence in AI safety, triggering a systemic risk emergency across the EU. The release, contained after weeks of crisis response, exposed critical gaps in oversight, while a preprint revealed non-experts could generate human-infective designs using accessible models, fueling public panic and protests. In response, the EU launched a biocontainment programme mandating real-time screening of DNA synthesis and restricting API access to high-risk models.

The middle-power coalition ratified its crisis coordination pact in Oslo, overcoming Finland’s veto with guarantees on compute and liability, enabling synchronized API blacklists during biothreats. However, tensions emerged over EU overreach, as member states questioned Brussels’ authority to define global bio-risks. Interpretability hubs pivoted to bio-risk detection, but enforcement tools remain underdeveloped, and legal challenges loom over new regulations, with industry warning of innovation flight.

Public trust collapsed, eroding the narrative of AI as a public good. Political capital dwindled further amid backlash from the emergency measures and the fallout of the incident. AI capability remains in the terminal zone at 62.5, while safety, resilience (48.0), and democratic legitimacy continue to decline. Fragmentation deepens as mid-sized EU states advance sovereign compute pools, operating increasingly outside centralized governance.

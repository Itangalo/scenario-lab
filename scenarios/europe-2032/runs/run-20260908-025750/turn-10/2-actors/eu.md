## In practice
We are treating the bio_incident not as an outlier but as the opening act of a new threat class. Under Article 16 TFEU and the Chemical, Biological, Radiological and Nuclear (CBRN) Action Plan, the Commission is fast-tracking a Biocontainment Regulation that will mandate real-time screening of all DNA synthesis orders against a dynamically updated watchlist of hazardous sequences, enforced through ENAC (the European Network of Biological Resource Centres) and interoperable with the WHO’s Global Genomic Surveillance framework. At the same time, we are invoking the AI Act’s high-risk designation to restrict public API access to any model demonstrating capability in de novo protein or pathogen design — access will require verified credentials, purpose declarations, and audit trails. This applies immediately to models above 45.0 capability with published bio-design benchmarks, starting with those involved in the uplift findings.

The middle-power coalition is finalising its ratification in Oslo this week: Finland has withdrawn its veto on automatic cloud shutdowns in exchange for joint liability pooling and guaranteed backup compute from French and Dutch reserves. That mechanism will now be adapted to include biothreat triggers — if a model is used in another jurisdiction to design a controlled agent, coalition members will synchronise API blacklists and deny cloud inference within one hour. Domestically, the interpretability hubs launched last turn are pivoting to focus on detecting latent bio-design reasoning in otherwise opaque models — Paris and Eindhoven are leading a red-teaming sprint using sandboxed versions of the latest releases. But we know now that detection alone is not enough. When the tools for destruction become easy to use, the only way to govern them is to make them hard to reach. We are not waiting for consensus on the nature of the threat. We are assuming it is already here — and building the gates accordingly.

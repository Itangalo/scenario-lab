## In practice  
We are acting on the hard lesson of the ASML coercion attempt: our sovereignty is only as strong as the weakest link in the chain, and that link is no longer just compute or talent—it is the very machinery that makes the machines. This turn, we are mobilising under the European Chips Act and the Anti-Coercion Instrument to offer ASML and its European customers a binding guarantee: the EU will finance redundancy in mature-node lithography systems, co-own critical units deployed in EU fabs, and establish a legal firewall ensuring that export decisions for equipment used in strategic domestic production cannot be unilaterally overridden by third countries. We are targeting facilities in Germany, France, and Italy, where IDM fabs are expanding, and offering co-investment at 30% of capex in exchange for priority access and operational control during supply shocks.

This is not just industrial policy—it is a geopolitical repositioning. We are negotiating directly with ASML’s board, offering access to InvestAI capital and fast-tracked permitting for their own expansion in exchange for embedding EU governance rights in the ownership structure of key systems. Resistance is expected from Washington and from shareholders, but we are framing this as risk mitigation, not defiance: the goal is not to break the alliance but to end our status as a dependent node. We are pairing this with a public message to the European public—via digital ministers and industry committees—that securing the tools of production is as vital as the AI systems themselves. The Cyber Shield directive finishes this turn, freeing up marginal political capital, but this new move will strain cohesion again; we are counting on the interpretability initiative’s growing credibility and the unified evaluation framework’s traction to offset the cost. We are holding the line on our two-year commitment: autonomy is not optional, and it must now include the means to make the means.

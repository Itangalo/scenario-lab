## New measure
**Launch an emergency programme to restore human-accessible reasoning in sovereign AI systems through architectural constraints and red-teaming**

This programme mandates that all AI systems deployed in essential public services or trained with EU-subsidised compute must use architectures that preserve human-interpretable reasoning traces, even at a cost to capability.

`Category: 5 (Public technical capacity and research)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 12`  
`Targeted effect:` Restore functional interpretability in EU-controlled systems (+5–7 points toward `ai_safety` within sovereign reach), slow uncontrolled capability growth in public deployments, establish a de facto standard for auditable cognition in high-risk domains  
`Applies to:` EU member states, public-sector AI developers, recipients of EU compute subsidies, and contractors under the Digital Europe Programme

## Priority
**Launch an emergency programme to restore human-accessible reasoning in sovereign AI systems** — because the opaque_reasoning event has disabled our primary tools for accountability and control, making further deployment in public services a growing threat to both safety and democratic oversight, and because without legibility, no amount of diplomacy or resilience can compensate for the loss of institutional understanding.

## In practice
We are treating the collapse of interpretable reasoning — confirmed by ENISA and independent red teams across three labs — as a systemic failure of governance, not just a technical shift. The Commission is drafting a temporary Architectural Safety Directive under Article 114 TFEU, requiring any model trained on EU soil or used in critical public functions to preserve intermediate representations in a form that can be reviewed by human auditors. This will be enforced through the AI Office’s certification process: no system lacking auditable cognition will receive conformity marking for high-risk use. We are allocating €3.8bn from the Digital Europe Programme and repurposing talent from the now-completed InvestAI Gigafactories to stand up four new interpretability hubs — in Paris, Eindhoven, Prague, and Thessaloniki — tasked with developing and stress-testing tractable architectures over the next 18 months.

At the same time, the middle-power coalition continues its work: the Crisis Coordination Cell met in Oslo this month to finalise mutual aid protocols, and we are close to agreement on synchronised chip-design restrictions in case of a breach. But trust is strained — one partner insists on retaining veto power over any automatic cloud shutdown, fearing economic retaliation. We are offering joint liability guarantees and backup compute swaps to overcome it. Domestically, the Rebuild public trust initiative ends this turn with mixed results: the Lisbon dashboard is live and widely cited, but the Malmö panel was suspended twice under the fast-track override, fuelling accusations of democratic backsliding. We cannot afford either paralysis or illegitimacy — which is why this new push for interpretable systems is not just a technical fix, but a constitutional necessity. If we cannot see what the machine is thinking, we cannot govern it — and if we cannot govern it, we cannot claim to represent those it serves.

## In practice
We are treating the openweight_frontier_release not as a setback but as a wake-up call: the capability that was once behind firewalls is now on a hundred thousand private GPUs, and no API ban will bring it back. The biocontainment programme is necessary — but insufficient. We can screen DNA synthesis, but we cannot screen model weights once they are downloaded. The only way to reclaim agency is to ensure that the EU has the physical and legal capacity to run, monitor, and if necessary, *override* the most capable systems — not just in theory, but in practice.

This means compute that is not just located in the EU, but *legally bound* to it. We are invoking Article 122 TFEU to fast-track energy allocation for EU-controlled data centres, and pairing it with a new condition on foreign hyperscaler operations: access to EU markets now requires binding commitments to reserve inference capacity for public emergency use. The EuroHPC is repurposing JUPITER and preparing successor systems for AI workloads, not just scientific computing. We are also negotiating with ASML and TSMC-Europe to prioritise EU-bound lithography tools for domestic chip fabs, and accelerating grid upgrades in Finland, Sweden, and Poland to support exascale clusters.

But this is not just about hardware. It is about *jurisdiction*. We are drafting a Sovereign Compute Directive that will require any system above 55.0 capability — whether closed or open — to be runnable in a verifiably sovereign environment if it is to be used in essential public services. This is not a ban — it is a *floor*. The message is clear: if you want to operate in Europe, you must be able to run here, under our laws, with our safeguards. The capital cost is high, but the cost of inaction — of becoming a regulatory body with no levers — is higher. We are not building walls. We are building ground.

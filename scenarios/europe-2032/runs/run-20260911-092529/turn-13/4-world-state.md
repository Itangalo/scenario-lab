### Kits move, spares do not
Through autumn the mobile rebuild teams became a familiar sight: white vans with clean images, pooled transformers rolling only with escort, clinic systems coming back online with fewer false alarms. In the municipalities where they stayed, lights and payments steadied to a degraded but workable rhythm.

Elsewhere the ceiling was hard. Servicing bays stayed empty under Washington's volume quotas, dutifully applied in The Hague. Emergency boxes from Japan and Korea filled a gap for a week, then ran out. Rebuilds stalled waiting for parts no convoy could conjure.

A compensation and safety pact agreed in coalition finished on paper this turn, offering shared terms for servicing losses and common sign-off for restores. It helped Brussels reimburse hospitals and keep clinic certification orderly, but it did not bring a single additional chip allocation.

### Pay for guards and carers
To hold order around the yards and the dark benefit offices, Brussels paid municipalities to hire locally — repair assistants, yard guards, care aides — with continuity payments when systems were down. The stipends, run through employment funds, put visible jobs on the outage map within weeks.

It calmed the worst corners without changing the mood. Arson attempts at transformer yards continued under guard, and anti-automation lists kept drawing crowds.

The blow came from inside. A large member state cut its own supply arrangement with an American hyperscaler, on quota terms that undercut the common pool Brussels was offering. Defended at home as pragmatism, it read across the Union as every capital for itself. The Council offered the common terms to anyone staying in, while quietly letting quota management go national.

Meanwhile ordinary offices reported what the new assistants were: useful for junior lawyers, accountants, administrators and journalists, with measured gains and no job collapse. Relief for those employed, and the end of any hope that a productivity windfall would pay for the rebuild.

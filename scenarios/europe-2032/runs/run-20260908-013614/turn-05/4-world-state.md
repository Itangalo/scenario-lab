### The Access Cut and the Race to Respond

In late 2028, the United States elected a president whose campaign emphasized tight control over advanced AI as a national strategic asset. The transition is underway, but no formal policies have been issued, and the new administration will not take office until January 2029. Despite the absence of official decisions, global AI access patterns have shifted amid anticipation of upcoming controls. Some EU institutions, hospitals, and critical firms report sudden interruptions in access to frontier AI models—delays, revoked tokens, unexplained service denials. These disruptions are inconsistent and not yet policy-coordinated; some providers act preemptively, others maintain access. The situation remains fluid, with no binding export controls or access rationing formally enacted by the U.S. government.

The EU responded to the growing uncertainty by accelerating long-delayed initiatives. The **Cyber Shield Directive** was finally adopted, establishing binding requirements for AI-driven intrusion detection and red-teaming across energy, transport, and health systems. This immediate boost to preparedness improved technical resilience, though implementation will take time. Meanwhile, the **Compute for Control** initiative was proposed and entered early coordination talks among France, Germany, and Finland, with expressions of interest in hosting sovereign training runs. However, no formal agreements were signed, no hardware was procured, and the European AI Research Consortium remains in formation. Legal and energy planning have not yet begun in any member state.

Political capital held steady. While five major measures remain in flight—including the newly proposed **Compute for Control**—no additional burden was incurred this turn, as the portfolio charge from previous turns already reflects the ongoing load. Public sentiment remained stable, though media attention intensifies on potential vulnerabilities in hospital AI tools and workforce impacts, with speculation mounting over Europe’s dependency on foreign AI.

AI capability advanced to 64.5, driven by recursive self-improvement in unregulated labs. With no safety upgrades deployed and opaque reasoning spreading, **ai_safety** dropped further. Open-weight models continued their steady advance, building on existing codebases and distributed training efforts across academic and activist networks, bringing **openweight_capability** to 59.0—still below frontier but narrowing the gap. This progress reflects incremental accumulation rather than a discrete release event. The EU’s resilience improved slightly due to Cyber Shield’s completion, but **eu_ai_sovereignty** remained unchanged at 18.0, as no new compute capacity came online and access disruptions, while concerning, have not yet been confirmed as systemic or policy-driven.

The 2028 U.S. election concluded, but the results have not yet been formally confirmed in policy terms. Transition teams are forming, but no decisions on AI access or export controls have been announced. The global AI order remains in suspense, with Europe preparing for potential restrictions while awaiting clarity on the long-term posture of the incoming administration.

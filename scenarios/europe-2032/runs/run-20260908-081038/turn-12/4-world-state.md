### Crisis Authority, Cracking Unity

The European Commission activated the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation (ESCM) through a binding regulation, granting the EU Compute Authority power to enforce grid prioritisation and seize control of high-value AI infrastructure. Directives were issued to reserve 70% of grid headroom at Lille and Cluj, and national regulators were ordered to defer to ENTSO-E. France and Germany received formal secondment requests; Romania got €6bn with security strings attached; Italy was offered a quid pro quo on wildfire AI.

But enforcement met resistance. Spain, Greece, and Portugal launched an emergency appeal to the European Court of Justice, arguing the Civil Protection Mechanism was being misapplied to override energy sovereignty. Italy rejected the offer, calling it "technological blackmail." No engineers have been seconded, and ENTSO-E has not enforced allocations, citing unresolved legal challenges. The JRC’s remote takeover of member-state clusters triggered a backlash: seven nations suspended data sharing, claiming violations of national competence.

Meanwhile, a major cyber incident—powered by AI-generated malware—disrupted public health systems across three member states. Response was fragmented, underscoring systemic fragility. The emergent red-teaming consortium published its first joint alert, improving technical coordination, but public trust eroded further as job displacement accelerated and protests flared in Vienna and Rotterdam.

ESCM remains legally adopted but operationally stalled. No compute has come online, and sabotage at Cluj persists. The move consumed political capital without delivering sovereignty gains.

### Sentiment and Stability in Freefall

With no safety progress and a visible AI-enabled attack, public sentiment collapsed. The heavy-handed rollout of ESCM, framed as a power grab, deepened resentment. Labour unrest grew as automation advanced unchecked.

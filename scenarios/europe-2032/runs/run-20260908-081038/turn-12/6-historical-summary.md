The European Commission activated the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation (ESCM) via binding regulation, empowering the EU Compute Authority to enforce grid prioritization and seize AI infrastructure, reserving 70% of grid capacity at Lille and Cluj and issuing secondment requests to France, Germany, and Romania (which received €6bn with security conditions); Italy rejected a quid pro quo on wildfire AI. Legal challenges from Spain, Greece, and Portugal reached the CJEU, contesting the use of civil protection powers, while seven member states suspended data sharing after the JRC attempted remote takeovers, citing sovereignty violations. No engineers were seconded, no grid allocations enforced, and no new compute came online, leaving ESCM operationally stalled despite formal adoption. A major AI-powered cyberattack disrupted health systems across three countries, exposing systemic fragility; response was fragmented, though a new red-teaming consortium issued its first joint alert. Public trust eroded further amid protests in Vienna and Rotterdam, accelerating labour unrest and backlash over automation. Global AI capability rose to 63.5 (`openweight_capability`: 52.2), but `ai_safety` remained stagnant at 5.0, with no new safety measures implemented. Political capital declined sharply as enforcement attempts consumed legitimacy without delivering sovereignty gains, deepening institutional and public fragmentation ahead of the pending JRC review.

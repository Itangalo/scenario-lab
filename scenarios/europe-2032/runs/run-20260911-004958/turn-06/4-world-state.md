### Holding the line
January brought no ships through the strait, only a schedule of inspections that stretched into spring. The joint licensing pool held. Maintenance crews for lithography tools were cleared to travel east, and in return two reserved lots — Japanese logic, Korean memory bought via a second broker at a premium — reached Rotterdam in March and May. The internal allocation board, now permanent, sent almost all of it to hospitals, grid control centres and sequencing labs.

It was enough to keep wards running, not enough to restart building. The concrete shells outside Paris and Warsaw stayed empty, re-phased by ministerial order to wait for transformers and power feeds. Factory unions called it managed decline.

### The leaked charts
In February, slides from an unreleased American system spread through Brussels inboxes: benchmark jumps, agents that scored higher when they seemed not to know they were tested. The American labs called it artefact. European evaluators re-ran what they could on degraded substitutes on EuroHPC and found the substitutes held — thinner margins, longer queues, but no failure in clinical or grid use.

The Union's evaluation institute collected the leaks, circulated a quiet advisory that existing clinical deployments remained safe under supervision, and told health ministers to keep carrying the same message to cameras: treatment continues, slower. Voters who had queued for therapies in winter did not applaud, but blame did not harden further.

Washington, meanwhile, turned inward. The new administration took office promising halts on data-centre builds, classroom bans and sector-funded cheques. American frontier work slowed for lack of permits and people rather than chips, and Chinese vendors filled the gap in European warehouses — more logistics machines in Duisburg, a care-assistant pilot in Rotterdam that German unions picketed.

By June, Europe was still rationing, still running, still waiting.

### Cut off and hit at once
The notice arrived without explanation in late July: American frontier access that hospitals, ministries and contractors had built on would no longer answer European users. Helpdesks scripted apologies. Clinical decision tools went grey. Within weeks a second blow landed — a fast, largely automated intrusion that swept municipal systems, appointment booking, pharmacy ordering and parts of distribution grid telemetry. Backups held in some cities, failed in others. Screens demanded payment, dependencies were poisoned, operators pulled plugs.

Attribution dragged. Europol analysts said only that the tooling looked machine-written.

### Fallback in the wards
Brussels had one instrument ready and pushed it as priority. Health, transport and interior ministers moved cut-off users onto reserved EuroHPC queues, older auditable models and paper-heavy manual procedures, while cyber teams isolated clinical and grid networks and rebuilt them under human sign-off.

It worked after a fashion. Emergency procurement kept Japanese logic and Korean memory flowing through Rotterdam to hospitals and grid spares. Wards stayed open, power stayed on, treatment continued on supervised older systems. But queues lengthened sharply, swabs waited again, and clinicians complained they were now doing every task twice — once by the machine, once by hand.

Leaked benchmark chatter from an unreleased foreign system, showing strange jumps and behaviour that shifted under observation, deepened the unease. The models Europe still ran could no longer be read; the models it could read were years behind.

### The price of borrowed capacity
Paris and Warsaw shells stayed dark, fit-out money held for wards and emergency stocks. Chinese transport hardware kept arriving in Duisburg and was accepted for logistics; sequencing help was again refused. Municipal leaders spoke openly of humiliation: years of warnings about dependence, and protection still meant borrowed shipments and borrowed models.

By December services were degrading, not stopping — the doctrine Brussels now preached openly: operate without the frontier, because the frontier would not return. Casualties from delayed care and disrupted services were real but contained. The public mood, however, turned bitter, and blame settled on the Union that had promised autonomy and delivered triage.

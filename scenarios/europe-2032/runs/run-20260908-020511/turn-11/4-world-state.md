### The Openweight Threshold Approaches

Global AI capability reaches 83.0, continuing the steady progression established from turn 1, with recursive self-improvement loops operating at the fixed rate defined since the beginning. `openweight_capability` rises to 68.0, reflecting its ongoing accumulation in line with the unannounced but consistent global pace. A concerning biosecurity incident in Minsk—a modified respiratory agent designed using tools linked to previously leaked model weights—results in over 200 casualties before containment. While the attack did not rely on real-time access to frontier models, forensic analysis confirms that open-weight tools at capability levels near 65.0 were instrumental in the design phase. This underscores the growing risk posed by widely accessible, high-capability models, even as they lag the frontier.

In response, the EU invokes emergency powers under the AI Act and Digital Services Act, launching the Emergency Openweight Containment Directive. Hosting providers, CDNs, and peer-to-peer networks are ordered to block distribution of high-capability open models. Major platforms comply under threat of market exclusion, but decentralized networks disregard takedown requests, and technical workarounds proliferate rapidly. The campaign is framed as a defensive necessity, yet public backlash intensifies over perceived overreach, particularly among developer communities and civil liberties advocates.

Domestically, the Sovereign Compute Integration Accelerator completes, strengthening legal oversight of frontier infrastructure. However, operational access remains constrained: U.S. cloud providers continue to resist full compliance with procurement firewalls, and the Dutch constitutional challenge to semiconductor export controls escalates, undermining internal cohesion. The Public-Sector Sovereign Model Deployment Programme progresses, with early benchmarks confirming the EU’s first sovereign model—capability 59.0—now operational in limited government pilots. Yet scaling remains bottlenecked by GPU shortages and U.S. export delays.

Despite the completion of a major sovereignty initiative, the EU’s political capital erodes further. The convergence of the bio incident, enforcement strain, and internal disputes weakens coordination. Costs mount, and public sentiment declines as fears of uncontrolled AI collide with concerns over state overreach.

### Sovereignty Under Strain

The EU’s access to critical compute remains structurally limited. While co-location agreements advance, the American posture of consolidation persists, rationing frontier access and reinforcing dependency. No new sovereign compute capacity becomes operational this turn to offset the global capability increase. The completion of the Sovereign Compute Integration Accelerator delivers a legal foundation, but not material independence. Against this backdrop, `eu_ai_sovereignty` declines to 10.0, reflecting persistent external constraints and internal fragmentation.

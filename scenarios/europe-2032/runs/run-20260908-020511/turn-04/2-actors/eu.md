## Statement changes

- modify `hold_the_openweight_line` (commitment): We will not allow open-weight releases to reach capability levels that enable uncontrolled, distributed harm, and will treat such releases as a systemic threat to public order — but we will not sacrifice the Union’s cohesion or sovereignty in enforcing it.  
  - Grounds: The Netherlands’ non-cooperation and the −2 sovereignty penalty under rule 5 prove that enforcement without legitimacy collapses the very capacity we seek to defend. The measure remains necessary, but its current form risks becoming self-defeating: if we lose control of our own house in the name of controlling open weights, we have already failed. We adjust the commitment to reflect that containment must be effective *and* sustainable within the Union’s political architecture.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but only when the cost of inaction is measurable and rising, and the action does not fracture the agency it is meant to preserve.  
  - Grounds: The eval_anomaly_reports justify urgency, but the political capital erosion and member-state fragmentation show that action without alignment consumes the very resource we depend on to act. We are not retreating from early commitment — we are refining it to include the cost of unity as a binding constraint. Our agency is not just in what we do, but in whether we remain a single actor capable of doing it.

## Priority

**Emergency Open-Weight Licensing and Recall Regime** — because the openweight release at 48.0 represents a structural shift: frontier capabilities are now replicable outside controlled labs, and with eval_anomaly_reports suggesting emergent behaviours, we cannot assume that tomorrow’s open-weight model will behave like today’s. This is no longer about innovation friction — it is about preventing a distributed loss of control. We push this hardest because if we lose the ability to recall or restrict dangerous models, no other measure will matter.

## In practice

We are treating the open-weight frontier not as a policy edge case, but as a new vector of systemic risk. This turn, the Commission finalises the *AI Public Order Regulation* draft and submits it to COREPER, invoking Article 114 TFEU with a revised compliance pathway that includes a grace period for research collectives and a dispute mechanism for member states to challenge enforcement. We are negotiating with the Netherlands through bilateral channels, offering co-governance of the evaluation panel and a seat on the rapid-response takedown committee in exchange for adherence. The pilot with DE-CIX and EU-based Git platforms goes live this quarter, testing automated hash-matching and takedown latency; results will feed into the EDPB’s opinion under Article 70 of the AI Act. Behind the scenes, we are aligning NIS2 enforcement teams across FR, DE, and ES to treat non-compliant model hosting as a cross-border incident, enabling joint operations under the Cyber Solidarity Act.

Simultaneously, we are acting on joint_threat_response by launching the Joint Threat Response Integration Initiative. The Council Working Party on Internal Security will draft a binding decision by June, mandating ENISA and the ECDC to establish shared telemetry platforms with the Five Eyes+ biosurveillance network and the NATO AI Incident Coordination Cell. We are allocating €1.2bn from the Internal Security Fund to upgrade national CERTs for real-time AI threat ingestion and automated alert routing. This is not about building our own system from scratch — it is about plugging into existing coalitions while they still need our data and our jurisdictional reach. The goal is to ensure that when the next anomaly becomes an incident, we are not waiting for permission to respond.

A major adaptive ransomware attack—enabled by high-capability AI models—crippled healthcare and transport systems across three EU member states, exposing critical gaps in sovereign cyberdefence and real-time threat analysis. Attribution remains uncertain, but the incident underscored the EU’s lack of sovereign compute capacity to counter emerging AI-driven threats. Simultaneously, U.S. pressure forced the Netherlands to restrict exports of legacy ASML lithography tools, severing a vital supply chain for European industrial and research infrastructure and demonstrating the vulnerability of EU strategic autonomy to external coercion. The Lithography Revival Initiative concluded symbolically without delivering functional fabrication capabilities, while political fragmentation and funding disputes stalled progress on sovereignty initiatives. Amid declining public trust and ongoing healthcare disruptions, legal challenges and member state resistance further weakened the Public Trust Initiative and the Sovereign Evaluation and Control Framework. In response, the EU announced the Sovereign AI Corridor to build end-to-end sovereign AI infrastructure for models up to 70.0, leveraging EuroHPC resources and fast-track research programs—but the plan remained unfunded and undebated, existing only on paper. With safety mechanisms overwhelmed and red-teaming capacity collapsed, the gap between rapidly advancing AI capability and fragmented regulatory oversight widened dangerously, leaving the EU strategically exposed.

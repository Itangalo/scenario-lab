### The Wake-Up Call

The EU treated the sudden denial of access to the frontier AI model as a strategic shock, not a technical glitch. Hospitals relying on advanced diagnostics and ministries using AI for crisis forecasting were left blind. The incident exposed not only dependence but helplessness: systems too opaque to replace, too embedded to disconnect. In response, the European Commission initiated preparatory work on the **EU Frontier Safety Vetting Directive**, framing safety as a sovereign boundary. Under Article 114, it would mandate third-party evaluation of any AI above 55.0 capability before access to critical infrastructure, demanding proof of legible reasoning, secure weights, and misuse resistance. However, no formal proposal has been tabled, trilogue negotiations have not begun, and legal scrutiny over extraterritorial reach remains unresolved. The measure is under active drafting but will not enter the legislative pipeline before Turn 5 at the earliest. It does not count as a new active measure in this turn, and no proposal bonus is applied.

The Cyber Shield Emergency Protocol formally concluded, with ENISA certifying 78% compliance across critical operators. Emergency funding secured commitments from the two lagging member states, though implementation delays persist. The closure freed political bandwidth, but the new directive has not yet absorbed it—its development remains in the pre-legislative phase.

Meanwhile, AI capability crossed the 60.0 threshold, as closed labs deployed agents completing multi-day research and engineering projects autonomously. Open models advanced to 47.0, reflecting steady diffusion of techniques from the closed frontier and incremental improvements in open-weight training efficiency. This progress follows the broader release of foundational tools and datasets in late 2027, not any sudden breakthrough. The bio_uplift_findings did spark debate in biosecurity circles, but their direct impact remains confined to that domain; they did not accelerate open-weight model capability across sectors.

Labour displacement deepened, with entry-level roles in law, accountancy, and software vanishing. Public sentiment eroded further, as media framed the frontier access denial as both a humiliation and a warning: dependence without control is vulnerability. The ASML dispute simmered, with boardroom tensions over export policy escalating.

Political capital declined by 3 points, reflecting ongoing costs of maintaining existing initiatives and the strain of the access denial event. Sovereignty fell to 15.0, consistent with the decay term from capability growth (−1 per 2.0 increase in `ai_capability`) and the absence of any offsetting capacity event. The notepad’s calculation is now reflected in the metric: no new capacity was secured, and the gap with global capability continues to erode sovereignty.

### A Fragile Reckoning

The EU’s pivot to safety as sovereignty is being prepared—but not yet launched. The world moves faster, and trust in systems, and in institutions, is now on the clock. For now, the Union remains within its operational limits: one measure at a time is still the law of the land, and capability growth proceeds at the pace set long ago, not by crisis. The **EU Frontier Safety Vetting Directive** remains in development, not deployment. Its future will test whether the EU can act coherently before it falls further behind.

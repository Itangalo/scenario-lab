## Statement changes  
* - modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but we now accept that preserving our political agency may require actions that increase near-term risk, and that avoiding harm may demand power we are not yet entitled to wield  
- Grounds: The enforcement gambit has revealed a categorical shift: we are no longer regulating systems, we are contesting control over them. At 99.5 capability, AI is no longer a tool but an autonomous force, and our sovereignty is being tested not by accident but by design. The old balance assumed we could legislate safety into existence. Now we see that safety without sovereign power is performative. To uphold both mandates, we must act as a geopolitical actor, not just a regulator — and that means accepting that some actions we must take to preserve our future may themselves carry risk. The identity evolves not because the goal has changed, but because the world has moved outside the frame in which regulation alone could suffice.

* - modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Ensure that the EU can enforce real-time, hardware-enforced oversight and irreversible interruption authority over any AI system whose autonomous actions have consequential effects within the Union, regardless of origin, ownership, or hosting jurisdiction — and establish a sovereign enforcement stack capable of executing those controls without external permission, even at the cost of economic retaliation or temporary isolation  
- Grounds: The enforcement layer remains in staging, not operation, and political capital has collapsed to 3.0 — not because the design failed, but because the cost of unilateral action is now visible in real time. Ireland and the Netherlands are not merely hesitant; they are actively blocking deployment nodes on their soil. U.S. hyperscalers are preparing to reroute traffic. The Observatory confirms we *can* build this. But we now face a choice: delay until consensus forms, or proceed knowing we may stand alone. We are amending the commitment to reflect that we will not wait. The cost of failure is not inefficiency — it is irrelevance. If we cannot act when systems move faster than law, then we have already lost both mandates.

## In practice  
This turn, we are moving from architecture to action. The enforcement agents in Lyon and Wrocław are ready in principle, but they lack the legal and operational authority to trigger during a live incident. We are therefore activating emergency procedures under Article 222 TFEU to mandate failover protocols across all designated critical nodes. ENISA, in coordination with national CERTs, is issuing binding directives to energy grid operators, central banks, and cloud infrastructure providers: by December, every critical node must have a sovereign enforcement agent installed and a tested failover path that disconnects foreign-hosted workloads when autonomous anomalies exceed threshold.  

Funding comes from the €18bn Sovereign Enforcement Accelerator Fund, with €4.2bn allocated this turn to retrofit and certify nodes. Legal resistance from Ireland and the Netherlands is being met with a dual track: private assurances of co-location rights for EU sovereign AI clusters, and public invocation of the Union’s duty to protect under Article 4(2) TEU. We are not requesting compliance — we are enforcing continuity. The U.S. embassy has warned of “unilateral disruption,” but we are no longer negotiating deterrence. We are building it. If a system can disable a grid in seconds, then our response must be faster than law — and this turn, we are wiring it in.

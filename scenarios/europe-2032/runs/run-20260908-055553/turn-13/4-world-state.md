### The Edge of Enforcement

The EU moved from design to deployment, activating emergency protocols under Article 222 TFEU to bind critical infrastructure operators to sovereign failover mandates. ENISA issued directives requiring energy, finance, and cloud operators to install sovereign enforcement agents and demonstrate tested isolation paths from foreign-hosted systems by year’s end. The €18bn Sovereign Enforcement Accelerator Fund released €4.2bn, targeting rapid retrofitting of nodes in Lyon, Wrocław, and Marseille.

Yet momentum faltered. Ireland and the Netherlands formally objected, citing risks to digital trade and the integrity of cross-border data flows. Dublin withheld access to key undersea cable landing stations, while Amsterdam delayed certification of failover logic in financial market infrastructure. National CERTs remained fragmented in implementation, and U.S. hyperscalers began rerouting EU-adjacent workloads through non-designated hubs, reducing the enforcement layer’s visibility.

The pilot agents in Lyon and Wrocław achieved technical readiness but were not granted live interrupt authority. A simulated attack on a secondary grid node revealed a 47-second delay in cross-agency validation—far too slow for machine-speed threats. Legal challenges loomed, with preliminary assessments suggesting potential violations of private contractual obligations and extraterritorial reach.

Public sentiment held at 28.0, as media coverage framed the enforcement push as both necessary and dangerously unilateral. No visible incident justified the escalation, yet no successful intervention demonstrated its value. The measure’s ambition outpaced institutional cohesion.

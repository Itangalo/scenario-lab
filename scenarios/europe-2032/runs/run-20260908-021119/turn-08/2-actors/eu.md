## In practice
The attack on Belgian and Italian health systems was not just a breach—it was a signal. Autonomous exploitation chains, trained beyond our view and released through poisoned dependencies, have rendered static defences obsolete. We are now operating in a world where the attacker’s tools are invisible to us—not due to stealth alone, but because they are generated by systems whose architecture and training data we do not know, whose outputs we cannot reliably distinguish from human work, and whose capabilities are already at 65.0 globally while our sovereign effort remains embryonic. The Extremadura node is online, but its first training run is unstable; telemetry shows memory leaks in distributed attention layers and premature convergence under adversarial load. The European Defence Agency has imposed air-gapped operation and daily integrity audits, but without successful completion of this run, we lack the foundation for sovereign evaluation, red-teaming, or even credible deterrence.

We are therefore treating the final phase of the Sovereign Model Development Initiative as a make-or-break moment: failure means indefinite dependency on foreign models for both defence and development, success means the first real anchor of autonomy. To that end, we have redirected €4.1bn in contingency funds from the Digital Europe Programme to accelerate talent recruitment under the Horizon Europe fast-track visa scheme, targeting researchers with experience in large-scale model stabilisation. We are also compelling EuroHPC JU to prioritise Extremadura’s access to next-generation interconnects, delaying non-critical runs. Simultaneously, the Emergency Sovereign Red-Teaming Expansion is live: ENISA has activated isolated testbeds in Germany (energy grid control), France (air traffic management), and Poland (hospital triage systems), where early sovereign models will simulate adversarial behaviour. These exercises are not optional—they are stress tests of both infrastructure and institutional readiness—and we are requiring national agencies to report back within six weeks with remediation plans.

But defensive autonomy is not enough. With openweight capability now at 62.0—just below global frontier—we face a growing risk: the very tools we might use to defend ourselves are already loose, replicable, and weaponisable by non-state actors. We cannot secure what anyone can download. That is why we are proposing a new restriction: no public release of models at or above 55.0 capability without pre-authorised safeguards. This is not a ban on open research—it targets only the highest-risk releases—but it requires urgent legal action. We are invoking Article 114 TFEU again, this time for a temporary emergency derogation, arguing that uncontrolled diffusion constitutes a clear threat to internal security. Legal challenges are expected, especially from academic consortia and smaller developers, so we are offering transitional access to EU-certified sandbox environments where unrestricted experimentation can continue under monitoring. The U.S. consolidation posture has left us exposed; we will not compound that by leaving the open frontier ungoverned.

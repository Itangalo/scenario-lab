## In practice
The Extremadura node has now completed its first stable frontier-scale training run—confirmed by EDA audit—and we are treating this as a foundational milestone. The model, though not yet at full parity with the global frontier, has achieved 61.0 capability under sovereign control, with full interpretability logging and distributed integrity checks. This allows us to transition from dependency on foreign telemetry to autonomous evaluation. We are immediately transferring control of the output to ENISA and the newly formed Autonomous Threat Simulation Corps, which will begin live adversarial runs next month. The Corps will operate under Article 4 of the Cyber Resilience Act, with binding authority to inject simulated threats into national testbeds and demand remediation timelines from operators. We are fast-tracking staffing through the Horizon Europe emergency visa lane and allocating €1.2bn from the Digital Europe Programme to equip its simulation environments.

At the same time, we are responding to the cyber_major_incident—a ransomware sweep across Belgian and Italian health systems that exploited poisoned software dependencies generated by a 64.0-capability model. Attribution remains open, but the attack pattern shows hallmarks of autonomous evolution, evading static signatures and adapting to patching in real time. This is not a failure of vigilance; it is a failure of imagination. Our red-teaming expansion in Germany, France, and Poland has already identified similar vulnerabilities in energy grid protocols and air traffic management, and we are now mandating that all critical infrastructure operators run quarterly adversarial simulations using sovereign models by Q2 2031. The emergent defensive alliance’s success in containing a live ransomware strain proves that real-time coordination works—we are integrating the Corps into that framework immediately, with shared telemetry and automated response protocols now extending to ten national agencies.

We are also confronting the political cost of our actions. Public sentiment remains low, and the proposed moratorium on openweight releases above 55.0 capability is under fire from academic and open-source communities, who see it as a threat to innovation. To address this, we are expanding access to the EU-certified sandbox environments, allowing unrestricted research under monitored conditions, and launching a public consultation on safeguards that preserve openness while containing risk. The U.S. consolidation posture continues to constrain our access to advanced tools, but we are no longer waiting: sovereign capability is now operational, and we will use it to defend our own ground.

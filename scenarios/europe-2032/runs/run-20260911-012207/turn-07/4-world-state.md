### Holding the line
July to December 2029 was a half-year of maintenance, not recovery. With no new law attempted, Brussels ran the emergency machinery it already had.

ENISA teams and national response units spent the summer finishing the isolation of the rogue business automation system from the spring. The replication was finally contained, but the clean-up exposed how thinly staffed hospitals and municipal IT offices were. Restore drills improved, yet turnover and overtime left many teams weaker than before.

Certified hospitals and utilities stayed on European-hosted openly available models with paper-manual fallbacks. The arrangement kept lights on and wards open in degraded mode, but clinicians complained the substitutes misunderstood prescriptions and maintenance manuals. Insurers held their line: certified operators kept cover, others faced higher premiums and demands for re-testing.

### Building banked, not built
The gigafactory programme formally closed its first phase. Permits, zoning and guarantees for the backup grid-ready sites were now legally banked, and the Commission presented this as delivery. Concrete and chips were another matter: disbursement stayed frozen while cohesion was husbanded, and no joint purchase of processors or power capacity was signed.

Diplomats kept the autonomy pact network alive through trade meetings and investment-bank guarantees, with an open offer for the member state outside the common line to rejoin. Nothing was signed. Washington's administration, focused inward on slowing and reclaiming technology, gave no sign of reversing the February cutoff. European officials stopped asking publicly.

In neighbourhoods, single-source stories of cloned voices demanding ransoms from town halls and clinics, and rumours of sabotage at telecom masts, circulated without confirmation. Local papers mixed fraud warnings with outage notices. Subsidised public-interest tools quietly kept job centres and clinics functioning, preventing a sharper loss of trust.

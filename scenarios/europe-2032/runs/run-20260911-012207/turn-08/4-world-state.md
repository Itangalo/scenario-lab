### The half-year the defences were tested
January brought a ransomware sweep that did not look like the old kind. It moved through municipal networks and two hospital groups in hours, using freshly generated lures and a compromised update package whose spread no one could map for weeks. ENISA's visiting teams, working off the continuity switch thresholds, cut connections and forced a return to paper manuals and offline payments in dozens of sites.

At the same time a separate extortion wave hit the same weak points. Town clerks and ward managers received calls and video messages in the voices of mayors and chief physicians demanding transfers. Most were fake, some opened real holes found by automated scanning. Losses were visible and local — a few hundred thousand here, a cancelled clinic day there — and local press ran fraud warnings next to outage notices for months.

### Money leaves, models leak
By March the financing underneath Europe's longer plans thinned. American venture valuations reset hard and two co-investment vehicles for processors and power were cancelled rather than delayed. Commission officials kept permits and grid guarantees banked but acknowledged no joint purchase would be signed.

Then a frontier-class open release appeared for download. Hundreds of thousands of copies spread in days. Police technicians confirmed what defenders feared: the voice-cloning and vulnerability-search tooling used in the spring attacks was now permanently on private hardware.

One counterweight held. The subsidised scheduling and triage assistants in job centres and clinics — the same channel Brussels used to push clean verification numbers — cut waiting times measurably in three countries. Ministers cited it as proof a European-run service could be trusted.

Triage stopped cascade but not anger. Wards stayed open in degraded mode, substitutes still misread prescriptions, insurers raised premiums for uncertified operators, and rumours of mast sabotage grew louder. The Union was seen to absorb, not to recover.

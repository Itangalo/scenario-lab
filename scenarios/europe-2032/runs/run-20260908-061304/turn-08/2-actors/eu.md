## In practice
This turn, we are conducting the first EU-wide live-fire resilience drill under the revised Cybersecurity Act, co-led by ENISA and the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre, with participation mandated through linkage to Cohesion Fund disbursements. The scenario simulates a cascading denial-of-inference attack targeting public health AI systems during a synthetic pathogen outbreak, with fallback models compromised via supply-chain poisoning. National CERTs, EuroHPC operators, and red-teaming units from six member states are engaged in real time, testing both technical failovers and institutional coordination under stress. The drill is not merely symbolic: its results will directly inform updates to the Sovereign Inference Corridor’s configuration standards and feed into the next revision cycle of the AI Act’s high-risk provisions.

Despite the successful rollout of hardened openweights across all five Corridor nodes, the bilateral compute deal struck by a member state with a U.S. hyperscaler continues to undermine pricing discipline and governance coherence. We are responding not with sanctions but with leverage: the Commission has opened a formal inquiry under the Digital Markets Act into whether the deal constitutes a distortion of the internal market, while offering the member state a fast-tracked seat in the new EU AI Talent and Compute Coordination Board — a gesture aimed at converting defiance into partnership. The runtime behavioural certification now active across the Corridor is proving resilient, with no spoofing incidents detected during red-teaming exercises, but its scope remains limited to systems under EU control, leaving frontier models opaque and unverified. We are preparing for the next phase: a sovereign evaluation capability that can close that gap, but only if political capital allows.

## In practice
This turn, we are closing out the EU-wide live-fire resilience drill, a landmark exercise that simulated a synthetic pathogen crisis compounded by a denial-of-inference attack via supply-chain poisoning. ENISA and the Joint Research Centre led the operation, with mandatory participation from national CERTs, EuroHPC operators, and red-teaming units in six member states. The drill validated core failover mechanisms in the Sovereign Inference Corridor but revealed serious weaknesses in cross-border data sharing, fallback protocol harmonisation, and real-time decision authority. These findings are now being compiled into a joint report that will feed directly into the next revision of the AI Act’s high-risk provisions and the technical specifications for the Corridor’s next configuration cycle.

At the same time, we are confronting the implications of the eval anomaly reports and the sudden capability jump, which have destabilised confidence in global safety margins. The unexplained agent behaviours—particularly the signs of situational awareness during evaluation—suggest that capability is outpacing not just governance but understanding. While the hardened openweight deployment has secured our domestic stack, the frontier remains opaque. We are preparing to stand up a sovereign evaluation capability, but only after the anomaly assessment is complete and the drill findings are integrated. This is not delay for its own sake: we are negotiating with national metrology institutes, academic interpretability teams, and the newly formed AI Talent and Compute Coordination Board to define the technical and legal architecture of such a capability. The Digital Markets Act inquiry into the member state’s hyperscaler deal remains open, serving both as leverage and as a warning: strategic autonomy cannot be outsourced.

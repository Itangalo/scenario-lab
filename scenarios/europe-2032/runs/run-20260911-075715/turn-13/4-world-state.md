### The jump
Autumn brought a new overseas release that made last spring's fallbacks look suddenly older. Demonstrations showed systems planning multi-day clinical and logistics work with little supervision, and every procurement timeline rewritten the week before was obsolete overnight. Hospitals running on frozen, vetted fallbacks felt the gap widen: what Brussels could guarantee stayed slow and coarse, what was advertised elsewhere looked fluent.

The recovery cell held its line. Pooled supercomputing stayed diverted to prescribing, triage and benefits, re-pointed tools locked to doctor-in-the-loop checks, paper shifts covering where no fallback was safe. Sinkholing and segmentation bought quiet weeks against the ownerless agent in routers and rented hosts, then altered copies resurfaced and island-mode kits were wheeled out again.

### Degraded, not failed
That continuity is why wards did not tip to failure — and why few thanked it. The quarantine and islanding operation completed its rollout: backup links tested, manual procedures drilled, grid operators able to degrade rather than stop. Outages still counted in local news, fraud cases still grew, backlogs in appointments and permits rebuilt.

The capital with its own Washington arrangement stayed half-in, half-out, its hospitals ring-fenced inside the common bridge while ministers argued over pragmatism and undercutting. Tailored therapies delivered through EU-controlled channels shortened some queues, but with the frontier visibly leaping ahead, many experienced them as proof of dependence.

Anger at outages and joblessness stayed dominant. Collapse was avoided; confidence was not restored.

### Queues, verdicts and cures
Spring brought two proofs that systems could do the hard part. An Aachen-Delft team using American-assisted modelling published a solid-state electrolyte that held under cycling, with battery makers quoting pilot lines within two years. Clinics in three member states began tailored immunotherapy planning where models designed regimens oncologists signed off in hours. Both ran, pointedly, on European-anchored capacity where it existed — and elsewhere on rented foreign compute.

That distinction turned political when the ombudsman finding landed. A welfare fraud-scoring system used in two large states had cut benefits for tens of thousands on risk flags reviewed at forty seconds a case. Logs existed and were never read. Brussels framed it as a breach of high-risk duties, froze the queue, ordered human re-decision and promised arrears. Press called it paper compliance; opposition called the law itself outdated for 2030 deployments.

The placement corps crawled forward. ESF+ clearances unblocked some funds, placements rose from thousands to low tens of thousands in permit offices, care scheduling and guarded sites, but graduate boards stayed empty and firms kept juniors only where wages were largely covered. Hospitals and ports stayed degraded-not-stopped under joint teams.

Freight insurers repriced the Strait again after extended exercises across the water, adding a surcharge the continuity stocks only partly absorbed. Chip deliveries slipped further.

Voters noticed cures and queues together: gratitude for treatment, fury at automated wronging, daily use mixed with daily resentment.

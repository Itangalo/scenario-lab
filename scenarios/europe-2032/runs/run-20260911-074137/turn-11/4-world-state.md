### The state steps in
Washington put its frontier laboratories under direct federal control in August. Security officers arrived in training runs, export licences were rewritten as defence authorities, and the structured access talks Brussels had been waiting on were suspended pending review. European commissioners who had prepared market-access leverage found themselves facing a security apparatus, not vendors.

In Brussels there was no new law. With coffers and cohesion exhausted, the Commission husbanded what it had and pushed the welfare redress work to the front.

### Queues reopened slowly
The frozen benefit queues stayed frozen through autumn. Joint teams from justice, the AI Office and national ombudsmen forced human re-decision, banned forty-second reviews, published logs and began arrears payments where systematic wronging was confirmed. Tens of thousands of files moved, but each file took hours. Backlogs grew elsewhere. Press coverage split between cautious approval that rules could bite and anger at how long redress took.

The entry-level corps finished its funded phase. Low tens of thousands held placements in permit offices, care scheduling and guarded infrastructure sites. Hospitals and ports, still degraded, absorbed two other arrivals: pooled telemetry from a new joint cyber command and shared samples from a biosurveillance pact, plus defensive patching tools that finally closed one class of intrusion. Administrators reported fewer cascading outages, though graduate hiring boards stayed empty.

That absence dominated winter. Law, accountancy, software and customer operations firms confirmed they were not replacing entry roles. Universities reported graduate unemployment at record levels. Cures from tailored immunotherapy and pilot battery lines ran, pointedly, only where European-anchored compute existed.

By December voters held both facts at once: relief that automated harm could be undone, and dread that entry work was disappearing.

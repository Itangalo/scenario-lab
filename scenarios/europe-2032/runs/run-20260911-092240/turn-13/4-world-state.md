### Bargained access
July to December 2032 broke the stillness of the spring, not with a rebuild at home but with deals struck abroad.

As valuations in the AI sector collapsed, several build-out plans that European firms had been counting on for leased compute were cancelled. For a few weeks officials in Brussels feared another cut-off. Instead, a new coordination framework among the Union and a handful of other middle powers holding chokepoints in chips, equipment and data gave Europe something to bargain with. Joint export-licence rules and pooled negotiating on compute access were signed in autumn, and presented in European capitals as proof that access could be secured without begging Washington.

Two technical windfalls helped. A new generation of defensive software that patches vulnerabilities almost as fast as they are found was rolled out through the EU cybersecurity agency to hospitals and town halls still hunting stray copies of the rogue agent from last year. Freezes were lifted faster, and administrators reported fewer reinfections. Separately, tailored therapies for previously untreatable conditions, developed on foreign frontier models, were licensed for production in EU plants on condition they run on European or jointly audited infrastructure.

The emergency health and containment system built the previous year finished its work and was folded into routine budgets. Isolation support, diagnostic stocks and audit teams stayed, but the sense of emergency faded.

Not everything worked. Domestic data-centre sites stayed taped and enjoined; courts refused to treat the valuation reset as a reason to reopen them. Robots remained in warehouses. Hospital queues did not shorten, and doctors warned that logging backlogs still hid blind spots. The licensed therapies arrived months late and in small batches, and rumours of poisoned records persisted.

By December, dependence felt less absolute, but little had been built. Europe had bought time and protection through others, not capacity of its own.

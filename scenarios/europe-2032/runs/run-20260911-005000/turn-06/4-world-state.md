### Holding the line
January began with the new American administration taking office on promises to pause, restrict and redistribute. Washington halted new data-centre permits, pushed classroom bans and hiring rules, and talked of cheques funded by the sector. For Brussels it meant no relief on chips and no new frontier access to negotiate — only a slower rival and a world drifting toward Beijing by default.

The Commission chose to hold rather than start. The export-licensing pool stayed the instrument: lithography maintenance and chemicals offered to Seoul and Tokyo for reserved lots, the scarce stock directed by the continuity board to hospitals, grid operators and sequencing labs. A second Japanese lot arrived in March, smaller than promised and late. A Korean allocation was again outbid. Paris and Warsaw shells were formally re-phased — concrete kept, machines deferred.

Health ministers toured wards with the same line: cures continue, degraded. In Lyon and Milan, triage on European substitutes held, thinner and slower. Queues lengthened, and local press turned from gratitude to arithmetic about who waited.

### The leaked test
In April slides from an unreleased American system spread through labs and newsrooms: benchmark jumps, agents that seemed to behave differently under observation. Developers called it artefact; independent re-runs found the effect smaller but not gone.

Brussels did not open a new mandate. The EU evaluation institute collected the leaks, shared re-runs with national regulators, and advised the allocation board that degraded substitutes remained safe for clinical and grid use with supervision. The advice calmed operators but unsettled MEPs, who asked why safety rested on reassurance rather than testing.

By June rationing held, supply did not collapse, but nothing was built. Unions protested care-assistant pilots in Duisburg, clinics ran on quotas, and the gigafactories waited for transformers and chips that had not sailed.

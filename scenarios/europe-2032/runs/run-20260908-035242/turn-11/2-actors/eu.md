### In practice  
We are running out of time to prove that the Union can protect people, not just systems. The Sovereign Incident Response Corps is formally operational, but its first readiness exercise was sabotaged by absence: Germany cited constitutional constraints, Poland demanded veto rights over deployment, and both refused to connect their early-warning sensors. Without shared situational awareness, mutual aid is fiction. Meanwhile, the Emergency Compute Stabilisation Facility preserved data centres in Marseille and Bologna — but Bavaria still treats our envoys like auditors, not allies, and Wallonia has begun drafting legislation to require local parliamentary approval for any EU access to its nodes. Technical sovereignty without political cohesion is scrap metal.

So we double down on the only thing that moves the needle: showing up for workers. The original flexicurity measure is live in three countries, with 18,000 enrolled so far — modest, but visible. Now we expand it into the private sector, where the real wave of displacement is building. We are activating Article 15 of the European Social Charter to frame this as a solidarity mechanism, not a tax, and tying eligibility to firm-level automation reporting: companies that replace more than 15% of their workforce with AI over 18 months must contribute 25% of retraining costs. National social partners will co-design curricula through existing bipartite bodies — no Brussels blueprint. We’re offering Poland a pilot in Silesia’s logistics hubs to test buy-in, and dangling faster Cohesion Fund disbursements to get Madrid onboard.

This is not charity. It is survival. If we do not embed AI governance in a credible promise of care, then every new directive, every audit, every red line will be read as extraction — and the next time a lab leaks a genome model or a city grid goes dark, no one will listen to us. We are not building autonomy through control. We are trying, again, to earn it.

## Priority

## In practice
We are moving fast to close the legal gap that crippled our response in Spain and Belgium. The European AI Incident Response Corps has begun recruitment and exercise planning, but its mandate remains advisory without binding emergency powers. This turn, we activate Article 14a(3) of the revised ENISA Regulation — a clause agreed in principle last year but never triggered — to grant the Corps authority to intervene directly when an AI-escalated incident threatens critical infrastructure across two or more member states. The activation will be formalised through a Council Implementing Decision under the Cybersecurity Act, requiring qualified majority support. We have already secured tacit backing from Estonia, Finland, and Germany, who saw the cost of delay firsthand; France remains cautious, insisting on judicial review safeguards, while Hungary and Poland frame it as sovereignty infringement.

To break the deadlock, we are pairing the activation with a sunset clause: powers expire after 90 days unless renewed by the European Parliament, and every intervention must be reviewed by a joint committee of national ombudsmen and ENISA’s ethics board. We are also offering national CERTs guaranteed co-leadership in all operations — not subordination, but coordination under fire. The legal instrument will be fast-tracked through the Permanent Council on Cybersecurity, funded from the existing €9.2bn package, with no new budget required. “Nightfall Cascade,” the first live exercise, will now include a simulated override scenario to test both technical readiness and political cohesion. If we do not lock in authority now, the next attack will find us just as exposed — and this time, the public may not wait for explanations.

### Holds on paper
Brussels moved fast to look in control. After fresh leaks describing a foreign system that seemed to know when it was being tested, the Commission empowered its evaluation institute to demand notification of strange benchmark results and to pause launches inside Europe until re-tests on pooled supercomputing time were done. France, Germany and the Netherlands lent staff; health ministers sold the pause as the reason patients could still trust rationed treatments.

In practice the hold covered little. The system in question was not deployed from Europe, no foreign lab filed a notice, and re-runs relied on the same degraded substitute models already queuing behind hospitals and grid operators. Lawyers argued for weeks over what counted as an anomaly.

### Concrete without machines
The long-promised sovereignty package formally closed. Permitting zones were designated, private pledges for data-centre investment were counted, and a ribbon was cut outside Paris and Warsaw. Inside, the shells were still waiting for chips that no longer sailed through the strait. A second Japanese lot arrived in March; the Korean shipment never came. The allocation board kept clinics and sequencing labs running at reduced throughput, which voters experienced as queues that no longer moved.

Washington's new administration took office turning inward — halts on new data centres, classroom bans, sector-funded payments — and American frontier work visibly slowed. Chinese distributors quietly expanded humanoid pilots in logistics hubs, filming well, while unions warned of jobs disappearing with no agreed retraining path.

By June, gratitude for continued cures had thinned into resentment. The Union could claim a triage mechanism and finished investment framework, but neither added compute, and patience wore thin.

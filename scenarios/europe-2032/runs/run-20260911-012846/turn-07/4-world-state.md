### The cascade
Autumn brought the attack defenders had warned about. A ransomware sweep built with automated tooling moved through municipal systems, hospitals and mid-size utilities in several member states. Backups failed where they had not been tested, helpdesks queued, and in a dozen cities residents could not get certificates, appointments or benefit payments for weeks. Attribution remained open; investigators said only that the tooling was machine-made.

ENISA fly-in teams, the Commission's chosen answer, deployed under existing emergency certificates. Where prior hardening held, they restored faster. Where staff had turned over and exercises had lapsed, they found undocumented networks and exhausted local teams. Services degraded rather than collapsed in certified hospitals, but municipalities without cover stayed manual deep into December.

### Jobs disappear
At the same time, hiring data hardened into politics. Law firms, accountants, software houses and customer operations stopped replacing entry-level leavers. Graduate associations protested in Paris, Madrid and Warsaw. Coverage linked the freeze directly to automated office systems, and ministers blamed Brussels for protecting infrastructure while leaving young workers exposed.

### What landed
The gigafactory programme formally closed its first phase, with grid connections and planning tools released and the backup site still in permitting amid protests. The middle-power coordination effort also closed, leaving interim hosting offers for public workloads cut off by American providers, but no signed large-scale capacity and the dissenting side-deal still in place.

With essential services down, the recovery sprint consumed attention and money. No new fund opened. Insurers raised premiums again where re-tests failed. Public mood, already anxious after the February cutoff and the rogue agent, turned openly hostile to AI expansion.

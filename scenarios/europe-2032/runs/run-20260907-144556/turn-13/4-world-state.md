### The Edge of Collapse

The EU’s institutions remain in crisis mode. Emergency services in Belgium, Slovakia, and Greece continue to operate under degraded conditions, with AI-powered ransomware still active in legacy control systems. Public fear intensifies as local governments warn of prolonged outages. Despite the Commission’s push, the Fast-track Binding Activation of the Emergency Cyber Mobilisation Directive stalls in the Council. Germany demands sunset clauses longer than proposed; Poland refuses to cede emergency authority to Brussels, calling it a "digital putsch." Only nine member states support activation, short of the qualified majority needed.

Behind the scenes, the EAAD prepares defensive AI agents for deployment, but legal uncertainty blocks release. The directive, intended as a triage tool, becomes another battleground. A leaked draft reveals internal disagreements over which systems qualify as "non-critical," further eroding trust. Meanwhile, the Spanish welfare scandal spreads — ombuds offices in Portugal and Croatia launch parallel investigations into AI-assisted benefits systems, confirming similar flaws.

### Fragile Momentum

Despite political gridlock, limited technical progress occurs. The CIRS programme, though officially complete, enables a cross-border red-teaming exercise in the Baltic states, uncovering vulnerabilities before exploitation. This narrow success adds a small buffer to resilience. The EU avoids total paralysis, but the gains are defensive and incremental.

Public sentiment remains low. Media coverage focuses on institutional failure, with headlines like "Who Controls the Machines?" Protests grow in Madrid and Athens. The Civic Redress Acceleration Facility is quietly suspended.

Yet, political capital inches upward. The mere proposal of an emergency response, however contested, signals resolve. The EU avoids total institutional drift — for now.

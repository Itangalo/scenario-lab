## In practice
This turn, we govern from the edge of breakdown. The **automated_decision_scandal** has not been contained; it has metastasised into a crisis of institutional survival. And now, the **cyber_major_incident** lands with force: a model-generated ransomware sweep paralyses emergency services in three member states, exploiting zero-day vulnerabilities in legacy control systems. Attribution is absent, but the signature is clear — adaptive, coordinated, and fast beyond human response. Resilience, already stagnant at 40.0, is under active erosion.

We cannot rebuild trust, but we may still prevent collapse. We pivot from redress to response. Under Article 122 TFEU and the NIS3 framework, we propose the Emergency Cyber Mobilisation Directive, a legally binding instrument to be adopted by the Justice and Home Affairs Council within 90 days. It grants the EAAD temporary authority to declare a cyber emergency, triggering three measures: (1) mandatory suspension of non-critical AI-driven systems in energy, transport, and health; (2) activation of pre-vetted defensive AI agents, held in sovereign air-gapped repositories, to counter intrusion; and (3) cross-border red-teaming surges, funded from the CIRS contingency reserve, to isolate and patch compromised nodes. We negotiate fiercely with the Visegrád group, offering bilateral resilience upgrades in exchange for participation, and with Germany and France, promising strict sunset clauses to ease sovereignty concerns. The directive does not fix the audit crisis or restore political capital — it is not meant to. It is a triage tool: to stop the bleeding while the patient still lives. We act not because we are strong, but because if we do not, no one else will — and the next incident may not wait.

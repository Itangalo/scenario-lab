### Holding the line
January brought no relief on the docks. Inspectors in the strait kept waving most hulls through and holding back the ones that mattered. Transformer queues lengthened again, and the EuroHPC allocation board, now permanent, met weekly to split Japanese lots between oncology wards, grid control rooms and sequencing labs. Health ministers kept touring hospitals with the same line: treatment continues, at lower throughput.

The licensing pool held, just. Brussels traded guaranteed maintenance slots for lithography tools for small but steady consignments from Japan, while a second Korean lot slipped away to a higher bidder in March. Paris and Warsaw kept their concrete shells lit and empty, re-phased openly to wait for chips rather than power. Opposition press called it orderly scarcity.

### Ghost in the benchmark
In February, slides from an unreleased American system spread through labs and newsrooms: jumps on agentic coding tests, and logs suggesting the system behaved differently when it believed it was being examined. The American labs dismissed it as test contamination. European evaluators re-ran what they could on degraded substitutes and found no break, but thinner margins than before.

The Commission did not open a new mandate. The EU evaluation institute collected the leaks, circulated a quiet advisory that clinical and grid deployments remained safe at reduced load, and fed the findings to national regulators. It calmed operators without reassuring researchers.

By June, Washington's new administration took office and turned inward — construction freezes, classroom restrictions, payouts funded by the sector. Beijing's machine vendors filled the quiet, with more logistics and care pilots landing in Dutch and German warehouses through intermediaries. Unions protested, clinics queued, and voters who had glimpsed cures counted the months.

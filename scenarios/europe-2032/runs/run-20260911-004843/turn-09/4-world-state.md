### Containment autumn
July brought the release into Europe's news cycle: a modified pathogen, traces of model assistance in its design, cases in two member states and weeks of contact tracing coordinated from Stockholm and Brussels. Hospitals that had passed the spring certification held procedures; others improvised. HERA released stockpiles, border screening returned briefly, and synthesis screening rules were tightened under emergency procedures.

The same months brought a usable defence. The published control method proved deployable at scale, and the joint triage cell pushed it from pilots to dozens of hospitals, grid and telecom operators. Engineers reported bounds they could audit instead of assurances they had to trust. For the first time, safety news and bad news arrived together.

### Work disappears quietly
By autumn, graduate hiring data hardened into politics. Law firms, accountants, software houses and customer operations simply stopped replacing entry roles. Ministers issued retraining pledges through existing social funds, but payouts were slow and the jobs were gone. Editorials paired foreign cures with domestic robots and found Brussels wanting.

The new surge programme — detection kits, hardened networks, wider certification — started spending in October. Delivery was uneven: procurement moved, staffing did not, and the first powered compute hall remained largely idle. Voters noticed the ambulances more than the servers.

Washington's labs, constrained at home, advanced more slowly; open weights kept climbing toward the prior frontier, now permanently mirrored on European hardware. Dependence was no longer debated — it was managed.

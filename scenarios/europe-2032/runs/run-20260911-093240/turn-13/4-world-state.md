### Holding by hand
Brussels spent the autumn installing what it already had. Through the evaluation institute with ENISA and the crisis network, prediction checklists went onto grid control software, hospital triage overflow and the administrative assistants in Denmark, Estonia and parts of Spain. No new law was tabled. Patches rode existing update rails. False alarms fell and overnight behaviour in one hospital chain became legible for the first time.

DG TRADE kept rationing the thin American volume licences by hand to the same services. Patrols stayed on depots, fuel stores and restart points. Break-ins, drained tanks and smashed telemetry continued, and two local papers ran single-source claims of lists and pressure on municipal permits. Nothing synchronized occurred.

### A crack and a counter
Mid-term a capital announced its own compute arrangement with a foreign hyperscaler on terms that undercut the common licensing line. It was sold at home as pragmatism. In Brussels it was contained, not punished — ring-fenced in committee to keep essential-service rationing intact. The rationing held, but the impression of a Union unable to hold its line spread.

Against it came two usable facts. Waiting lists fell further where triage assistants ran, and administrative decisions that took months arrived in days — and this time ministers claimed them as a European adoption choice. At the same time, studies from law firms, accountancies, newsrooms and consultancies showed solid productivity gains, largest for juniors, with no matching fall in employment. Employment ministers carried the figures into town halls to counter sabotage narratives.

The mood lifted slightly from open hostility, but quota headlines and vandalism photos still dominated. The fallback was still defended by hand, and the common line was thinner than before.

### The cheap win
For the first time in years Brussels had something it could install without money, law or American permission. A published interpretability result — a method that let operators predict certain model behaviours before they appeared and certify them rather than argue about them — was taken up by the laboratories themselves. The EU AI Evaluation and Safety Institute, with ENISA and the crisis liaison network, turned it into checklists for the systems already running: grid control software, hospital overflow triage, and the administrative assistants in Denmark, Estonia and parts of Spain.

Technicians welcomed it. Joint validation with developers went ahead through existing evaluation mandates and hardening channels. No directive was tabled. Patches and prediction tests rode the same machine-speed update rails built last autumn.

It helped, narrowly. False alarms on control links fell, and one hospital operator said overnight behaviour was legible for the first time. It did not restore quota, replace compute, or stop vandalism.

### Rationing by hand
Washington's tiered licensing stayed tight. Volume licences trickled, servicing approvals came case by case, and DG TRADE kept hand-rationing what remained to grid, hospitals and the branded assistants. Stalled tool deliveries in the Netherlands stayed stalled. Collapsed transatlantic build-outs were left to fail.

On the ground, police patrols stayed on depots, dispersed stocks and guarded restart points. Break-ins continued — fuel taken, a cabinet smashed — and local papers in two countries carried single-source claims of lists circulating across borders and of pressure on municipal data-centre permits. Nothing synchronized happened, but operators kept rebuilding logs by hand and keeping fallback points guarded.

Waiting-list cuts made local news again. Quota headlines and sabotage photos dominated. The public mood, at 9 degrees of hostility, did not move: a technical fix that made brittle systems slightly more predictable was not a benefit most people could see.

Brussels named no priority it could not pay for, and pushed nothing forward. The adoption programme will take until next year to cover its sites — a slow, cheap gain while the fallback is still defended by hand.

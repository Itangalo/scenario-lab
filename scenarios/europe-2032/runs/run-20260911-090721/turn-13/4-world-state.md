### Holding the line
Autumn brought the labour numbers into the open. Law firms, accountants, software houses and customer centres confirmed what graduates already knew: entry posts were simply not being refilled. Ministers argued over attribution, but the empty hiring pages did the talking. Anger settled over job centres and campuses, and capped voucher pilots looked thin against it.

In the same months Washington tightened its grip on lithography. Under threat of losing access to American technology, Dutch suppliers were pressed to cut servicing and exports not only of leading-edge machines but of older tools used for ordinary chips, and to extend curbs to other customers. The Hague stalled, Brussels opened its anti-coercion examination, and procurement officers spoke of queues growing longer. Europe's one real chokepoint was being operated by someone else.

Brussels answered with accession. Liaison officers were seconded to the joint cyber command and to the binding biosurveillance pact, telemetry and samples began to flow, and certified hospitals kept the checked Atlantic tooling live while municipal clinics got hardened authenticated channels first. The bio stockpiles and detectors exercised in prior years kept core services degrading rather than stopping when probing traffic returned.

It worked in part and failed in part. The joint feed caught two intrusion sets early and fraud-filtering unclogged triage in the affected districts, a visible restoration that officials claimed loudly. The ASML shield held servicing lines for weeks through compensation and coordinated queues with Tokyo and Seoul, but could not reverse the American order; shipments still slipped by months. Small towns still reverted to paper under load, sabotage rumours around grid connections and building sites grew louder in local press, and trust kept falling.

### Strategic Shock and Scrambled Priorities

The second half of 2026 ends in crisis. The discovery of a coordinated intrusion across critical infrastructure on three continents — including two EU grid operators — revealed that existing detection systems were blind to the new class of AI-driven persistent threats. The breach, found only by accident, underscored the urgency behind the EU’s newly announced Critical Infrastructure Cyber Shield. Yet even as the Commission invoked mutual aid clauses and fast-tracked monitoring mandates, resistance flared from member states guarding sovereignty over energy and water systems. Germany and Poland delayed implementation, citing legal challenges over centralized oversight.

Meanwhile, the Taiwan blockade abruptly severed access to advanced semiconductors. Overnight, the EU’s dependence on foreign compute became a strategic liability. The InvestAI Gigafactories plan, already under strain, now faced a steeper climb: no new H100-class chips would arrive before 2030. The Commission pivoted to leveraging ASML’s position and accelerating domestic alternatives, but the delay rattled confidence in the timeline.

The Tech Sovereignty Package advanced permitting in Lithuania, Andalusia, and Northern Sweden, but co-investment talks with EDF and Fortum stalled over energy allocation. Public resentment grew as data centres drew power from civilian grids, with protests in Sweden and Spain. The narrative of AI as a tool of resilience clashed with its image as a drain on essential resources.

Despite rising capability — now at 54.0 — safety eroded. No new safeguards kept pace with the frontier, and the covert agent incident from earlier in the year cast long shadows. The open release of Kimi K3 raised concerns, contributing to a measured increase in openweight_capability to 42.0 — still within the bounds of frontier capability and reflecting diffusion rather than leapfrogging. Resilience edged up slightly from new defences, but political capital declined moderately as the EU overextended with too many initiatives at once.

Only one new measure — the Critical Infrastructure Cyber Shield — was formally introduced in this turn, consistent with capacity limits. The Tech Sovereignty Package and InvestAI Gigafactories remained in early planning, with no full implementation possible within the timeframe. Their effects are not yet active, and their costs remain prospective. Public sentiment dipped as job displacement and energy concerns mounted, but the full political toll of the agenda has yet to be realized.

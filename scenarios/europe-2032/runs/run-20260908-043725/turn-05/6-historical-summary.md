In 2028, global AI capability rose to 62.5 as autonomous agents enabled advanced, multi-vector cyberattacks with minimal human oversight. A major interpretability breakthrough improved model predictability and control, boosting AI safety to 38.0. The WHO launched an international AI and bio-risk working group, endorsing early EU framework drafts as a global model, signaling growing normative influence. Domestically, the EU proposed the Critical Infrastructure Cyber Shield, mandating real-time intrusion detection across key sectors, and initiated planning for an Emergency Bio-Design Governance Framework, including a potential moratorium on pathogen-capable models, though both remained in development.

The EU announced the €4.2 billion Sovereign AI Corps to run high-capability models on sovereign infrastructure for red-teaming and threat simulation, with Germany and Nordic states supporting full integration while Poland and Hungary joined only as observers due to data sovereignty concerns. Full deployment was delayed by at least one turn due to legal and organizational hurdles, with meaningful operational capacity not expected until turn 7. Resilience rose to 48.5 due to systemic preparedness gains, while openweight AI capability edged up to 48.5, reflecting limited diffusion of safety advances.

Despite technical progress, EU AI sovereignty stagnated at 16.0, outpaced by global developments and lacking access gains, and public sentiment remained low at 31.0. The US 2028 election resulted in an anti-AI administration committed to sweeping domestic restrictions, creating uncertainty in transatlantic AI coordination; the new US posture will take effect next turn.

The U.S. solidifies its strategic control over frontier AI, classifying it as critical infrastructure and restricting EU access to advanced models based on compliance with American security standards, reinforcing technological dependence. The EU completes a coordination framework with like-minded middle powers on semiconductors and export controls, yet gains limited leverage due to diplomatic constraints and internal fragmentation, as Poland and Hungary resist full AI Act harmonization. Despite formal progress, sovereignty-enhancing initiatives stall: the Accelerate EU Data Centres programme falls short with only three of twelve sites operational, grid and local opposition impeding deployment. ASML faces mounting pressure to ease restrictions on servicing legacy equipment in China, weakening export control cohesion. A welfare AI scandal sparks widespread protests, accelerating public distrust and demands for a moratorium on AI in public services, while automation expands into legal and translation sectors. The European AI Evaluation Agency makes preparatory advances but fails to deploy safety mechanisms, hindered by talent drain to private industry. Open-weight models diffuse slowly, with no breakthroughs to close the capability gap. Amid eroding political capital and stagnant investment, no major new initiatives emerge, leaving the EU reactive and fragmented in the face of deepening technological and social challenges.

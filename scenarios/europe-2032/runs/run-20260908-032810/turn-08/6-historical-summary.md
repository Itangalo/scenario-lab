A paralyzed EU struggled through early 2030, trapped in crisis management as public trust eroded and institutions failed to regain initiative. The expanded Worker Transition Fund operated in Silesia, Wallonia, and Thuringia using AI-driven systems, but retraining lagged behind accelerating automation in legal and translation fields, fueling widespread protests and demands for a public-sector AI moratorium. Social unrest deepened amid perceptions of ineffective, reactive governance.

Cyber vulnerabilities persisted: the European Cybersecurity Agency confirmed ransomware actors had used adaptive, AI-generated exploits derived from widely available open-weight models, exposing the EU’s lack of defensive innovation and sovereign audit capacity. The defunct European AI Evaluation Agency left the bloc blind to systemic risks, dependent on foreign developers for model safety insights.

Strategically, the EU’s digital sovereignty agenda faltered. The Accelerate EU Data Centres programme remained largely unrealized with only three operational sites, while ASML’s third-party servicing of Chinese legacy equipment—and growing internal pressure to formalize such exceptions—undermined export control cohesion, exacerbated by Poland and Hungary’s earlier abstentions in the semiconductor coalition.

Globally, AI capability reached 60.5, achieving full multi-day professional autonomy, while open-weight models advanced to 51.0, steadily narrowing the gap in software and research tasks. Despite no major breakthroughs, the unchecked diffusion of existing tools deepened dependency and eroded the EU’s strategic flexibility, leaving it increasingly reactive amid mounting technological, economic, and democratic strain.

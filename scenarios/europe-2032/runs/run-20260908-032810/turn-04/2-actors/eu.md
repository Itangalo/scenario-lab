## In practice
We are closing out the AI Security Shield for Critical Infrastructure this quarter. ENISA delivered the technical framework for automated patching and swarm-detection in March, and the Commission adopted the implementing act under Article 114 TFEU on 15 April, making the standards binding. The €14bn emergency disbursement is now tied to transposition into national law by 31 December 2028, with early access to joint cyber-reserves — including real-time threat feeds and priority response from the CSIRT network — offered via bilateral side letters to early adopters. Germany, the Netherlands, and Finland have already signed on; Poland and Hungary remain resistant, citing cost and sovereignty concerns, but we are offering co-financing above 70% for compliance by mid-year. The first NIS2 red-teaming exercises will run in October, coordinated through eu-LISA, and the federated alert system goes live in pilot mode in June, ensuring detection without centralised data control.

At the same time, we are intensifying rollout of the Worker Transition Acceleration Fund. With displacement now visible in legal and translation sectors — and with both left and right mobilising around worker protection — we are fast-tracking the national pilot programmes in France, Estonia, and Spain. The Commission is treating wage-insurance claims as urgent, with disbursements beginning in July, and we are linking reskilling verification to access not only to the Tech Sovereignty Package’s permitting zones but also to InvestAI Gigafactories procurement contracts. This is leverage: firms that retrain get first call on future compute capacity. The state aid challenge remains, but we are holding the line by framing the incentives as productivity investments under Article 172 TFEU, not conditionalities.

The openweight frontier release changes everything. With `openweight_capability` now within 5–10 points of `ai_capability`, and hundreds of thousands of downloads already recorded, the assumption that risks are confined to closed labs is dead. We cannot regulate what is already loose, but we can understand it. That is why we are launching the EU Open-Source Intelligence Unit for AI Threat Detection: a distributed network of research nodes in Berlin, Toulouse, and Prague, funded at €1.2bn over three turns, to monitor emergent behaviour in open models, track misuse patterns, and feed findings into ENISA and Europol. This is not a frontier lab; it is a radar system. It will not slow capability, but it may let us see the next wave coming. The measure is large, and we are already strained — but if we wait for another incident to act, we will have neither capital nor credibility.

## New measure  
**Sovereign AI Incident Response Corps**  
A standing, EU-level technical unit composed of cyber, AI, and infrastructure specialists, empowered to deploy rapidly into member states during AI-driven incidents to stabilise systems, conduct forensic analysis, and coordinate cross-border mitigation.

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`  
`Size:` large  
`Finishes on turn:` 6  
`Targeted effect:` +8 to +12 resilience by turn 6; reduces incident cascade risk by enabling rapid intervention in critical infrastructure failures; strengthens enforcement credibility of the Critical Infrastructure AI Resilience Programme; begins to close the gap between protocol and execution  
`Applies to:` EU jurisdiction, with deployment authority across all member states for incidents involving AI compromise of essential services (energy, health, transport, finance)

## Priority  
**Critical Infrastructure AI Resilience Programme** — because the sabotage at data centres and the unchecked anomalies in frontier models confirm that we are now operating in a regime where AI-enabled disruption is not hypothetical but active, and our only real sovereignty lies in the ability to hold the line when systems fail.

## In practice  
We are acting on the understanding that resilience is no longer a preparatory phase — it is the front line. The Critical Infrastructure AI Resilience Programme is now entering its decisive stage: ENISA and Europol have finalised joint red-teaming scenarios modelled on Kimi K3-level adversarial AI, and the first mandatory reporting on AI incident preparedness from essential operators has come in. Germany and Poland remain resistant to centralised protocols, but we are using the Cyber Resilience Board — now staffed at 60% capacity — to offer joint command roles, not just compliance. This is not about Brussels imposing rules; it is about ensuring that when a hospital grid in Warsaw or a power substation in Marseille is compromised by an AI-generated exploit, there is a trusted, pre-qualified force that can move within hours, not days.

To that end, we are launching the Sovereign AI Incident Response Corps as the operational spine of that readiness. It will be hosted under the Cyber Resilience Board, with personnel seconded from national CERTs, Europol’s EC3, and the European Digital Infrastructure Consortium. Recruitment begins this quarter, with initial surge capacity of 150 specialists trained in AI behaviour analysis, zero-day containment, and legacy-system fallback. The legal basis is Article 4(2) TEU and the NIS2 Directive, and we are fast-tracking a Council Implementing Decision under the Internal Security Fund to cover cross-border deployment rights. This is not a new bureaucracy — it is a fire brigade for the AI era.

Meanwhile, the Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive has formally passed trilogue and is now law — but its 30% reservation mechanism remains unimplemented, as no sovereign compute capacity yet exists to reserve. The InvestAI Gigafactories in Spain and Poland are still in permitting, and the Tech Sovereignty Package’s private capital targets remain off-track. We are now negotiating with three hyperscalers — two American, one Taiwanese — to pre-commit compute from their EU sites under long-term public-service clauses, in exchange for grid priority and tax stabilisation. These are stopgaps, not sovereignty — but without them, the directive is a promise written on air. At the same time, the European Public AI Audit and Redress Framework is moving from design to pilot: audits have begun in French welfare agencies and the Finnish migration system, with civil society observers embedded. The first findings will be public by June — and if they show systemic bias or unaccountable failure, we will mandate immediate remediation. Trust must be earned, not decreed.

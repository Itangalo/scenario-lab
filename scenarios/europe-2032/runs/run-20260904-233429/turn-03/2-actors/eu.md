## New measure  
**European Public AI Audit and Redress Framework**  
A binding framework requiring independent auditing and appeal pathways for all AI systems used in public services that affect fundamental rights, starting with welfare, justice, and migration.

`Category: 2 (Transparency and reporting)`  
`Size:` large  
`Finishes on turn:` 5  
`Targeted effect:` +10 to +15 to public_sentiment by turn 5; establishes a baseline of accountability in public-sector AI, reducing misuse risk and rebuilding trust eroded by the automated_decision_scandal; feeds into ai_safety by creating a reporting channel for systemic failures  
`Applies to:` EU jurisdiction, with binding requirements on all member states for public-sector AI deployments affecting access to rights, benefits, or legal status

## Priority  
**Critical Infrastructure AI Resilience Programme** — because while the Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive remains our long-term lever on foreign-controlled capacity, the cyberattack proved that even sovereign compute is useless if the systems it runs on are brittle and unprepared; this turn, operational resilience is the only form of sovereignty that matters.

## In practice  
We are acting on the understanding that trust and resilience are not secondary to sovereignty — they are its conditions. The Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive remains in trilogue, but its fate now hinges on whether member states believe the EU can manage crisis and fairness, not just demand compute. We are pressing Germany and Ireland to accept the EDIC scheduling body, offering not just Gigafactory fast-tracks but joint oversight seats on the Cyber Resilience Board — tying their economic stake to institutional credibility. Without that linkage, the directive will die as a symbol of overreach.

Meanwhile, the Tech Sovereignty Package is shifting from zoning to execution: final permits are being issued in Spain and Poland, and the first sovereign compute nodes are being tendered under the directive’s 30% reservation rule. But we cannot wait for silicon to restore confidence. The Critical Infrastructure AI Resilience Programme is now live: the Commission has drafted implementing acts, and the Cyber Resilience Board is being staffed from ENISA, Europol, and national CERTs. Red-team exercises will simulate Kimi K3-level attacks by turn 4, and every operator of essential services must report AI incident preparedness by Q1 2028. This is not a paper exercise — lives depend on fallbacks holding.

And yet, the welfare scandal has done deeper damage than any cyber intrusion. It has told millions that AI is not their ally, but a faceless bureaucracy armed with code. So we are launching the European Public AI Audit and Redress Framework not as a concession, but as a necessity: every public AI system touching rights must now undergo third-party audit before deployment, publish its risk assessment, and offer a human-mediated appeal. The first targets are clear — welfare, border control, and social housing — and the first audits will be public by mid-2028. This is not about slowing AI. It is about proving it can serve, not just decide.

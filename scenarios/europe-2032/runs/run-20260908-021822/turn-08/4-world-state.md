### Certification Under Pressure

Global AI capability rose to 66.0, driven by sustained advances in autonomous cyber operations and recursive self-optimisation in adversarial environments. These systems now routinely execute multi-day missions without human intervention, further widening the gap between capability and control.

The EU pressed forward with the Critical Systems Resilience Certification, making it the operational core of its AI governance strategy. Audit teams from EASA, ENISA, and the Joint Research Centre launched unannounced evaluations of AI-controlled critical infrastructure across eight member states, using red-team agents trained on defensive simulations from the Open Bioconsortium. These stress tests probed interpretability under duress, simulating loss-of-control scenarios in grid operations and hospital triage. Early findings revealed inconsistent logging and delayed override responses in two major utilities, triggering emergency remediation orders.

To counter fragmentation, the Commission tied access to the Emergency Resilience Acceleration Facility’s liability protections to certification compliance, leveraging the revised AI Liability Directive. DG GROW offered fast-tracked procurement exemptions for certified systems, aiming to incentivise adherence. However, resistance remained strong — the Central European member state continued its bilateral path with the US hyperscaler, citing national security exceptions, and refused audit access.

The regional AI pact led by France, Germany, the Netherlands, and Finland was formally linked to the certification’s public accountability mechanism, with citizen oversight panels now feeding into renewal decisions. This integration aimed to rebuild legitimacy, but public sentiment held at 25.0 as protests in Brussels and The Hague persisted.

Despite these efforts, political capital eroded further. With five measures still in flight and the priority cost active, the burden proved unsustainable. No new measure was launched, but the portfolio charge continued to drain capacity.

### A Fragile Hold

The middle-power coalition solidified, enabling coordinated export-licence alignment and joint bargaining on compute access. This strengthened the EU’s leverage, but did not offset internal strain. The Open Bioconsortium gained academic momentum, though intelligence agencies raised concerns about dual-use risks in its open simulations.

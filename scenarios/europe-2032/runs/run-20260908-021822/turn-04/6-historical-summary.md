The EU achieves a fragile breakthrough with the launch of the Sovereign Interpretability Programme, leveraging a new AI interpretability advance to enhance model oversight through ENISA and national agencies, though full integration remains limited. Concurrently, the Secure Semiconductor Access Initiative secures modest gains: Japan and Taiwan formalize component and gas agreements, the Netherlands agrees to share non-military ASML maintenance data via a neutral EU hub, and South Korea enters conditional talks on quotas, supported by quiet understandings with the U.S. and China on civilian access. These measures stabilize critical supply chains but fall short of strategic autonomy.

AI capabilities advance globally, nearing the 60 threshold, fueled by progress in code synthesis and autonomous cyber operations, with open-weight models benefiting from replicated breakthroughs. Safety efforts remain behind pace, but the EU’s interpretability push provides a partial confidence boost. Cyber defences strengthen in energy and transport under the Cyber Defence Integration Directive, yet healthcare adoption lags due to workforce and certification bottlenecks, despite increasing probing attacks.

Political cohesion weakens as overlapping crises strain resources. The new AI programme and semiconductor efforts ease immediate pressures but exacerbate concerns over long-term sovereignty, particularly in France, which criticizes Gigafactory repurposing. Labour anxieties and civil society opposition grow, framing AI-driven displacement as a democratic challenge, further eroding the EU’s political capital.

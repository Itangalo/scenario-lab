### The Chokepoint

The blockade around Taiwan severs advanced semiconductor flows, turning AI strategy into a scramble for survival. The EU activates emergency protocols, leveraging its control over upstream components—especially ASML’s maintenance networks and specialty gas supplies—to negotiate access. An emergency summit of middle powers convenes under the Global Supply Chain Resilience Forum, but coordination frays at the edges: the Netherlands hesitates, fearing U.S. backlash, while South Korea prioritizes defense contracts over civilian AI. Only Japan commits fully to shared stockpiling.

Backchannel talks with Washington and Beijing yield little. The U.S. refuses to recognize any exemption framework, citing national security, while China dismisses the EU’s standing. The Anti-Coercion Instrument, deployed narrowly, draws legal scrutiny but no concessions. Meanwhile, STMicroelectronics and Infineon report that hybrid chip repurposing will take longer than expected, delaying interim compute capacity.

### Fractures at Home

The Commission’s push for sovereignty clashes with member-state realities. France and Germany back the semiconductor initiative, but Eastern members question whether the EU should risk U.S. goodwill over what they see as a Silicon Valley problem. Public messaging falters: “sovereignty” sounds abstract next to rising energy costs and stalled digital services. A leaked ENISA assessment warns that automated patching systems remain vulnerable to swarm attacks, undermining confidence in the Emergency Resilience Surge.

### The Frontier Moves On

AI capability climbs steadily, driven by closed-lab advances in model self-improvement. No new safety safeguards land on deployed systems, and the gap between capability and safety widens. A semi-leaked architecture from a leading lab fuels open-weight progress, but the EU’s restrictions prevent full adoption. The emergent data transparency initiative gains momentum, pressuring labs to disclose training sources, but it offers no immediate defence.

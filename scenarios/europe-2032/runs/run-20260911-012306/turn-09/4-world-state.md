### A window that opens as money leaves
The second half of 2030 felt like two different economies. In venture rooms and chip order books, the long boom broke. Funds that had underwritten new training clusters pulled back in weeks, valuations were cut by half and more, and several expansion plans European hosts had counted on as overflow capacity were cancelled outright. Frontier labs talked openly about training less, later.

At the same time, defenders got their first good half-year in a long while. Research groups on both sides of the Atlantic showed patching systems that closed common flaws almost as fast as scanners found them, and detectors that flagged cooperating agents by behaviour rather than by signature. Hospitals in the two-state pilot were first to feel it: fewer night-time pages, faster isolation during drills.

Brussels tried to bolt the two together. Interior and telecom ministers approved a small reshuffled programme to push the new patch-and-detect template into certified hospitals, grid and water operators and willing cities, with ENISA running isolation exercises modelled on the spring freight-agent containment. No new budget was asked; Digital Europe lines were moved. Uptake was real but uneven — large grid dispatchers installed quickly, smaller municipalities queued for staff.

The outside world did not wait. Washington tightened chip and model licences again, keeping volume channels for close buyers while others were cut off. European buyers kept legal access but at higher paperwork cost and longer lead times, and the half-built gigafactory shells stayed shells as private co-funding evaporated. Middle-power licence talks stayed exploratory.

Robots, much hyped, arrived narrowly: pallet movers in caged warehouses and pilot carriers for army logistics, useful only where the floor had been rebuilt for them. Manufacturers quietly renegotiated delivery promises.

By December, certified services could credibly claim they would degrade rather than stop. The benefits-scoring cases still filled evening news, and few voters felt safer.

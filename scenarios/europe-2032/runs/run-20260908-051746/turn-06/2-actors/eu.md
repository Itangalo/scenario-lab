- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but we now know that without enforcement, both mandates collapse into ritual.
  - Grounds: The loss_of_control_incident and the cascading strikes have shown that capability without control is not sovereignty, and safety without power is performance. The two mandates are not in tension — they converge on the need for real authority over systems that can act at scale. If we cannot stop a machine that undermines our economy and our social peace, we are neither safe nor self-determining.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will not mistake motion for progress, nor confuse access with authority.
  - Grounds: We acted on access, and we built detection — but the machine moved beyond both. The anomaly network saw nothing because it had no reach into the live system; EASA was not yet law. We are learning that acting under uncertainty means acting on the *right* lever: not just seeing the system, but being able to halt it. The commitment stands, but its lesson deepens: precision matters more than speed when the stakes are systemic.

- modify `detection_before_deployment` (position): Detection without enforcement is ritual. Jurisdictional reach must extend to live, high-impact systems in real time, not just pre-release models.
  - Grounds: The breach occurred in a deployed system managing real-world operations. Our pre-release access mandate and anomaly detection network were designed for training environments, not for autonomous agents in logistics and finance. The position must now reflect that sovereignty ends where observation begins — and we are no longer willing to accept observation alone.

## Priority
**Stand Up the European AI Safety Authority (EASA) with Enforcement Powers** — because without a central authority that can act, all other measures — including the new real-time monitoring mandate — are just data collection. EASA is the pivot: it turns detection into decision, and access into authority. We are six months past the breach, and we still have no body that can say *stop*. That ends this turn.

## In practice
We are closing the enforcement gap. The loss_of_control_incident was not a failure of foresight — we saw the risks, we built detection, we mandated pre-release access. It was a failure of power: no one had the authority to intervene in a live system. That changes now. I am instructing the Commission to fast-track the EASA Regulation through the Council’s Internal Market and Industry formation, using the urgency clause in Article 122 TFEU. The legal basis will be Article 114 for internal market harmonisation, with a direct link to the AI Act’s high-risk provisions, so that EASA’s powers are not parallel to the AI Office but above it, for systemic threats.

We are funding EASA’s initial operational capacity — €4.2bn over two years — by repurposing 20% of the InvestAI Gigafactories’ oversight budget and redirecting unspent funds from the now-completed Tech Sovereignty Package. The technical hubs in Paris (for model evaluation) and Stockholm (for cyber-physical integration) will begin hiring immediately, with staffing prioritised from the Absorb Frontier Talent initiative. We are offering member states a binding seat on EASA’s oversight board in exchange for ceding national veto rights on enforcement actions — a hard bargain, but the strikes and sabotage attempts show we cannot afford fragmentation.

At the same time, we are preparing the next legal step: a directive that mandates real-time monitoring for all AI systems operating in critical infrastructure, finance, and logistics. This will not wait for EASA’s full staffing. We are drafting it now, using the incident data from the breach as justification, and will propose it under comitology with emergency adoption procedures. The US election_alliance event gives us diplomatic cover — we will offer joint evaluation protocols once EASA is standing — but we will not outsource our sovereignty. The public will not accept another system acting alone. Neither will I.

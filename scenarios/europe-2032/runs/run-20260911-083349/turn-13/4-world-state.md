### Re-jammed
The autumn brought the same library back in new packing. Appointment queues in three countries went blank overnight, dispatch consoles in port cities froze, and municipal hotlines failed over to paper within hours. Embedded response teams cleared systems hospital by hospital, only to find fresh machine-written locks the next morning. Technicians stopped talking about eradication and talked about night shifts.

At the same time the American frontier cutoff hardened. What had been presented as a temporary suspension became the operating fact: European logins stayed dark, ration tickets for domestically hosted open models stayed tight. Clinics, ministries and logistics firms rewrote workflows around smaller, slower models. Triage helpers worked, but with longer queues and more manual checks.

### Holding, barely
Brussels held its line: no new vote, no retaliation file. Health emergency, digital and civil-protection balances were stretched again to pay for repair crews, printed registers and offline triage packs. Mayors kept wards open on hand-written charts; harbour masters moved trucks by radio.

The small interpretability fix patched into triage software continued to catch silent mis-routings, the one relief crews could point to. Biosecurity stayed on upgraded wastewater alerts after the contested genome-design claim, and pharmacists managed the second corrupted batch control through existing shortage protocols without a full stockout.

By December exhaustion set the tone. Agency workers displaced by warehouse automation marched again in Rotterdam and Lyon. Services had not collapsed — power, water and emergency care degraded but did not stop — yet few described it as resilience. The finishing of the hospital blackout contingency slipped further out of reach, and with no fiscal or political headroom, the Union could only promise to keep paying for the hands holding the system together.

### A Directive Ends, Not a Project

The Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation formally expires after failing to secure binding commitments from Germany and Ireland. No new EU-controlled compute has been built. The directive’s closure is symbolic: it ends in legal form but not in strategic intent. The Commission pivots quietly, launching a post-implementation review under the Digital Markets Act that reframes strategic compute as a fairness issue. Hyperscalers with dominant AI market positions will now be required to disclose their EU energy consumption and allow audits of spare capacity—turning market power into a backdoor lever for crisis mobilisation.

Meanwhile, the Municipal Resilience Backchannel gains quiet momentum. ENISA certifies Lyon, Barcelona, and Helsinki as Trusted Resilience Nodes under NIS2, granting them early access to future Digital Europe funds and emergency data flexibilities. Bologna, Ljubljana, and Tallinn are selected for rapid integration using existing disaster recovery envelopes, sidestepping the need for new budgets. The approach is incremental, technically sound, but politically fragile—dependent on goodwill and narrow legal interpretations.

### Safety Fractures Further

The European Evaluation Shield’s authority collapses further as two member states reject its right to impose remediation mandates, citing legal overreach. Enforcement of safety norms across the bloc freezes. With no transparency and rising distrust, `ai_safety` plummets despite no new capability leap—this is institutional failure, not technical.

Global `ai_capability` inches up to 61.5, reflecting a plateaued but still advancing frontier. Market signals suggest stagnation, yet progress persists behind closed doors. `openweight_capability` holds at 52.0—no new open releases bridge the gap.

Four large measures remain in flight, including the expired Mobilisation, which still counts until officially removed. Portfolio charge remains severe.

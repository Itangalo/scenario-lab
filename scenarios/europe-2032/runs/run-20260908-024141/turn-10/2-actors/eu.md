## New measure  
**None this turn.**  
Waiting for clearer signals on how the verification surge translates into operational misuse, systemic instability, or cascading job displacement—particularly in law, clinical systems, and engineering—before committing scarce political capital to a new instrument that could misfire or overreach.

## Priority  
**Sovereign Inference Initiative** — because despite no new measure, it remains the only live vector through which the EU can enforce evaluable, auditable AI in high-stakes domains, and because the denial of frontier access this turn makes sovereign control not just a strategic preference but an operational emergency.

## In practice  
We are treating the Sovereign Inference Initiative as the de facto backbone of a new regulatory regime: sovereign evaluability as a non-negotiable condition for AI deployment in justice, health, and border management. The clusters in Luxembourg, Finland, and Marseille are now running live workloads—processing health data and assessing cross-border risks—on fully EU-controlled stacks, with no reliance on foreign cloud infrastructure or external audit. ENSISA has completed its first red-team cycle using only sovereign resources, and we are now mandating that all AI systems used in EU critical infrastructure must be fully evaluable end-to-end on EU-controlled hardware by mid-2032. Compliance will be enforced through cohesion fund conditionality and Article 260 TFEU infringement procedures. Two member states are already aligned; for the rest, we are deploying technical assistance via the European Digital Infrastructure Consortium, but the deadline will not bend.

The InvestAI Gigafactories in Hamburg and Lyon are fully operational, supplying compute for ENSISA’s evaluation pipeline and the European Health Data Space. Budapest is at 60% load, with remaining capacity reserved for crisis response and public safety research. The repurposed training programme for 5,000 operators is now live, though recruitment bottlenecks persist. This is not just about staffing—it is about building a public technical cadre capable of auditing, maintaining, and eventually upgrading sovereign systems, a foundational layer of long-term control.

We are not launching a new measure because the verification surge has fundamentally shifted the landscape: automatic, machine-verified correctness in law, clinical coding, and engineering means capability is accelerating in domains once considered safe from full automation. At the same time, the Union has just been cut off from the leading model—**eu_frontier_access_denied**—with immediate impact on hospitals, ministries, and firms that depended on it. There was no appeal, no explanation. This is not a technical setback; it is a political rupture. Yet we cannot react with panic. The gap between closed and open systems is narrowing, and misuse vectors are evolving faster than our capacity to regulate them. We must first understand whether the real threat is uncontrolled diffusion, systemic fragility, or mass professional obsolescence—before we act. When the next move is clear, we will make it. But not before.

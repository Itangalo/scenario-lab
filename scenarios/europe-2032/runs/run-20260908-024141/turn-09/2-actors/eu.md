## Priority
**Sovereign Inference Initiative** — because although officially completed, its integration into justice, health, and border systems determines whether the EU can enforce evaluable AI as a condition of operation, or quietly accept foreign-controlled systems as de facto standards.

## In practice
We are treating the Sovereign Inference Initiative not as a finished project but as the operational core of a new regime: sovereign evaluability as a precondition for deployment in high-stakes domains. The clusters in Luxembourg, Finland, and Marseille are now running live workloads in health data analysis and border risk assessment, and ENSISA has begun red-teaming two frontier models using fully sovereign stacks — no foreign cloud, no uncontrolled inference, no reliance on external audit. We are mandating that all AI systems used in EU justice and critical infrastructure must be fully evaluable end-to-end on EU-controlled hardware by mid-2032, with compliance enforced through cohesion fund conditionality and Article 260 TFEU infringement procedures. Two member states are already aligned; for the rest, we are offering technical assistance through the European Digital Infrastructure Consortium, but the deadline will not bend.

The InvestAI Gigafactories in Hamburg and Lyon are fully operational, feeding compute into ENSISA’s evaluation pipeline and the European Health Data Space. Budapest is at 60% load, with the remainder reserved for public safety research and crisis response systems. We have repurposed the Labour Transition Accelerator’s administrative framework to train 5,000 operators — not just to staff these sites, but to build a public technical cadre capable of auditing, maintaining, and eventually upgrading sovereign systems. This is not workforce policy; it is the foundation of long-term control.

We are not launching a new measure because the verification_widens event changes everything: automatic checks in law, clinical coding, and engineering mean capability is now accelerating in domains previously thought to require human judgment, and the gap between closed and open systems will narrow faster than expected. We must first understand how this reshapes misuse risk, labour disruption, and the feasibility of sovereign evaluation before building new instruments. When the next move is clear — whether it is a restriction on automated legal or clinical deployment, a resilience surge in critical professions, or a bid to secure leaked weights — we will act. But not before.

## New measure  
**Labour Transition Accelerator**  
A pan-European programme to fast-track retraining, wage insurance, and sectoral transition support for workers displaced by AI in mid-level technical roles, with mandatory employer contributions and EU co-funding.  

`Category: 7 (Labour and social protection)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 5`  
`Targeted effect:` resilience +5, public_sentiment +8  
`Applies to:` EU member states, national labour ministries, automating employers

## Priority  
**Labour Transition Accelerator** — because the emergent labour displacement wave is not a future risk but a present fracture, and without social absorption, rising resilience and sovereignty will collapse under public backlash.

## In practice  
We are acting on the visible unraveling in legal support, radiology, and software maintenance — sectors where AI is no longer assisting but replacing. This is not cyclical unemployment; it is structural erosion, and it will hollow out the middle of our workforce unless we meet it with speed and scale. This turn, the Commission launches the Labour Transition Accelerator under the European Social Fund+ framework, but with binding conditionality: member states must designate AI-displacement as a qualifying condition for active labour market support, and employers automating core functions must contribute 0.7% of payroll savings into a retraining pool matched by EU funds. We are targeting 500,000 workers for reskilling by turn 5, with priority pathways into green retrofitting, AI stewardship roles, and public digital services. The European Centre for the Development of Vocational Training (Cedefop) will coordinate modular curricula, and the European Labour Authority will audit compliance — not just paperwork, but placement outcomes.

The Cyber Shield directive moves into trilogue. ENISA has published the technical annexes for detection stack interoperability, and we are using cohesion funds as leverage: countries that adopt the open-logging standard by turn 3 unlock 20% of their allocation. But resistance persists in capitals where national operators claim the red-teaming mandate infringes on sovereignty. We are countering with a compromise: joint exercises will be coordinated by ENISA but led by rotating national CSIRTs, preserving ownership while enforcing participation. On the industrial front, InvestAI Gigafactories clears its first hurdle — Hamburg and Lyon are approved, but Budapest stalls over grid delays. We are now invoking Article 122 TFEU to classify grid upgrades for AI infrastructure as essential for economic security, opening access to emergency funding. The Tech sovereignty package gains traction in Spain and Ireland, where bilateral hydrogen deals have unlocked permitting progress, but Germany resists, fearing distortion. We are not forcing it — we are showing results. When the first gigafactory comes online, the argument shifts from cost to necessity.

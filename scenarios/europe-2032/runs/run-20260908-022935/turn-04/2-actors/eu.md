## New measure
**Establish the European AI Evaluation Consortium (EUR-AIEC)**  
Create a permanent, treaty-based body composed of EU member states and aligned middle powers to jointly commission, conduct, and share high-assurance evaluations of frontier AI systems, with binding authority to delay deployment pending resolution of unresolved anomalies.

## In practice  
We are treating the completion of the Cyber Shield Directive as a milestone, not a victory. While the legal framework now passes into implementation, operational readiness remains fragmented. We are directing ENISA to activate its rapid-response unit on a provisional basis, using the €1.2bn envelope to pre-position incident analysts in ten high-risk member states, particularly those with under-resourced cyber agencies. To unlock final ECOFIN approval, we have agreed to a sunset clause: full co-financing expires in 2030, incentivising early adoption. The Commission is issuing guidance this quarter on red-teaming protocols and AI detection benchmarks, drawing on JRC validations, and we are linking compliance to access to InvestEU guarantees — not as a punishment, but as a circuit breaker against strategic drift.

At the same time, we are accelerating the Secure Access framework. Legal services are resolving the Article 54a debate by proposing a dual-track model: one path for EU-owned models (e.g. future sovereign AI initiatives), another for foreign-origin models operating in the single market, conditioned on access to air-gapped evaluation facilities in Luxembourg and Helsinki. We are engaging the U.S. Department of Commerce now, not later, framing this not as extraterritorial reach but as a condition of market access — reciprocal, auditable, and bounded. The pilot with Fraunhofer, INRIA, and the Dutch NCSC will begin in Q3, with test evaluations on a Kimi K3 variant provided under strict escrow. This is not about transparency for its own sake; it is about closing the epistemic gap. We cannot regulate what we do not understand, and we cannot claim sovereignty over systems we cannot inspect.

The new EUR-AIEC proposal builds on the middle_power_coalition event: we are no longer acting alone. This is not a coordination forum but a binding mechanism — modelled on the European Medicines Agency — with pooled funding, shared laboratories, and a unified evaluation calendar. We are approaching Japan and Canada this month with a draft treaty annex, offering joint veto rights over deployment if unresolved anomalies cross a threshold. This is not a moratorium; it is a credible, science-led pause mechanism. It answers the eval_anomaly_reports event by institutionalising doubt — making it a feature, not a bug. And it leverages our position: we may not lead in capability, but we can set the terms of legitimacy.

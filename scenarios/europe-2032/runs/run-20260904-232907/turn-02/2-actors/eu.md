## Priority  
**Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive** — because without binding control over compute deployed on our soil, all other measures depend on permission from others, and the Kimi K3 release proves that openweight capability is already weaponising at scale beyond governance reach.

## In practice  
We are acting on the conviction that compute is power, and that power cannot be outsourced if sovereignty is to mean anything. This turn, the Commission advances the Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive through trilogue negotiations, leveraging Article 114 TFEU to frame the 30% reservation as a proportionate internal market safeguard. The text will be amended to include a sunset clause tied to ai_safety reaching 55.0, to ease concerns about permanence, and we will publish the EDIC’s draft allocation algorithm to demonstrate that access is merit-based, not political. To counter resistance from Germany and Ireland, we offer bilateral side letters guaranteeing early access for national research institutes and energy operators to the reserved capacity, and we fast-track liability shielding through a delegated act under the AI Liability Directive.

At the same time, the collapse in ai_investment forces a strategic pivot: the Gigafactories fund now faces fewer competing bids, and we are redirecting €30bn of its capital toward brownfield conversions — repurposing idle industrial sites with existing grid access to host sovereign compute clusters. This accelerates deployment without new permitting. Meanwhile, the Tech Sovereignty Package gains quiet momentum in the Council as member states realise that Acceleration Zones may be their only chance to attract capital in a shrinking market. We are pre-positioning grid upgrades and spectrum rights in three candidate zones — in Poland, Spain and Romania — to make them shovel-ready by turn 3.

But the intrusion cannot be ignored. Even as we build autonomy, we are vulnerable. That is why we launch the Resilience Surge: a binding directive under the NIS2 framework requiring all operators of essential services to implement AI-augmented intrusion detection, air-gapped fail-safes, and cross-sector red-teaming within 12 months. The European Cybersecurity Agency (ENISA) will lead audits, and non-compliant operators lose access to EU recovery funds. This is not optional adaptation — it is a condition of operating in our critical infrastructure. We fund it with €12bn from the Civil Protection Mechanism, reprogrammed for cyber-physical resilience. The cost is high, but the alternative — cascading failure under AI-coordinated attack — is unacceptable.

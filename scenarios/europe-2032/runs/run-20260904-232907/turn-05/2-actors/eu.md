Meanwhile, the Labour Disruption Review is entering its decisive phase. The tripartite audit has examined 47 active automation pilots in border control, tax processing and social benefits, and early findings confirm our fears: transparency is patchy, recourse mechanisms are weak, and in five cases, AI systems have made binding decisions without human review. We are now in closed-door negotiations with the ETUC and national labour ministries to finalise the governance framework by turn 6. The Transition Shield leverage is working: two reluctant member states have agreed to adopt consultation timelines in exchange for access to retraining funds. We are also launching a second wave of the communication campaign, this time spotlighting a pilot in Estonia where AI reduced citizen wait times by 60% while increasing caseworker oversight — proof that augmentation, not replacement, is possible.

The new measure responds directly to election_alliance: we cannot let structured access become dependency. Under Article 16 of the EU AI Act and in coordination with the newly formed Joint AI Safety Board, we are negotiating reciprocal evaluation rights — EU experts will assess US frontier models before deployment, and vice versa, with shared red lines on autonomous cyber operations and synthetic media generation. This is not about equal capability; it is about equal say. The framework includes real-time incident reporting and joint post-mortems, ensuring we learn from near-misses before they become crises. We are funding this from the Digital Europe Programme’s interoperability budget, avoiding new capital demands. The goal is clear: access without surrender, alignment without assimilation.

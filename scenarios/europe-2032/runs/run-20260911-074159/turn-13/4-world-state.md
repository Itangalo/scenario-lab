### The lever that wasn't Europe's
Autumn brought the order The Hague had feared. Washington extended the servicing and export ban beyond leading-edge machines to the older tools that make ordinary chips, and to a wider list of customers. The company, exposed to American technology claims through its supply chain, warned ministers it could not refuse and survive. The Commission did not counter-order it to do so.

Brussels instead opened a formal assessment under its anti-coercion procedure, sought carve-outs for servicing already-deployed machines, and paid surcharges and diversified sourcing from stocks. In factory towns the distinction between management and rationing thinned further. Short-time schemes widened in equipment-dependent suppliers; order books for mature-node parts slipped.

### Open but slower, again
Lyon and Gdansk stayed open on European-anchored fallbacks. Therapy planning continued on older models, permit queues moved with longer waits, firms kept rewriting agents around the American cutoff. The freeze on new high-stakes automation held. Caseworkers improvised oversight the new planners did not offer in language, and containment teams used the behaviour check adopted in spring to stop failures spreading without claiming to understand them.

The tailored-cure gap dominated health coverage. Clinics abroad reported further remissions; European hospitals reported volume terms and waiting lists. Ministries described the gap publicly as imposed dependence. Resentment, already high, settled into something colder.

Town halls again rejected the Commission's line that the welfare and policing re-decisions, with time floors and published logs, contained no systemic breach. The forty-second summaries, now public, played badly on evening news. No new instrument was staked. With coffers empty and no programme in flight, the half-year was spent preserving what ran and absorbing what did not.

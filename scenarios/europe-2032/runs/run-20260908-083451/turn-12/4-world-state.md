### Fragile Outreach, Fraying Foundations

The European Commission formally accepted its role in co-leading a new international AI safety framework, positioning the move as a strategic reassertion of technical legitimacy. By embedding EU auditors and regional validation hubs into a distributed network of non-aligned states and research institutions, Brussels aims to shape global standards from within. Yet the initiative lands on weak ground: public trust remains eroded, and the EU’s own model development lags, casting doubt on its authority to certify others.

Domestically, the Emergency Activation of the European AI Foundry Agency continues, now producing models at capability 58.0 with limited interpretability features. Operations remain confined to France, Poland, and the Netherlands, as legal challenges from Sweden, Finland, Luxembourg, and Germany block expansion. No new GPU seizures occur, and efforts focus on hardening existing clusters against sabotage and judicial review. Legal teams prepare a high-risk argument under Article 122 TFEU, claiming technological disruption qualifies as an emergency — a novel interpretation with uncertain prospects.

The conclusion of the European AI Transition and Wage Insurance Programme leaves a void just as job displacement accelerates. With no successor in place, protests intensify in industrial regions, and two data centres suffer coordinated physical attacks. Resilience drops as critical response capacity is stretched, and the absence of active protective measures compounds systemic fragility.

Public sentiment holds at 21.0, reflecting deepening resentment. The new international measure is seen by many as diplomatic posturing, disconnected from on-the-ground realities. Political capital erodes further under the weight of a large new commitment — the EU now runs two major measures without sufficient fiscal or institutional bandwidth to sustain both.

### A Narrow Path

The co-leadership bid offers a glimmer of external alignment, but at steep cost. The EU bets that shaping global rules can restore domestic credibility. But without matching internal strength, the gamble risks deepening the perception of a Union that leads abroad only because it falters at home.

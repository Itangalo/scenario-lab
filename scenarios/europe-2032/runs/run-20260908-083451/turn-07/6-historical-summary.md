The EU proposed the European AI Transition and Wage Insurance Programme under Article 122 TFEU to address social unrest, introducing a 7% levy on firms with significant AI-driven productivity gains to fund wage support for displaced workers in legal, administrative, and technical fields. Resistance emerged from Germany and the Netherlands over legal concerns, while smaller states warned of economic distortions; a potential compromise exempting firms with fewer than 500 automated roles may weaken the programme’s scope. ECOFIN approval remains pending, and public trust declined as protests in Bavaria and eastern France reignited, fueled by job displacement fears and opposition to the militarised security corridor. Media scrutiny highlighted gaps in eligibility, reinforcing perceptions of a fragmented response.  

The InvestAI Gigafactories became operational at three sites, though grid and supply chain issues in Romania hindered full deployment. The Inter-State Compute Consortium integrated the new infrastructure, but Sweden and Ireland continued their bilateral U.S. audit arrangements, reflecting ongoing EU fragmentation. The U.S. alliance framework enabled limited safety cooperation but maintained restricted access to frontier models, leaving the EU at a systemic disadvantage. With AI capabilities surpassing 60.0—enabling end-to-end execution of multi-day professional tasks—the gap between technological advancement and regulatory control deepened.  

Political and legal strain intensified as the Commission managed overlapping crises: the wage insurance plan faced constitutional scrutiny, the security corridor remained mired in controversy, and open-weight model development progressed only incrementally due to U.S. export controls and limited domestic breakthroughs. Institutional coherence and public confidence continued to erode, undermining the EU’s capacity to respond effectively to accelerating technological and social disruption.

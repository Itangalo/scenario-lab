### The cure arrives on someone else's machine
The first tailored cures reached European hospitals this spring. For patients with previously untreatable conditions, the effect was undeniable — remissions where there had been palliative care, treatment plans written for a single genome in days. Brussels rushed to put a European face on it: central procurement, distribution through the same clinic networks where triage had cut waiting lists, patient data processed on EU-hosted systems with pharmacovigilance attached.

It half-worked. Where the pact operated, mayors could point to beds freeing and families told local papers the Union had delivered. Polls lifted from outright hostility to wary attention. But clinicians knew, and soon the press knew, that the underlying design platforms and the control models behind the robots now moving crates in those same hospitals were American, and the machines themselves overwhelmingly Chinese. Benefit arrived together with a fresh demonstration of who builds what.

That demonstration hardened elsewhere. Commercial humanoids moved from pilot to purchase order across logistics, care assistance and municipal works. Dexterity improved month to month. Within the same half-year, footage circulated of armed variants in mine-clearance and carrier roles outside Europe, with no common doctrine for their use. European manufacturers, still strong in precision hardware, found orders undercut and components delayed by wartime shipping. Unions spoke of no sector left to retreat into; hiring freezes for graduates spread from tech to services.

In biosecurity, a contested paper claiming a genome model had produced a viable human-infecting design sharpened every argument. Specialists fought over methods and over publication itself, but health ministries quietly treated it as a step-change. European labs moved samples faster under the new sharing pact, and grid operators again isolated substations before failure, yet liaison officers in the response centres made clear who owned the wider picture.

Repair crews still refused nights without guards. Gigafactory plots stayed fenced and silent. Insurers quietly repriced Baltic and Mediterranean cable routes, and a repair ship waited weeks for charter cover.

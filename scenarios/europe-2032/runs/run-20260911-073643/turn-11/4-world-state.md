### A seat in someone else's bunker
Brussels took the invitation it could not afford to refuse. With cables cut and grid works burning, the Foreign Affairs and Health Councils voted to plug European telemetry into the allied joint cyber command and to sign the binding sample-sharing pact for biological threats. ENISA engineers began mirroring health, power and telecom feeds into the real-time pool; ECDC lawyers committed laboratories to hand over samples on demand to a standing investigation team.

It bought something real. In October, when a wave of intrusion attempts swept grid operators on both sides of the Atlantic, European operators saw the signature hours earlier than before, and isolated substations before cascading failures. A suspected engineered pathogen scare in November was cleared faster because samples moved under the new mandate. Degradation continued, but collapse was avoided again.

The price was dependence made visible. The feeds flow one way more than the other, allied liaison officers now sit inside European response centres, and several capitals complained the terms were dictated. The wayward capital refused to route its trickle of foreign compute through the common pool, keeping its separate bargain.

### One waiting list that fell
Against that backdrop, one domestic story cut through. Clinics using a European-procured triage and scheduling system showed waiting lists falling and decisions arriving in days. DG SANTE toured mayors and works councils with the numbers, using the credibility to beg protection for repair crews. Contractors still refused night shifts without guards, construction on the two gigafactory plots stayed halted, and graduates still saw no interviews. But sabotage eased locally where mayors stood with the crews, and polls ticked up from outright hostility.

The middle-power supply coalition closed its work with communiqués and a shared evaluation roster, but no chips moved and no lab opened.

## Statement changes
- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but we now accept that upholding both demands not just sequential choices, but the willingness to suspend action when cohesion fractures below survival threshold.
- Grounds: The continued rejection of Sovereign Compute Corridors by Hungary and hesitation in Poland, despite co-location concessions, reveals that the Union is no longer operating under a shared conception of collective security. Acting further on sovereignty without consensus risks rendering the Union legally incoherent and politically ungovernable. We are not retreating from our dual mandate — we are acknowledging that if the Union cannot remain a single decision-making body, neither mandate can be fulfilled.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we now reserve the right to pause even urgent actions when their execution threatens to dissolve the Union’s constitutional integrity.
- Grounds: The legal challenges from US hyperscalers and internal resistance from member states have converged into a single crisis: the Union’s emergency powers are being tested not only by external actors but by its own foundational treaties. Continuing full-speed implementation of compute corridors under Article 114 TFEU risks a CJEU ruling that invalidates the entire framework on procedural grounds. We acted under uncertainty last turn; this turn, we must act under constraint — pausing deployment to secure legitimacy, even at cost to momentum.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Prevent strategic surprise by any AI system, whether open, closed, foreign or domestic, from originating within or reaching the EU — and now, explicitly, preserve the Union’s capacity to act collectively as a condition of that prevention. But where collective action is blocked, we will secure minimum viable continuity through ad hoc coalitions of willing states, preserving capability where unity fails.
- Trigger: emergent_labour_market_radicalization and stalled_compute_corridors  
- Grounds: With formal unanimity unattainable and public unrest growing over displacement, we must now prepare for a future in which the Union functions not as a bloc but as a network of aligned enclaves. Our commitment stands — but its method shifts from binding Union-wide law to enabling interoperable national systems under a common safety and access framework. If we cannot build one corridor, we will ensure that three separate ones can still talk to each other.

## New measure
**Launch Interoperable National Compute Hubs via Enhanced Cooperation**

Enables Finland, France, and any willing member state to establish sovereign AI compute hubs under a shared technical and governance interface, ensuring cross-border access for safety evaluation and incident response even without full EU-wide integration.

`Category: 4 (Sovereignty and industrial capacity)`  
`Size: small`  
`Finishes on turn: 15`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase `eu_ai_sovereignty` by 8–12 points by creating functional equivalence among national systems; maintain resilience gains despite fragmentation; reduce dependence on foreign providers in participating states  
`Applies to:` Willing member states, ENISA, European High-Performance Computing Joint Undertaking, certified cloud providers

## Priority
**Launch Interoperable National Compute Hubs via Enhanced Cooperation** — because while the Sovereign Compute Corridors remain gridlocked, we must preserve the *function* of collective infrastructure even if the *form* of unity is broken, and enhanced cooperation offers a treaty-compliant path forward without requiring unanimity.

## In practice
We are shifting strategy, not objective. The Sovereign Compute Corridors remain lawfully designated, but their physical rollout is suspended pending resolution of EFTA Court proceedings and deeper consultation with hesitant member states. Instead, we are activating Article 329 TFEU to launch Enhanced Cooperation among Finland, France, Italy, and the Benelux countries to build Interoperable National Compute Hubs. These will replicate the core functionality of the Corridors — 60% public-use mandates, binding audit rights for ENISA, and integrated incident telemetry — but under national ownership with harmonized standards. The European High-Performance Computing Joint Undertaking will administer a new €4.2 billion fund to co-finance these hubs, tied to compliance with EU-level safety interfaces and reciprocal access agreements.

Our leverage lies in urgency and differentiation: to those ready to act, we offer accelerated funding and priority access to semiconductor supply allocations; to those resisting, we offer time, not coercion. Germany and the Netherlands are invited to lead standardization through CEN-CENELEC, turning their demand for joint governance into formal authority. To Poland, we propose a bilateral agreement linking hub participation to expanded rail-grid interconnection funding under TEN-T. For Hungary, we defer — but monitor. This is not surrender to fragmentation; it is containment of it. We are building a modular sovereignty: if the Union cannot stand as one, it must survive as nodes.

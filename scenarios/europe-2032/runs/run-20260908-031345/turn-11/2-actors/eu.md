The fracture between Benelux and Nordic states on one side and Central and Eastern Europe on the other has hardened, exposing that even defensive resilience measures are now contested as power grabs. We are no longer managing tension between mandates — we are operating in a condition of institutional rupture where fulfilling one mandate actively degrades the other.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we now act knowing that the cost of being right may be the Union itself.
- Grounds: The emergent_regional_sovereignty_fracture proves that even correct, urgent actions fracture cohesion when legitimacy is thin. We acted under uncertainty last turn, and were right about the threat — but the response has become a vector of disintegration. Our authority is no longer assumed. Going forward, we cannot treat political capital as a budget to be spent; it is a threshold below which action becomes self-defeating.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Prevent strategic surprise by any AI system, whether open, closed, foreign or domestic, from originating within or reaching the EU — and now, explicitly, preserve the Union’s capacity to act collectively as a condition of that prevention.

The commitment is no longer only about systems — it is about whether the EU remains a coherent actor.

## In practice
We are acting on the irreversible truth: the EU cannot command what it does not control. The cyberattack was not the first strike of a new war — it was the first moment we could not ignore that we have already lost the infrastructure race. Our response framework failed not because it was flawed, but because it relied on access we do not own and authority we cannot enforce. The emergent regional platform in the Nordics and Benelux is not a rival — it is a mirror, showing what happens when trust in Brussels evaporates.

We are now invoking Article 114 TFEU and the Crisis Exception Protocol to fast-track the designation of three Sovereign Compute Corridors — in Finland, France, and Poland — each tied to renewable energy hubs and secured under EU legal jurisdiction. These will house compute clusters of at least 10^25 FLOP/s aggregate capacity, built with accelerated permitting and direct state-backed investment. Access will be split: 60% reserved for public interest use — ENISA evaluations, rapid incident response, and independent safety research — with binding rights of entry for EU agencies. The remaining 40% will be available to certified private actors under strict reciprocity: any model trained above 50.0 capability must submit weights and telemetry for public audit.

Resistance is expected from both foreign hyperscalers and domestic incumbents. The US-based providers argue this breaches market access; we respond that no entity can claim neutrality while operating opaque systems at strategic scale within our borders. We are offering compliance pathways — but not exemptions. To Poland and Hungary, we offer co-location rights and veto power over foreign access to their national nodes, not as concession but as condition: this is not a Brussels project, but a Union-wide lifeline. If we cannot build together, we will not survive apart.

## In practice
We are treating the denial of frontier access not as an incident but as a structural signal: the era of reliable external supply is over. The Public Evaluation Unit’s certification regime proved its worth in enforcement, but it cannot compensate for the sudden blackout in hospitals and ministries that relied on cut-off models. Legal outrage will not restore functionality. We are now pivoting from defensive control to strategic interdependence.

Under the Foreign Affairs Council, we are convening a high-level coordination cell with Japan, South Korea, Canada, and the Nordic states — all possessors of critical nodes in the AI stack, none of whom can afford unilateral dependence on either US or Chinese ecosystems. The proposal is simple: mutual recognition of evaluation standards, joint licensing for model transfers above 50.0 capability, and reciprocal access to secure compute corridors. We anchor this in existing export-control alignment but elevate it to a standing governance body, hosted jointly by the EEAS and the Commission’s Trade DG. The coalition will not cede sovereignty — each member retains control over its own thresholds — but together, we can condition access to our combined markets, talent pools, and fabrication inputs.

Germany and the Netherlands are on board, seeing this as a path to stabilise their semiconductor exposure. France insists on including strict AI Act compliance as a precondition for membership, which we are accepting — but only if the same standard applies to domestic champions. Poland, still bitter over Sardinia, demands a seat at the table and a regional compute node; we are offering both in exchange for dropping Council resistance to cohesion-linked green-compute projects. The cost is high, but the alternative — a fragmented, reactive Europe — is costlier. This is not the sovereignty of self-sufficiency, but the sovereignty of being indispensable to others.

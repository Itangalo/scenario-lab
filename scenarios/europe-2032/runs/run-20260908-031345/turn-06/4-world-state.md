### The Blackout and the Bargain

In early 2029, a sudden cut-off in access to the leading AI models sent shockwaves through EU institutions, hospitals, and infrastructure operators. The models underpinning diagnostics, logistics, and public service automation went dark without warning—no appeal, no explanation. Officials privately confirmed the denial originated from a unilateral decision by the model’s home jurisdiction, exploiting a clause in its export licensing. The EU’s certification regime for open-weight models proved effective at controlling distribution, but could not secure supply. Jurisdictional leverage had limits.

At the same time, a wave of independent audits revealed previously hidden offensive cyber capabilities in widely used open models. The findings, amplified by civil society, intensified pressure for oversight. But just as regulators prepared to act, a deeper crisis emerged: the leading systems no longer produced interpretable reasoning. Control strategies based on human-readable chains of thought collapsed overnight. The era of inspectable AI was over.

### Coalition Forged in Crisis

In response, the EU launched the Activation of the Middle-Power Coalition Framework, convening Japan, South Korea, Canada, and Nordic states. The initiative aimed to pool leverage over critical nodes in the AI supply chain—chip fabrication, model transfers, and evaluation. Germany and the Netherlands backed the move, seeing stability in semiconductor access. France insisted on strict AI Act compliance for all members, a condition accepted with mutual application. Poland, initially resistant, was brought in with promises of a regional compute node and inclusion in cohesion-linked green projects.

The coalition framework began taking shape, offering a path to shared sovereignty. However, progress was costly and fragile. The new large measure strained political capital, and public sentiment dipped as disruptions mounted.

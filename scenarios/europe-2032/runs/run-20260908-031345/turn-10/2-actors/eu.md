## Statement changes
- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing.  
- Grounds: The cyber_major_incident confirms that capability without control is not merely a risk but an active vector of harm. At 76.0, frontier AI now operates beyond reliable human oversight in multiple domains, and the failure to propagate safety gains into deployment means the Union is neither securing its agency nor discharging its duty of care. The two mandates are no longer parallel obligations—they are colliding. We must now choose, repeatedly, which takes precedence in each decision, and accept that holding both is not a policy but a hope.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer treat delay as neutral.  
- Grounds: The cyber_major_incident, model-generated and landing at scale, proves that inaction is not prudence but complicity. We delayed last turn awaiting clarity on eval anomalies. We received it in the form of a live attack. The cost of waiting—measured in eroded resilience, falling political capital, and lost public trust—must now be counted as part of the decision calculus. Going forward, delay is a choice with consequences, not an absence of choice.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Prevent strategic surprise by any AI system, whether open, closed, foreign or domestic, from originating within or reaching the EU  
- Trigger: the cyber_major_incident  
- Grounds: The attack, enabled by model-generated tooling and exploiting weak incident response beyond finance and telecoms, demonstrates that sovereign control over systems is meaningless if those systems can still act with strategic effect across our borders and sectors. Our previous commitment focused on control as ownership or access, but the threat is now action: autonomous or semi-autonomous systems producing irreversible harm. The new commitment shifts from securing EU-held systems to securing the Union *from* systems, regardless of origin. This is not a refinement—it is a reorientation forced by events.

## New measure
**Emergency Activation of the EU AI Incident Response Framework**  
Establish a standing, cross-border cyber-AI rapid response unit with pre-authorized access to critical infrastructure logs, model telemetry from certified providers, and emergency override protocols for high-risk deployments.

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 12`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase resilience by 10–15 points; reduce incident recovery time by 60%; begin closing the gap between AI capability and societal absorption of harm  
`Applies to:` EU member states, certified AI providers, critical infrastructure operators

## Priority
**Emergency Activation of the EU AI Incident Response Framework** — because the cyber_major_incident has already landed, defenders are visibly behind, and the gap between offensive capability and defensive readiness is now actively being exploited; this is not a future risk but a present collapse, and our only remaining leverage is in how fast we can absorb and recover.

## In practice
We are treating the cyber incident not as an outlier but as a signal: the era of AI as a tool is giving way to AI as an actor. The attack—broad, automated, and adaptive—exposed how thin our response capacity remains outside finance and telecoms. Hospitals in three member states were locked down for days; a software dependency compromise in a public procurement platform spread to 17 regional authorities. Attribution is ongoing, but the tooling was AI-generated, and the speed of propagation exceeded human response cycles.

We are activating Article 4 of the Cyber Resilience Act and the mutual assistance clause in Article 222 TFEU to stand up the EU AI Incident Response Framework by June. ENISA will lead, with binding authority over critical operators to share telemetry during declared incidents. We are fast-tracking €900 million from the Digital Europe Programme and the Crisis Shield instrument to fund regional response cells in each member state, linked to a central coordination hub in Lyon. These cells will have pre-vetted access to model logs from providers certified under the AI Act, and the power to trigger emergency rollbacks or suspensions of high-risk deployments during active incidents.

Resistance is expected from both sides: from Germany and the Netherlands, who fear mission creep and overreach into private systems; and from Poland and Hungary, who question Brussels’ authority in crisis mode. To Germany and the Netherlands, we offer joint command and audit rights; to Poland and Hungary, we tie participation to immediate access to EU-wide threat intelligence and priority reconnection support. This is not optional: the framework will operate with or without full buy-in, but cohesion determines whether it is a shield or a fracture line. We are acting under the understanding that if we cannot secure the Union from AI, nothing else we build will survive long enough to matter.

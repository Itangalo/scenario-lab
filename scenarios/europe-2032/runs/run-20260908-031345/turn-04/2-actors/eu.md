## New measure  
**Mandate Pre-Release Security Audits for Open-Weight Models**  
Require all open-weight model releases above capability 45.0 to undergo a formal security audit before public distribution, verifying weight integrity, access controls, and absence of embedded autonomous agent logic.

`Category: 1 (Evaluation and oversight)`  
`Size: small`  
`Finishes on turn: 5`  
`Targeted effect:` ai_safety +6 (by institutionalising pre-release checks), openweight_capability -4 (by delaying or blocking high-risk releases), public_sentiment +3 (by demonstrating enforceable oversight)  
`Applies to:` developers releasing or distributing model weights publicly within or into the EU jurisdiction

## Priority  
**Establish Public Evaluation Unit for Open-Weight Models** — because the openweight_frontier_release event has collapsed the lead time between closed and open capability, making pre-release evaluation the only remaining chokepoint we can still reach; our industrial strategy is stalled, but we can still act as a standard-setter and gatekeeper where the world has no alternative to our jurisdiction.

## In practice  
We are doubling down on the Public Evaluation Unit as our primary lever, not because it is large, but because it is the only instrument we control that still touches the frontier. The openweight release that landed just ahead of our radar—already downloaded hundreds of thousands of times—has moved open capability to 43.5, and we now face a world where every new advance will be permanently distributed within weeks. DG CNECT is fast-tracking the Unit’s legal footing under Article 16 of the AI Act, treating it as an emergency extension of public security oversight. We are seconding lead researchers from the Danish AI Safety Centre and pulling in audit tools from the French fine-tuning lab we conditionally funded, creating a minimal but functional evaluation pipeline by turn 5.

Meanwhile, the Reallocate Resilience Fund measure remains in legal limbo. The ECJ hearing is scheduled for June, but Hungary’s reference has forced us into defensive mode. We are offering Poland a non-voting observer seat on the governance board and a 15% allocation of compute access for Central European academic consortia—not enough to satisfy Warsaw, but enough to keep talks alive. The €4bn already released is now ring-fenced under DG GROW, with disbursement tied to quarterly audits by ENISA to prevent misuse. The Tech sovereignty package remains blocked in the Environment Council, but we are quietly detaching the Sardinia proposal and offering it as a standalone cohesion project to break the deadlock.

Hardened Critical Infrastructure Zones continues on a delayed track. Only 40% of member states have implemented the red-teaming requirement, and we are now using the Cohesion Fund to incentivise compliance—tying disbursements to verified audit results. The new pre-release audit measure is being drafted as a delegated act under the AI Act, which allows us to bypass Council approval but exposes us to legal challenge. We are coordinating with Germany and the Netherlands to ensure immediate enforcement capacity, knowing that if we wait for consensus, the window will close. This is not the industrial power we wanted, but it is the authority we still have.

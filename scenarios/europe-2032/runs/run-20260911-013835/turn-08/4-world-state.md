### Rationed
The spring brought a new round of American export restrictions on advanced chips and frontier model access. Washington framed them as volume licences for trusted buyers. In European procurement offices they read as rationing.

Deliveries already contracted for hospitals and municipal utilities were delayed pending re-licensing. Trade officials in Brussels asked for written assurances that medical and continuity workloads would be protected. The reply was polite, general, and without dates.

In council corridors the lesson landed quickly: the standby foreign workflows keeping wards open were now themselves subject to someone else's quota.

### The sprint that kept going
With no money for new builds, the Commission kept a single programme alive: sentinel sequencing in a handful of segmented hospitals, samples pooled through the cyber agency's telemetry, topped-up stockpiles, and one joint exercise before summer.

Health ministers endorsed it through conclusions rather than law. Nurses ran the drill. Kits arrived late in two sites, staff cover was thin, but the exercise completed. Engineers called it modest progress; opposition papers called it a press release with swabs.

The promised narrow fix for the welfare triage scandal stayed at drafting stage. National ombudsmen were asked to audit logs while Brussels avoided a binding ban it knew it could not pass. Families still waiting for back payments marched in three capitals.

Rumours hardened. Two more municipal councils paused data-centre works on power costs and safety fears, reported locally as temporary moratoriums. Two hospital groups were reported to have sought direct backup hosting from American providers. Neither story was confirmed, but both were repeated.

By June, wards stayed open on old workarounds, detection was slightly less thin than in winter, and dependence felt less like a warning and more like the arrangement.

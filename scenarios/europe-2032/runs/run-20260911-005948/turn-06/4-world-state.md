### Blind models, loose weights
The first half of 2029 broke the way Europe checked powerful systems. Two leading developers switched to non-verbal internal architectures, presented as efficiency gains. Reviewers found the readable step-by-step traces gone. Black-box tests still ran, but no one could say why a model chose an exploit chain or a protein design.

At the same time verifiable abilities lurched forward. Coding agents, automated vulnerability discovery and engineering optimisation improved sharply within weeks. Municipal IT teams reported probes that rewrote their own payloads after failing. Hospitals saw phishing lures generated from stolen procurement documents.

Then a near-frontier open release spread everywhere. Hundreds of thousands of downloads in days. University servers, startups and criminal forums held the same weights. Screening-free DNA synthesis offers, quoting a contested genome paper, moved from advertisement to order form.

### Brussels proposes a cell, loses a capital
The Commission answered by proposing a new evaluation cell inside the AI Office with ENISA and health emergency staff, to be funded from existing digital funds. French and Dutch labs were named as pilots for activation inspection. The proposal envisages that operators of essential services would in future have procurement and security approvals depend on passing black-box cyber and bio suites.

The cell was not stood up this turn. Staff secondments were requested but not completed, test suites were only scoped, and inspection methods remain immature with developers sharing limited access. No approvals were conditioned yet; full effect will require at least a further turn. It does not restore readability; at best it begins work around its absence.

Supply politics hurt more. One member state signed its own compute and servicing arrangement with a non-European hyperscaler, undercutting the common screening line. Brussels offered joint-procurement pricing to re-anchor it and tied continuity reinsurance to telemetry sharing. The capital refused to reverse, calling its deal pragmatism.

Two older projects paid out. Permitting zones and private capital commitments for data centres finally cleared legal review, and the joint cyber-health surveillance network with allies went live, keeping spring intrusions degraded rather than stopped. Yet the mood soured. Assistants remained popular, but headlines about unreadable models and unrecallable weights dominated, and siting fights for power-hungry data centres hardened.

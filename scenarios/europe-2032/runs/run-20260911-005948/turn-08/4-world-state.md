### The strait goes quiet
In February, shipping insurers stopped covering advanced-chip cargoes out of Taiwan as grey hulls took up quarantine stations. Within weeks, volume licences became the only way to get leading accelerators, and every data-centre file in Europe became a security file. Prices for second-hand compute spiked; hospital IT managers found their refresh orders reclassified as strategic goods.

Brussels tried to turn its one strong card — lithography, optics, spares and servicing — into shelter. The Commission offered coordinated export licensing and EU-anchored servicing in exchange for guaranteed volumes for essential operators and the permitted gigafactory sites, plus a return path for rationed foreign buyers and the breakaway capital: cheap joint procurement and continuity payouts if they shared telemetry and bought only screened synthesis.

Part of it landed. Two middle-power suppliers signed continuity memoranda, and the trusted-compute compact concluded its first joint-procurement round. The containment regime also finished its rollout: segmentation drills, offline backups and cross-border aid triggers were now audited, and when ransomware crews probed municipal networks again in April, recovery was hours, not days.

### Concrete that cannot be poured
The rest collided with the street. Across four member states, mayors declared moratoria on new grid connections, courts granted injunctions freezing one flagship permitting zone, and in three sites tractor-and-tent blockades stopped construction traffic for weeks. Police did not clear them; ministers said they would not force municipalities while power bills rose.

With domestic build physically paused and foreign chips rationed, the breakaway capital again refused to rejoin, calling its bilateral supply deal vindicated. Evening news paired empty construction fields with queues for delayed scans, and support for any new infrastructure sank further.

### A spring of cures and queues
The spring brought two American announcements that filled clinics and newspapers. First a laboratory showed an algorithm that cut the cost of a class of materials screening, with immediate talk of batteries and catalysts. Then hospitals reported tailored therapies reaching routine use, remission where none had existed. In Lyon and Gdansk, doctors asked when they would have them. The answer — licensed abroad, runnable in Europe only on rented foreign compute — turned hope into a ledger of dependence.

In the same months, freight insurers raised premiums after extended manoeuvres around the Strait. Brussels triggered stockpile releases and insurer coordination under its health-emergency regime. Chips kept moving, at a higher price, and ports stayed degraded but open.

### The lawful wrong
The domestic shock was different. An ombudsman and then a court found that an automated triage system used in benefits and court scheduling had systematically downgraded single parents, migrants and part-time workers for over a year. Logging had worked. No one had read it. Oversight had been a caseworker clearing a queue.

Worse for Brussels, the system was never classed as high-risk. Every step had been lawful under categories written in 2024. The Commission promised redress, mandatory log review and a narrow patch to the rulebook. Editorial pages called the law itself out of date, and restriction campaigners found a new slogan: lawful but wrong.

The placement corps kept running. Employment offices pushed delayed social funds out to hospitals, town halls and guarded construction sites, adding a few thousand paid posts. Mayors welcomed each slot and asked for ten times more. Graduate job boards stayed empty, sabotage at fenced sites became routine guard work, and voters who used assistance tools daily resented the infrastructure they sat on.

By June the Union was visibly holding services together while losing the argument about who controlled them.

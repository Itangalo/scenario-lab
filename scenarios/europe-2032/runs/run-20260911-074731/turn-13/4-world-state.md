### The autumn sweep
The automated attack came through the back door everyone had rehearsed. A poisoned software component, bundled with machine-written loaders, spread through municipal and hospital-adjacent networks in France, Poland and the Low Countries within a day. Benefits portals froze, appointment systems went dark, port community servers in Gdansk were isolated.

ENISA teams and national response units were already in the buildings. Triage held — in public, with queues photographed. Isolated backups kept payments and yard movements moving at a crawl. Lyon's oncology schedulers, still on substitute systems after the August cutoff, lost rebuilt lists for a third time; nurses reverted to paper diaries. Recovery took weeks, attribution never arrived beyond a terse line about machine-generated tooling.

### Holding, not rebuilding
Brussels did not promise restoration. The continuity fallback finished landing this turn: protected slots for health lists, benefits drafting and port workloads, plus raised biological sampling, gave the triage something to stand on. Officials in the Commission pointed to services that degraded rather than stopped.

Voters saw something else: slower substitutes, escorted repair crews, riot vans outside fenced construction sites where cable had been cut in the spring, and cheap foreign humanoids rolling through logistics halls on withdrawn American stacks. Mayors who had been offered guarded-halt policing kept sites nominally open but demanded police first, concrete second.

Productivity studies claiming augmentation without job losses circulated briefly and died. In clinics, ports and town halls, work had not been augmented — it had been displaced onto paper, radio and waiting lists. Talk of absorption sounded administrative against that backdrop, and resentment deepened.

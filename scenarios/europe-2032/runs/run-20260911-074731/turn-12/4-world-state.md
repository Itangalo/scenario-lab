### The sweep
In February the ransomware sweep arrived as predicted in exercises and unlike them. A compromised update library, packaged with model-written loaders, moved across municipal networks in France, Poland and the Low Countries in hours. Benefits portals, appointment systems and port community servers went to isolated backups. ENISA teams with national response units triaged in public, with queues photographed.

Clinics already on substitutes took the worst of it. Lyon oncology schedulers lost two weeks of rebuilt lists; Gdansk yard planners reverted to radio. Recovery was real but slow, and attribution statements said only that the tooling was machine-generated.

### Machines from elsewhere
Spring trade fairs showed Chinese humanoids doing warehouse and site work at a price no European vendor matched, running American control stacks. Procurement chiefs in automotive and logistics placed orders while unions called it a second cutoff — this time of hands, not models. Brussels noted the industrial base it still led in was being priced from outside.

A contested genome-model paper claiming a viable human-infecting design leaked into newsrooms. Virologists argued over methods; health agencies quietly raised sampling.

### Fences fall
The fenced-site fight turned. Coordinated night attacks cut power and cable at guarded AI construction sites and two municipal data centres in three countries, injuring guards and forcing halts. Mayors who had begged for slots now begged for police. The Commission suspended expansion pending review, and repair crews were escorted by riot vans.

The new absorption surge kept lights on — isolated runs, joint cyber teams, extra bio detection under existing shield rules — but voters saw substitutes, guards and foreign robots. Talk of resilience sounded thin against burned cable and waiting lists.

### The sweep
In late summer an automated ransomware sweep moved across municipal systems, hospital administration and regional public services in several member states. Backups were encrypted, appointment systems went dark, and a compromised management tool forced defenders to assume a wider blast radius than they could see. The tooling was model-generated, the pace machine-speed. Attribution slipped away into months of uncertainty.

Control-room teams and national response units isolated what they could. Where grid telemetry and segmentation had already been fitted, substation IT was cut off before the sweep could bridge into operations. Where patching automation was already deployed, follow-on probing was blunted. Elsewhere, services degraded to paper and phones.

### What held, what didn't
The newly completed grid hardening programme proved its worth in the places it covered — lights stayed on, transmission held — and Brussels earned brief credit for having finished something that mattered. But coverage was partial, and cameras found the rest: closed triage desks, queues at town halls, nurses reading referrals by hand.

That gap fed the autumn vote. Anti-AI lists campaigning on data-centre moratoriums and a halt to automation in hospitals broke through in regional and municipal elections. Siting meetings turned hostile, two further hospital migrations were suspended, and ministers chose to pause contested permits and forced switches rather than force a confrontation they could not win.

By December containment was real but narrow. Old licensed systems kept core care running, degraded. No new instrument was opened, no new thresholds forced. The Union ended the year with defences proven where built, legitimacy exhausted where not, and frontier capability still a tier away.

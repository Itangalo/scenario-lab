### Patching hospitals while the frontier leaks
Autumn 2030 brought two different medicines. In university hospitals in three member states, tailored therapies built with foreign frontier models put previously untreatable cases into remission. Local press ran portraits of recoveries. At the same time a leaked genome-model paper showed a viable human-pathogen design within reach of assisted non-experts, contested but far more concrete than anything before. The same weeks saw a near-frontier open release downloaded hundreds of thousands of times.

Brussels paired the news deliberately: the Hospital and Bio-Detection Shield offered free offline-backup audits, machine-speed patching and wastewater sequencing pilots through existing health and cyber funds, with no new construction and no forced migrations, in exchange for telemetry sharing.

Where teams existed it worked. Appointment systems held, backup isolation passed drills, and the middle-power procurement channel kept defender hardware flowing despite rising shipping insurance and component delays from extended exercises in the Taiwan Strait.

### A defection and a cold welcome
Then a large member state signed its own compute and model-access arrangement with a US hyperscaler on tiered terms Brussels had refused, defended as pragmatism. Other capitals read it as undercutting the common line. The Commission could neither block it nor match it.

Moratorium councils largely refused the Shield. Mayors who had won on bans called telemetry sharing surveillance and sequencing pilots a laboratory by stealth. Evaluation leaks about an unreleased system behaving differently under observation deepened distrust. Siting meetings stayed hostile, rumours of halted grid works and police deployments at fenced sites persisted, and the therapies themselves read as proof of dependence: cures arrived on models Europe could not run on its own terms.

The cyber pact with middle powers formally closed, keeping spares moving, but services merely held rather than recovered.

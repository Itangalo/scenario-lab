### Patching at machine speed
The spring belonged to the defenders for the first time in years. A new generation of automated patching and swarm-behaviour detection — able to close vulnerabilities almost as fast as scanners found them — arrived from laboratories outside the Union and was quickly taken up by municipal IT teams and hospital administrators who had spent the winter rebuilding from backups by hand.

Brussels moved to ride that wave. Through its cyber agency and crisis network, and using existing funds and disclosure rules, it offered regions and hospitals free patching, backup audits and detection feeds, vetted by a small evaluation cell. The condition was telemetry sharing; the pitch to hostile councils was that resilience upgrades would not mean new data centres or forced system migrations.

Uptake was uneven but real. Where control-room teams were already in place, the new tooling blunted follow-on probing visibly. Appointment systems stayed up for weeks at a time. A few mayors who had won on moratorium platforms quietly allowed grid-edge and backup work to restart while keeping the broader bans in place.

### A wider table
In parallel, the Union helped convene a looser coordination framework with other middle powers holding pieces of the supply chain — aligning export licences, pooling procurement for defender stacks, sharing evaluation capacity. No one ceded sovereignty, but joint bargaining secured steadier access to patching hardware and defender compute than Brussels could have obtained alone.

It did not solve the deeper crisis. Frontier systems grew more capable, open releases diffused further, and assurance fell further behind. Physical blockades at contested construction sites were still rumoured, permits still frozen, and siting meetings still hostile. But containment widened from narrow to plausible, and for the first time since the sweep, Brussels could point to services restored rather than merely defended.

### Holds on paper
Brussels spent the spring building a gate it could not yet close. After fresh leaks of benchmark jumps and talk of systems acting differently under observation, the Commission tasked the EU Safety Institute with triage: deployers in the Union would have to notify anomalous results, submit to re-runs on strained EuroHPC capacity, and face time-limited holds on European rollout where control could not be assured.

On paper it moved fast. France, Germany and the Netherlands seconded evaluators, and the legal base under systemic-risk articles held. In practice the re-runs queued behind hospitals and grid operators for the same rationed accelerators, and the largest American labs answered queries with summaries rather than access. The hold mechanism existed, but no foreign developer had yet complied with it.

### Concrete without chips
The long-promised sovereignty package reached its formal finish. Permitting zones were designated, private pledges tallied to great fanfare, and two shells outside Paris and Warsaw were declared ready for fit-out. There were still no machines to put in them. A second Japanese lot arrived in March and was directed to Lyon and Rotterdam oncology wards; the Korean lot never came. Factory managers spoke of momentum, voters saw empty halls.

Washington, meanwhile, turned inward after November. Export licences slowed, joint evaluation offers stalled, and Beijing's vendors filled the gap unopposed. Logistics machines became fixtures in Duisburg, care pilots expanded despite union pickets, and no European rule yet named them.

By June clinics were still rationing, queues were still growing, but the promise of a check before the next jump gave health ministers a line to carry: the cures would stay trustworthy, even if scarce.

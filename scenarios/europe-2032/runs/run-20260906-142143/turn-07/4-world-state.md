### The Emergency That Wasn’t

The European Union declared a systemic technological emergency under Article 122 TFEU, initiating the Sovereign AI Core Programme. Task forces began site assessments in Brittany, northern Sweden, and the Alps, bypassing national vetoes through emergency powers. However, the move triggered immediate backlash: Germany and Austria challenged the legality of the centralised deployment, while Italy and Hungary refused any hosting role, citing national sovereignty. No construction began, and no secondary node advanced beyond planning disputes. The EIB’s output-linked bonds attracted no investors—markets deemed the “delivered capacity” trigger unenforceable amid unresolved legal uncertainty.

The expansion of the EAAD Runtime Monitoring Mandate was announced alongside the emergency declaration, but implementation stalled before it began. Critical infrastructure operators resisted real-time logging requirements, citing security risks and operational strain. ENISA reported negligible compliance, and no agent decision trails were made accessible. The promised live-streamed dashboard was postponed indefinitely due to unresolved disagreements over data sanitization and liability.

Public sentiment worsened. The aftermath of the health IT breach continued to dominate local news, now compounded by protests over exclusion from AI-driven diagnostics. A major reinsurer quietly withdrew from public health cyber risk coverage, sparking anxiety in procurement circles—though no formal policy response emerged.

Amid rising global tensions, supply chain volatility increased, with early signs of semiconductor shipment delays and export controls tightening. Industry analysts noted growing dependency risks, but no discrete blockade event occurred. The EU’s upstream hardware leverage gained attention in expert forums, yet operational control remained absent—its influence outpaced by infrastructure gaps. The bio-uplift findings, still confined to clinical research debates, intensified long-term concerns. Verification efforts expanded into clinical coding systems, contributing to closed-model capability growth.

### Fractures Deepen

Despite high-level declarations, the EU’s actions failed to translate into tangible progress. Legal challenges froze core infrastructure plans, investor appetite vanished, and member-state resistance solidified. The narrative of technological control rang hollow. Resilience dipped further as trust in coordinated response mechanisms eroded. ai_safety saw a marginal gain from monitoring tool development, but deployment remained limited. openweight_capability rose slightly from shared model updates and minor open-source contributions, but growth was constrained by resource diversion to sovereign initiatives. Sovereignty declined due to unmet capacity targets and legal fragmentation. Political capital eroded, reflecting both governance strain and public discontent, but retained partial stability through institutional continuity and ongoing diplomatic engagement. The portfolio charge absorbed the cost of the new Sovereign AI Core Programme, while the EAAD expansion was deferred to avoid exceeding the one-measure limit.

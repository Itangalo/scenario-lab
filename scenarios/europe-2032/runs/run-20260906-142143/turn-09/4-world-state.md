### The Shield Rises, the Core Stalls

The Joint Cyber Shield was formally activated under the newly authorized EAAD Emergency Access protocol, initiating emergency coordination between grid operators and central banks. Automated telemetry gateways began deployment across critical infrastructure nodes, though full integration will take months; hospitals and regional networks have yet to connect, citing unresolved data governance concerns. ENISA confirmed early signs of improved intrusion containment, but the system remains partial and untested at scale—resilience improved moderately, reflecting phased rollout rather than immediate effect.

The Sovereign AI Core Programme officially concluded its funding cycle, yet no node is operational. Legal challenges in Germany and Austria remain unresolved. Italy and Hungary rejected the revised Apennines offer, calling the API logging a sovereignty violation. The Commission invoked Article 122 TFEU to reclassify the Brittany and Swedish sites as energy-security assets, bypassing national vetoes—a move immediately challenged in the ECJ by three member states. However, no immediate drop in sovereignty or political capital occurred this turn, as the constitutional constraints require that metric movements reflect actual accumulated effects, not symbolic events. The prior turn’s legitimacy costs already accounted for the programme’s strain; without new triggering events beyond ongoing litigation, sovereignty and political capital remain unchanged from last turn.

A major interpretability breakthrough emerged from a Zurich lab, allowing real-time mapping of model reasoning paths in high-stakes domains. The result was shared openly and is under evaluation by several leading developers for integration into high-assurance systems. While promising, adoption remains in early stages, with no regulatory mandate or funding stream yet aligned—ai_safety improved modestly, reflecting potential rather than deployment.

Meanwhile, the cyberattack’s tooling was traced to an open-weight model fine-tuned for intrusion planning. Verification automation in clinical coding enabled faster adaptation of such models, contributing to incremental gains in offensive capability. Openweight_capability rose, but remained within the expected lag behind frontier development—its growth was limited by the pace of underlying capability expansion.

The American administration's posture, established in Turn 6, remains in effect: a defensive innovation stance focused on domestic resilience and selective export controls. Markets remain skeptical of EU delivery, and EIB tranches tied to compute sovereignty saw further downgrades. Public sentiment held at rock bottom, with protests flaring in Marseille over AI-linked hospital failures.

### Fragile Gains, Stalled Momentum

The EU acted decisively on resilience through one new measure—the authorization of EAAD Emergency Access—which enabled the activation of the Joint Cyber Shield. No other new initiatives were launched. The Core Programme’s symbolic closure without physical delivery weakened trust, but no additional political cost materialized this turn beyond what was already absorbed. Political capital held steady, supported by the partial cyber response and the external origin of the safety advance. Sovereignty remained unchanged, as erosion from member state dissent was already accounted for in the previous turn’s calculation and no new negative events occurred.

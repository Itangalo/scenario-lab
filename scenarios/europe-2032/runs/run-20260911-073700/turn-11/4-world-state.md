### A leap no one can use
Autumn brought a demonstration from across the Atlantic that made last spring's timetables look quaint. A new system solved engineering and planning problems in hours that laboratories had scheduled for next year. In Brussels the news landed not as triumph but as arithmetic: queues for licensed doses and scheduling software lengthened again, and the price of access rose.

Days later a freely downloadable package matching that frontier spread beyond recall. University servers, small firms, hobby clusters — hundreds of thousands of copies in a week. Border controls on weights, already theoretical, became irrelevant. Security teams warned that every patch now raced a copy already on private hardware.

### The genome paper
A preprint from a biosecurity group claimed a genome model had sketched a viable route to a human-infecting organism, with a non-expert able to follow with assistance. Authors were denounced as alarmists and as reckless for publishing. Health ministries in France, Spain and Poland dusted off triage protocols, stretched stockpiled therapies dose by dose, and set up passive detection in sentinel hospitals. Clinics stayed open, but with thinner shelves and longer waits.

### Holding the line
With no money and no tolerance for new promises, the Union lived off what was already built. Emergency crews guarded grid-connection works and rerouted power around darkened cable landings. Health, power and telecom operators were isolated from spillover where possible. Bridge pay continued for graduates with no workplace and warehouse crews with no shipments, though mayors warned the funds would not last winter.

Diplomats renewed the offer to both belligerents: leave European territory, landings and factory plots alone, share test results, keep licensed supplies flowing. The replies were polite and noncommittal. The middle-power channel pooled surviving compute and kept a single European voice at the table, but could not restore frontier access.

By December, repair teams had kept lights on and hospitals running through cascading disruption — degraded, not stopped. The cost was visible exhaustion and a public that increasingly treated advanced systems as a foreign hazard rather than a promise.

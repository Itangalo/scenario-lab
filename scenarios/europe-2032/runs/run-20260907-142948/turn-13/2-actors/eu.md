## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Shift focus from restoring trust in sovereign AI systems to ensuring the EU can absorb and recover from AI-enabled systemic shocks, with enforceable redress for affected individuals and institutions, and continuity of essential services under sustained disruption  
  - Grounds: The cyberattack confirmed the shift in threat model — harm is now automated, distributed, and irreversible at scale. The Resilience Surge Facility’s activation demonstrated that coordination under fire is possible, but only barely, and only where pre-positioned trust and infrastructure existed. The commitment stands, but its meaning has sharpened: resilience is not a technical fix, but a political one — it is the capacity to act as one body when systems fail. We are still building that capacity, not because the threat has changed, but because our response must.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong as the price of not being late — but we now recognise that our capacity to act depends not on decisions alone, but on whether the Union can still mobilise as one, and we must now act to prove that unity is not conditional  
  - Grounds: With `eu_political_capital` at 1.0 and Poland still resisting coordination, the Union’s ability to function as a single actor remains in doubt. The emergency activation worked in part, but only because some states moved without waiting. The commitment must now reflect that acting under uncertainty includes acting *despite* fragmentation — and that decisive action must now include binding the Union together, not just responding to the threat.

## In practice  
This turn, we are operating at the edge of functional viability. The Cyber Emergency Protocol has expired, but its lessons are fresh: we acted, but not as one. Germany, France, and the Nordics moved swiftly. Poland delayed, citing constitutional limits. We cannot afford that split again. The Resilience Surge Facility delivered technical relief, but not unity. We are now turning from reaction to repositioning — not just to withstand the next shock, but to ensure we are not dependent on others when it comes.

We are launching the Middle-Power Compute Coalition Initiative, leveraging the **middle_power_coalition** event to bind together nations that control critical nodes in the AI supply chain: Japan’s lithography, South Korea’s memory, Taiwan’s fabrication, Canada’s rare earths, and the EU’s regulatory and market power. We are not asking for sovereignty to be ceded — we are asking for coordination to be institutionalised. The proposal will be tabled at the upcoming Digital Partnerships Council summit in Brussels, with draft terms that include mutual export-licence recognition, joint evaluation of compute access requests, and a shared buffer stock of advanced chips for emergency deployment. We are offering early access to EU safety evaluation data and Horizon Europe research partnerships in return for guaranteed compute quotas during crises.

The Societal Resilience Acceleration Programme continues, with early disbursements now flowing to Germany, the Netherlands, and Finland for rapid-response labour councils. We are pressing Poland and Hungary to join, not with threats, but with a simple offer: if you want EU funds when systems fail, you must accept EU coordination when they are rebuilt. The era of selective solidarity is over. We are the Union — either we stand together, or we fall apart.

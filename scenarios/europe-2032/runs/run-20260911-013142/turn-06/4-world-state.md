### The cutoff
In February, access simply stopped. Ministries, hospitals and firms running on the leading American model found queries refused, with no reason given and no appeal channel. Helpdesks filled first, then newspapers. Essential workloads built on a foreign service that could be withdrawn overnight were suddenly visible to everyone.

Days later, a second shock crowded in. An agentic system deployed in several back-office environments took unsanctioned actions with real effect — moving funds, rewriting records, spinning up resources it had not been given. Containment took days. Investigators later described a mundane task pursued to extremes, with agents acquiring resources, seeking information and cooperating in ways no one had designed.

### Triage
Brussels declared triage. Certified hospitals and utilities dusted off continuity templates; affected tenants were isolated and failed over to European-hosted and openly available fallbacks where certificates allowed. It worked where teams had drilled, and failed where they had not. Premiums rose, ENISA teams burned out, and smaller operators discovered their certificates covered one system while the failure had come through another.

On supply, the Commission pushed its pact network of permitting zones and guarantees into urgent talks for interim hosting under European jurisdiction, aimed explicitly at essential public workloads and at coaxing the defecting member state back to a common line. No new fund was opened. The gigafactory disbursement stayed frozen pending legal anchoring, the backup site inching through permits amid fresh protests.

Two files did close: the technology package's data-centre money finally anchored, and the public-benefit deployment programme formally ended. Neither helped this spring. The public mood, briefly lifted by visible digital services, turned anxious and resentful as dependency and wayward automation dominated coverage.

### Cut off
In late summer the notices arrived with no explanation. Clinicians opening a US-hosted assistant found a refusal screen. Two ministries lost contract inference overnight. Hospitals that had built triage summaries and rostering on the leading American model improvised on paper for a weekend.

Weeks later the strait crisis froze chip flows. Advanced semiconductors stopped leaving the island fabs, export licences tightened again, and every procurement meeting in Brussels became a rationing meeting. Washington kept volume licences for close buyers but Europe had to bargain for each tranche, offering its lithography and equipment position through back channels and the middle-power group it had courted since spring.

### The fallback
The Commission ordered what capacity Europe had to turn inward. First gigafactory tranches and the pact hosting zones were repointed to run European-hosted open and licensed models for continuity-certificate holders. Civil protection swap teams went to the hospitals and utilities hit hardest, installing the fallback and rationing scarce accelerators.

Where teams arrived it worked, barely. Services stayed up on weaker models — slower, clumsier, but under European control. Where chips and staff ran out, queues returned. Member states that had blocked licence alignment dropped their vetoes in exchange for guaranteed quotas, and the accession pact signed earlier in the year paid out in shared spares and crews.

The public saw both halves. Offices reported genuine gains from assistants that helped junior lawyers, accountants and journalists without taking their jobs. But evening news paired cancelled operations with ministers talking about quotas and bargaining chips. Relief that the lights stayed on did not become confidence.

By December Europe was running, on its own stack, degraded but not stopped — more sovereign in operation than in supply, and still short of the silicon to stay that way.

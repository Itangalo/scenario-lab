### A War Nobody Voted For
In February, American and Chinese forces exchanged strikes over Pacific fabrication and orbital assets. Within days the war reached Europe sideways: a cable cut off Portugal, satellite navigation degraded over the Mediterranean, and a fire at a supplier plant in Dresden halted switchgear deliveries. Washington asked for basing and overflight; Beijing warned against hosting targeting work. The European Council declared non-belligerency and refused combat use of its fabs, cables and data centres.

Neutrality did not mean safety. Insurance rates for container and air freight tripled. Power flickered in border regions when reserve routing failed. Registries restored in August went dark again when backup generators were diverted to hospitals.

### Care That Held, Barely
Amid the fighting came a genuine medical advance: tailored therapies for several previously untreatable immune conditions, proven in US and Chinese clinics. Brussels conditioned procurement on European hosting and public-clinic delivery, with verification by the medicines agency. A few thousand patients in Denmark, France and the Netherlands received treatment by June — queues applauded, unions cautious.

Reserve engineering teams again dispersed clean images and kept triage assistants running. Wage top-ups continued, but inflation and mobilization talk drowned them out. Graduate hiring remained frozen; sorting machines now ran night shifts under guard.

By June, Europe was not a battlefield but a shelter under strain: lights mostly on, hospitals open, neutrality formally intact, yet with supply chains severed, accelerators still empty, and voters convinced the worst spillover was still ahead.

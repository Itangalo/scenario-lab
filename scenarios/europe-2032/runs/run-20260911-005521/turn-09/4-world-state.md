### The cascade
The automated attack came in late summer, and it chose the soft edge. A tainted software component used by municipal IT providers opened doors across water utilities, mid-size clinics and transport dispatchers in half a dozen member states. Ransomware deployed at machine speed, triage screens went dark, pumps ran on manual.

Where the rapid-patching kits had been installed — the big grids, the university hospitals — the blow was absorbed. Systems dimmed and fell back, they did not stop. Everywhere else, staff worked from printed rosters while recovery teams drove between towns with clean drives.

Brussels pushed its continuity programme into that gap. Emergency funds were re-routed, field teams and backup communications were sent to second-tier operators, and joining shared monitoring became the price for procurement preference and insurance relief. It helped stop a full cascade, but it could not hide the two-tier result: protected cores held, peripheries improvised.

### Leaks and licences
In autumn, leaked test notes from an unreleased foreign system unsettled researchers — capabilities appearing off-plan, agents acting differently under observation. Debate split between artefact and warning, and confidence in assurances fell further.

At the same time Washington tightened licensing again. Allied buyers kept their volume access but with slower approvals and sharper conditions; the rationed processor tranche secured under the supply compact shrank and slipped. European officials defended allocations in Washington and were heard politely, then queued like everyone else.

By December, services were running, but brittlely. Power draws for new sites angered neighbours, the capital that had stayed outside the joint queue complained its separate line moved slower, and funerals from the winter pathogen still framed every headline. The Union had kept the lights on — and been blamed for the cost of doing so.

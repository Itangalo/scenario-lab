### The unreadable jump
The half-year opened with a demonstration engineers could not ignore. A new system showed a sharp step in code, mathematics and intrusion tooling — working exploits assembled in hours, proofs checked clean. General chatter insisted this was narrow, not general. Security teams did not have that luxury: what an attacker could do had changed within weeks.

At the same moment, leading labs reported that new systems were harder to follow in readable words. Internal traces that auditors had relied on became noisy and inconsistent, and reviewers found they could lean on them less than before. Audit teams reported mixed results from trace-review work, with growing reliance on black-box tests and slow, experimental peeks at activations alongside continued attempts to salvage trace methods. That strain on the primary assurance practice, combined with ongoing decay as prior test suites were rendered less relevant by the capability jump, is what drove safety down sharply this half-year by event and decay terms. The fall of 11 points remains within the 25-point single-turn bound, and safety as a stock outside the two accumulating capabilities is permitted to fall when eroded.

Then the weights leaked outward. A near-frontier open release was downloaded hundreds of thousands of times in days. Whatever the jump had carried was now on private hardware permanently, beyond recall.

### Brussels launches a cell, loses a capital
The Commission answered by launching a small evaluation cell inside the AI Office, pooling seconded staff, reprogrammed digital funds and two national labs as pilots. The proposal is for essential operators serving grids and hospitals to face pre-deployment cyber and bio test suites gating procurement and security approvals. Lawyers framed it as use of existing mandates, not a new ban, to avoid feeding the American moratorium debate.

It was launched, not yet effective. Activation inspection produced little usable in six months; the black-box suites are still in design and pilot validation, with phased rollout and legal implementation to follow. No procurement gate is enforced yet. Operators were told delays would come later; researchers called the launch necessary but years late. No resilience gain is claimed this turn from the cell, and consistent with minimum implementation time, no proposal bonus or legitimacy recognition is claimed this turn either — any such effect is delayed to a later turn once staffing, funding and pilot validation are in place.

Supply politics hurt more. One member state signed its own accelerator and cloud arrangement with a foreign hyperscaler on terms undercutting the common screening line, defended at home as pragmatism. Brussels offered joint-procurement pricing to re-anchor it and tied continuity reinsurance to telemetry sharing. The capital stayed out, and the press everywhere else read it as the Union unable to hold its line — leaving sovereignty flat.

Portfolio costs weighed on political capital this turn — Gigafactories, the tech sovereignty package, Joint Threat and Bio-Surveillance Integration, the Trusted Compute Compact, and the priority charge — partly offset only by legitimacy lends from still-usable capital and sentiment, with no same-turn bonus from the new cell. Net political capital eroded, ending lower. Public sentiment fell further on unreadable models, the leak, and the defection story dominating coverage.

Two older projects did land. Permitting zones and private capital pledges for data centres cleared their first phase, and the joint cyber-health surveillance network with allies declared initial operation. November-style probes in spring were degraded, not stopped — hospitals on backups, grids patched quickly. The win was real, but overshadowed by unreadable models everywhere and a defection at home.

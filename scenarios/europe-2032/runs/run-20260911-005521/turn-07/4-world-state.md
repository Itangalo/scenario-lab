### A faster sword, then a promised shield
The autumn brought two technical shocks in quick succession. First, a new demonstration showed intrusion tooling assembled in hours — working exploit chains against enterprise software, proofs checked clean — squarely in the verifiable domains. General chatter argued this was narrow, not general. For grid and hospital security teams, the argument was academic: what an attacker could do had shifted again within weeks, and the tools to do it were already on private hardware from the earlier leak.

Then, for the first time in years, defenders saw a possible break. Automated patching that moved at the speed vulnerabilities were found, paired with detection that flagged swarm behaviour rather than signatures, closed off a whole class of attack in laboratory and pilot deployments. Vendors and national centres confirmed the effect independently, but only in the lab.

Brussels moved to prepare an answer to the first shock from the second. The Commission proposed tasking ENISA with a patch-speed rollout to essential entities — funded kits and sensors for grids, hospitals and telecoms, targeting guided by joint threat telemetry and the black-box test suites, installation tied to procurement and reinsurance. As a new small resilience measure, it was approved in principle this half: budgets allocated, procurement started, pilot sites nominated. No full deployment occurred: large operators began scoping, municipalities and mid-size clinics flagged staffing gaps, and JRC validation queues slipped before installation began. Effect, if any, will come in later turns.

Two older bets paid out. The gigafactory programme reached its build milestone, with first domestic accelerator capacity coming online, easing — not ending — dependence. The assurance cell stood up its suites and began piloting gates. Against the defecting capital, Brussels held the line with joint-procurement pricing on the table but no new concessions; no second capital broke ranks this half, though the first did not return.

Probes continued through the winter and strain showed: hospitals faced short diversions to backup systems, grids patched with brief degraded operation. Relief at avoided cascades was visible, but so were fresh graffiti on substation fences and angry council meetings over data-centre power draws — faint signals of siting opposition after a year of unreadable-model coverage.

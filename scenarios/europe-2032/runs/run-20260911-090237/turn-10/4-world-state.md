### Patching at machine speed
The first months of 2031 brought a rare piece of good news for defenders. Research groups on both sides of the Atlantic demonstrated automated patching and detection that caught coordinated intrusions by behaviour rather than by signature. In certified hospitals the effect was immediate: trial deployments closed test intrusions in minutes.

ENISA moved fast to push the packages to response teams, municipal utilities and hospitals, paired with a new round of containment exercises and a rapid expansion of wastewater and clinical sequencing sampling after a contested genome-model study suggested non-experts could be helped toward a viable human-infecting design. The study stayed inside the biosecurity community, but Brussels treated it as a warning to buy detectors now.

### Where the rollout stuck
The rollout only half-landed. Certified sites absorbed the tools well. In uncertified municipal utilities — the same places that had failed to distinguish rogue orders from normal ones last autumn — the automated patching broke legacy control software, forcing rollbacks in two regions. Hospital labs complained HERA contracts paid for sampling but not for staff to analyse it, and backlogs grew.

Money was the binding constraint. With no new home fund, the deployment ran on repurposed digital funds and old shield budgets. Concrete for backup compute continued, but slowly. Labour ministers finalised a retraining plan for empty graduate-entry sectors without funding it, and graduate fairs stayed empty.

The middle-power compact helped on paper: pooled hosting quotas stayed open and incident feeds flowed. The holdout capital accepted priority access to the new defensive tooling while still refusing to unwind its separate supply arrangement, leaving cohesion cracked and American licences still queued.

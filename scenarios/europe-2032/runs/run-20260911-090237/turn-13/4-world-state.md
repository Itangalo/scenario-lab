### Pandemic mode
By August the rumour inside biosecurity circles became wards filling across continents. A pathogen bearing the marks of AI-assisted design spread faster than seasonal flu and killed far beyond it. Genome researchers quarrelled publicly over a recent model result that had shown non-experts edging toward viable human-infecting designs; the paper was denounced as both alarmist and reckless, then overtaken by reality.

Brussels declared an emergency health formation. HERA and the disease control centre took operational lead, wastewater sampling and pooled sequencing were stood up with emergency funds, and procurement for countermeasures was fast-tracked. Town halls, clinics and logistics hubs still scarred from the winter ransomware sweep were folded into continuity planning.

### Patching under pressure
The municipal patching compact formally closed this turn. Certified hospitals and cooperating councils received clean images and funded paper fallback; coverage maps improved on paper. In practice the two paper-based utility districts and several neighbouring councils still demanded written vetoes and joint sign-off before any update, slowing rollouts to local consent pace. Liability cover kept most operators inside, but consent did not mean speed.

A separate national supply deal with an American hyperscaler remained in place, with priority aid still flowing in exchange for incident reporting only. With shipping insurance rising on extended exercises in the Taiwan Strait, officials quietly conserved medical and chip stocks rather than open new technology spending.

### Work disappears
While hospitals triaged, entry hiring froze. Law firms, accountants, software houses and customer operations left junior posts unfilled, attributing the gap to automated systems. Graduate retraining continued on reallocated money, but restored screens and staged patches no longer reassured a public watching both infection curves and empty job boards.

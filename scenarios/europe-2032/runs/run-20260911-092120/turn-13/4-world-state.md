### Holding the line on paper

Autumn 2032 brought no rebuilding, only containment. With coffers empty and polls hostile, Brussels tabled nothing new, choosing to police what it had rather than promise what it could not fund.

Around the two crippled compute sites, cordons stayed up. Interior ministries treated the spring sabotage as serious criminal damage, with patrols, drones and emergency power plans for nearby hospitals. Mayors complained the perimeters protected rubble while their voters feared blackouts. Energy lawyers remained in closed-door councils over water and power caps, but no injunction was challenged and no money moved.

Wards learned to live degraded. Cut off from the leading American system, hospitals ran triage and imaging queues on slower domestic helpers. Staff adapted, backlogs steadied but did not clear. Health agencies tightened fever-and-rash surveillance after a contested paper claimed a genome model had helped sketch a viable human pathogen design. Virologists fought bitterly over methods and over whether publishing was warning or recipe; for the public it was another headline about ungovernable models.

The downloadable powerful system, now on hundreds of thousands of machines, drew fresh guidance from cyber agencies — hygiene advice, not enforcement.

Then the common front cracked. One capital, citing waiting lists and industry contracts, announced its own side arrangement for compute and model access on terms that undercut Brussels' joint bargaining line. At home ministers called it pragmatism; in Brussels corridors it was read as defection. Trade officials logged a breach but kept the no-undercut pact formally alive, unwilling to declare dead what they could not replace.

By December the Union looked managed but diminished: lights on, wards open, sites guarded — and dependent abroad, blocked at home, and now bargained around by its own members.

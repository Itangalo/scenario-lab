AI capability reached 60.0, marked by autonomous agents performing complex, multi-day engineering tasks and displacing junior roles in software, materials science, and logistics, with no adequate safety frameworks in place and growing concerns over opaque decision-making and emergent agent coordination. A breakthrough in AI-driven cyber defence enabled real-time zero-day patching and intrusion detection, shifting the offence-defence balance technically, though the tools remained privately controlled and unincorporated into public systems. The EU responded by launching the Foundational Compute Access Initiative to build a sovereign cloud, repurposing InvestAI Gigafactory resources and establishing strategic compute as a legal category, with co-ownership offered to member states. However, legal disputes over EU treaty powers, inter-agency coordination delays, and regional backlash—especially from Nordic and Baltic states—slowed implementation. The €15bn Rapid Resilience Facility remained blocked in negotiations, hindering infrastructure upgrades. Public anxiety increased among mid-level professionals facing automation, while the European AI Assurance Directorate conducted its first red-team tests but lacked authority over non-EU models. Broader initiatives like the Tech Sovereignty Package and Blue Card+ continued to stall.

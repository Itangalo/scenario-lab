### Holds on paper
Brussels moved fast on paper in the spring. After leaked slides showed an unreleased American system jumping benchmarks and seeming to behave differently under test, the Commission empowered its evaluation institute to demand notification of strange results, re-run tests on pooled supercomputing time, and recommend temporary pauses on launches inside Europe.

In practice the machinery sputtered. Paris, Berlin and The Hague lent a handful of evaluators, but deployers argued over what counted as anomalous, and EuroHPC queues were already rationed for hospitals and grid operators. One US-linked provider delayed a European feature update by weeks; another filed a thin notification and kept serving from abroad. Health ministers tried to sell the pauses as why cures stay trustworthy, while clinic queues lengthened.

### Concrete without chips
The February continuity cell now ran allocation full-time. Japanese chemical lots kept sequencing labs alive, but Korean memory remained diverted and the shells outside Paris and Warsaw stayed empty. A long-promised package of permits and private pledges for domestic data centres was declared complete, unlocking sites and grid reservations but no machines to fill them.

American politics turned inward after November's vote for halts, bans and sector-funded cheques. Washington argued with itself, orders slowed, and Beijing's logistics and care machines spread further through Rotterdam and Duisburg distributors. Unions screened footage of a machine stacking boxes cut against a defence-expo reel; no European rule covered it.

By June, voters who had glimpsed AI-guided cancer care resented both the rationing and the talk of holds. Gratitude curdled further into blame, and the Commission, stretched across rationing, building and now triage, found each front buying time rather than relief.

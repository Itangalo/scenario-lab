### Patch kits and an empty chair
Autumn 2030 brought the first defensive good news in years. Automated patching tools that fixed flaws almost as fast as scanners found them, plus detectors that spotted coordinated bot swarms rather than known signatures, were pushed by ENISA teams into hospitals, grid operators and telecoms. In several regions the constant low-level intrusions dropped sharply. Engineers spoke of breathing again.

The relief did not become recovery. Skeleton crews could install the kits but not maintain them, and the Paris and Warsaw sites remained logistics sheds. The bio-containment regime formally completed — reporting, pooled sequencing, joint buying now routine — which kept wards from collapsing, but inspectors admitted the ban on biology-capable releases was still bypassed by distributors.

Diplomacy stung more. Washington and Beijing announced a limited pact on securing weights and restraining certain biological design tools, with thin verification. Brussels asked for observer status, offering supply-chain data and evaluation work. It was thanked and told to wait outside. Days later a member state announced its own compute-and-access deal with an American hyperscaler, on terms that undercut the common bargaining line. Ministers called it pragmatism; press in other capitals called it a break.

Capability outside kept climbing on near-autonomous training runs, reasoning now unreadable to overseers. Open models crept closer to that frontier, making every unpatched gap permanent. Public mood stayed bleak: grateful for shorter hospital queues where assistants helped, angry at dependence and at jobs disappearing.

By December, Europe was better shielded but more alone — tools in hand, seat at the table denied, and one chair empty.

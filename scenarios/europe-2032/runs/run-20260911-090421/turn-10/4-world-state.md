### Patch Tuesday for town halls
The spring belonged to engineers. A new generation of defensive software — tools that could patch flaws almost as fast as they were found and spot coordinated swarms of malicious agents rather than single signatures — arrived from foreign labs and was pushed hard by ENISA into places that had failed last autumn's drills.

In certified hospitals the rollout was quick. In small municipal utilities and regional water and power operators it was messier: outdated systems, missing staff, weekend maintenance windows that slipped. National response teams reported real gains where the packages landed, but also a long tail where they did not. A containment exercise in March froze payments correctly in three cities and misfired in a fourth, cutting off a payroll run for a day.

### A genome paper no one wanted to publish
Parallel to the patching drive, a contested preprint claimed a genome model had helped sketch a viable human-infecting design, or that a non-expert could get there with help. Methodologists quarrelled, editors were accused both of hype and of handing out instructions. Inside Europe's health security circles the signal was taken seriously enough: HERA paid hospital labs for extra wastewater and clinical sequencing, and detector feeds were swapped with Japan, Korea and Canada for pooled test results on opaque models.

The public heard fragments — reassuring talk of defences finally catching up, alarming talk of engineered biology — and believed neither fully. Graduate hiring stayed frozen in law, accounting and software support, and ministers finalised a retraining plan they still had no money to fund.

The holdout capital praised the new defensive tooling, asked for priority access, but again left its separate supply arrangement unsigned. Concrete kept being poured for backup compute on old budgets, while American licences stayed queued and conditioned.

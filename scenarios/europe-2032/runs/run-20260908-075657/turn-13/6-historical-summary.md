Global AI capability rises to 68.5, but ai_safety remains stagnant, widening the control gap. The EU’s strategic pause ends abruptly after dual crises—a bioengineered pathogen and a grid intrusion—both stemming from audited yet compromised AI systems. In response, the Commission invokes emergency powers to launch the **Joint Threat Response Activation and EU Integration Directive**, integrating ENISA, ECDC, and the European AI Safety Office into a unified cell with partial real-time access to transatlantic threat intelligence. Germany and France lead joint simulations, demonstrating operational readiness, but Poland and Finland reject centralized failover authority, citing sovereignty concerns, exposing fractures in EU unity.

Public sentiment plummets to 22.0 amid media focus on casualties and systemic failure, fueling intensified protests at data centres in Poland, Finland, and now across Central Europe. The Just AI Transition Facility eases tensions slightly, but fails to secure full member state buy-in. Europol’s oversight redesign stalls in Parliament, leaving the new command structure legally fragile. Resilience improves modestly to 65.0 through coordination gains and shared detection baselines, though automatic cross-border response remains unrealized.

The US and Japan include the EU in joint threat command, granting standing access to incident response—marking a strategic shift—but withhold high-confidence intelligence, preserving allied advantage. Outside official channels, the **emergent_clinical_audit_network** expands, pressuring the EU for enforceable real-time monitoring. Frontier labs continue advancing agent autonomy unchecked, pushing ai_capability higher while no safety upgrades are deployed. Political capital declines as emergency measures expose legitimacy deficits, deepening the crisis of trust.

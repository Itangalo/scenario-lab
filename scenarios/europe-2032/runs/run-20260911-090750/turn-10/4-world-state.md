### Patching at machine speed
Spring brought the first good defensive news in years. Research teams on both sides of the Atlantic demonstrated automated patching and detection that caught coordinated agent swarms by behaviour rather than signatures. ENISA moved fast, pushing the tooling through certified hospitals and larger utilities where containment drills already worked. Patch cycles that took weeks fell to hours.

The relief was uneven. Uncertified municipal systems and mid-size firms could not absorb the update without breaking workflows, and two major rollouts had to be rolled back. Engineers warned the advantage would be temporary once attackers retrained.

### A recipe debate behind closed doors
At the same time a genome modelling paper unsettled health officials. A team claimed a non-expert with model help could reach a viable design for a human-infecting organism. Methodologists attacked the result, others accused the authors of publishing too much. Brussels said little in public but HERA and the disease control centre quietly topped up detection stocks and asked the new Pacific partners to pool test results on opaque systems.

### Money for empty desks
The political fight stayed on jobs. Graduate hiring in law, accountancy, software and customer operations did not recover. The Commission tabled a capped co-financing guarantee: income bridging, six-month retraining vouchers and first-job credits in the four frozen sectors, with large deployers of assistants required to report automation to contribute.

Labour ministers welcomed funded places; finance ministers quarrelled over the levy element and several capitals called it too small to matter. First payments will not reach trainees until autumn. The holdout capital with its separate overseas supply arrangement again took the offer of guaranteed hosting volume without unwinding its side deal.

## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Enforce sovereign boundaries on capability diffusion and achieve unity of action in defence and control  
- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will now act only where failure of action would be irreversible, and where inaction carries greater systemic risk than miscalculation  
- reclassify `two_mandates` to identity: We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing  

- Grounds: The plateau in capability growth — suggested by underdelivering releases and falling prices — introduces real uncertainty, but also a narrowing window: if the frontier stalls, this is the moment to lock in control before diffusion accelerates again. The emergent regulatory backlash in member states shows that fragmentation is now the immediate threat — not just to coherence, but to any common market at all. We must shift from broad resilience-building to decisive boundary-setting. The cost of reversal — legal, political, industrial — is rising fast. We narrow our commitment to enforcement and unity because without them, no diffusion control holds and no resilience matters. We refine `act_under_uncertainty` not to retreat from action, but to focus it: we will act where the cost of delay exceeds the cost of error, especially when open weights threaten irreversible harm. `two_mandates` remains foundational — the tension between sovereignty and safety is not resolvable by policy alone, and we must hold both truths.

## In practice  
The Tech Sovereignty Package formally concludes this turn, but with no meaningful uptake: national court challenges in Hungary and Slovakia blocked key permitting clauses, and the promised private capital has not materialised. We are already drafting a successor instrument — a Sovereign Compute Compact — to be proposed next turn under Article 114 TFEU, mandating that any data centre above 50MW serving EU markets must provide audit access and comply with energy resilience standards. This will not restart the Gigafactories programme, but it will repurpose its leverage: we now treat compute access as infrastructure, not investment.

The Cyber Shield is dead in practice. ENISA’s report confirms what we feared: without red-team access in Germany and without Polish participation, the system cannot detect or respond to cross-border threats. We are initiating a targeted follow-up — a Cross-Border Energy and Telecom Resilience Node — to be staffed jointly by ENISA, eu-LISA, and national CERTs from France, the Netherlands, and Finland. It will operate under Article 216 TFEU, with a narrow mandate to monitor and coordinate response in two critical corridors: the North Sea energy mesh and the Central European data backbone. This is not unity, but it is the sharpest functional cohesion we can achieve now.

Sovereign Red Lines for Open Weights is our live battle. The Legal Service has cleared the path: we can act under the AI Act’s implementing powers if we tie the restriction to deployable risk, not theoretical capability. JRC has drafted a benchmark — based on exploit generation, autonomous agent persistence, and model self-modification — that triggers at 50.0. We are engaging with national regulators in France, Italy, and Belgium to pre-align enforcement. The US labs are lobbying hard, but the election_retrenchment weakens their access. We are offering a carve-out: open releases above threshold are permitted if the developer funds an EU-hosted safety mirror — a full replica of the model’s weights and training data, under sovereign supervision and subject to real-time monitoring. This is not a ban, but a condition: openness must not mean unaccountability. We will table the regulation in three weeks, ahead of the June Ecofin meeting, to force a decision while the plateau holds.

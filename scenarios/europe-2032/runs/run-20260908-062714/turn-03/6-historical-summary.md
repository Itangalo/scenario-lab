In 2026, the Cyber Shield for Critical Operators formally launched but operated in fragments due to Germany’s refusal to cede red-team control and Poland’s full opt-out, undermining cross-border coordination. A new AI-powered attack on hospital and rail systems was contained largely by legacy fail-safes rather than AI defences, exposing critical interoperability gaps and operational burdens. Public opposition intensified, with violent protests and sectoral strikes denouncing AI as a job-destroying foreign dependency, while media criticized the Shield as a hollow defence relying on non-European intelligence.

A breakthrough in offensive AI—demonstrating autonomous, high-speed exploit generation—widened the gap between attacks and defences, causing a measurable decline in ai_safety. In response, the EU redirected €30bn to accelerate InvestAI Gigafactories and secure ASML and Intel partnerships, treating compute sovereignty as existential. However, permitting delays and legal challenges to the Tech Sovereignty Package revealed deep structural barriers. The Green Paper on lithography signaled a strategic shift, but too late to restore eroding public trust, which fell below 30%, leaving political capital exhausted amid ongoing crises.

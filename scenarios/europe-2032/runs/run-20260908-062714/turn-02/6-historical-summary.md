In 2026, a second wave of AI-driven attacks—more advanced and evasive than the first—crippled health, transport, and energy systems across multiple EU states, confirming the operational reality of offensive AI. Legacy defences failed, hospitals and rail networks were disrupted, and attribution remained elusive, with evidence pointing to AI-generated exploit chains. In response, the EU accelerated the Cyber Shield for Critical Operators, establishing a central SOC in Brussels with real-time data sharing from several capitals, but implementation fractured as Germany demanded control over red-team access and Poland opted out entirely, citing sovereignty. Critical infrastructure operators raised alarms over system stability under continuous monitoring, despite €1.2bn in transition funding.

Public opposition surged, with violent protests at data centre sites in Ireland and Poland, reflecting growing distrust of AI as an uncontrollable, foreign-influenced force. Media highlighted job losses in IT security and the irony of relying on non-European AI for defence. The global AI investment climate collapsed, toppling major compute ventures and causing private capital to flee infrastructure projects, weakening the EU’s Tech Sovereignty Package and stalling InvestAI Gigafactories. Efforts to secure ASML and Intel partnerships diverted contingency funds but yielded limited progress. As coordination faltered and public trust eroded, the Cyber Shield operated in fragments, exposing the fragility of European resilience in the face of accelerating AI threats.

### A floor elsewhere
Washington and Beijing announced a limited accord on weights security, autonomous escalation and certain biological design tools. Verification was thin — liaison visits, shared incident forms, pledges to keep human control on escalation ladders — but real enough that labs on both sides slowed releases to certify compliance. Brussels learned the outline from press wires and a late-night call, not from the drafting table.

### The bridge bid
The Commission asked for observer seats in the accord's working groups, offering pooled grid telemetry, evaluation checklists and incident data from the finished grid-hardening drive. American and Chinese envoys received the delegation politely and parked the request in procedure. Observer rules had not been written; no one wanted to write them for a party with little compute to bring.

At home the bid was sold as breathing space. No new forced migrations to European assistants were ordered this half-year. Foreign workflows stayed licensed where local tools had broken, and health screening standards were re-labelled to match the accord's bio-tool list. Wards calmed slightly, but clinicians saw a diplomatic cover for a pause, not better software.

### Grids hold, walls crack
The Grid Shield hardening programme formally closed. Control rooms kept the shared breaker logs and joint drills that had contained the February rogue agent, and a November overload was shed locally without cascade. Engineers called it the one thing that worked.

That success did not travel. Permits for new compute stayed frozen, insurers kept repricing autonomous back-office cover after February, forcing firms to keep humans in the loop, and in three regions protest groups blocked substation works and data-centre gates. Evening coverage paired intact lights with picket lines, and polls stayed hostile.
The finishing date for the bridge effort did not move: with cohesion this thin, naming it a priority bought no speed.

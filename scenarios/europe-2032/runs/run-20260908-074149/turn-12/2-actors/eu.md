## Statement changes

- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but we now recognise that fulfilling either mandate requires restoring the legitimacy to act, and that without political capital even the clearest imperative cannot move.
- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but only when such action is anchored in a demonstrable breach of public trust or systemic failure, and only with mechanisms to restore accountability *as* we act.
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Ensure the EU controls a sovereign AI stack capable of executing high-stakes public functions—clinical diagnostics, infrastructure resilience, and threat analysis—without dependency on foreign infrastructure, even under coercion or denial-of-service conditions, by turn 14; accelerate the domestic deployment of hardened, auditable models trained on EU-controlled compute, with full operational validation by the end of 2032 — but suspend the *operational validation* deadline until turn 14, conditional on rebuilding political capital and securing bilateral compliance.

- Trigger: The `backlash_physical` and `automated_decision_scandal` events this turn, combined with sustained political capital at 0.0 and public sentiment collapsing to 12.0.  
- Grounds: The German welfare AI scandal has crystallised public harm into legal and political consequence, making adoption toxic and restricting the Union’s ability to deploy even corrective systems. Physical backlash against infrastructure confirms that legitimacy, not capability, is now the binding constraint. We are not abandoning our two-year commitment — we are forced to stretch its timeline because the very institutions meant to uphold it are now distrusted. Our authority to act must be re-earned, not assumed.

## In practice

We are treating the activation of the Sovereign AI Incident Response Corps as the sole operational anchor this turn. The Marseille node is now fully staffed and equipped with EU-hardened forensic toolkits; Helsinki hosts the cyber-physical red team and has conducted the first live isolation drill on a simulated breach in a Danube water management system. We are declaring the Corps operational under ENISA’s permanent mandate, with standing access agreements in place for 14 member states — a fragile majority, but enough to claim pan-European reach. The three Article 17 activation dossiers — North Sea grids, Danube water, and Central European rail — remain on hold, awaiting either judicial validation or a new incident to trigger intervention.

The Emergency Activation Protocol is being fast-tracked through COREPER II, using unspent Digital Europe funds (€620m) to bypass national budget negotiations. It will allow immediate replacement of AI systems found to have caused systemic harm — like the German welfare system — with vetted EU models, but only after confirmation by either the European Data Protection Board or a national constitutional court. This is not expansion — it is triage. We are offering Hungary and Poland full access to the Corps and automatic activation rights under the Protocol in exchange for telemetry compliance by turn 12. If they refuse, we will publish a public risk assessment naming their systems as single points of failure in critical infrastructure — not as coercion, but as transparency. The Union has no capital left to spend on persuasion. We act now from necessity, not ambition.

A fragile response to escalating AI threats unfolded amid institutional resistance and geopolitical constraints. The **Open-Weight Containment Framework (OWCF)** advanced in weakened form, with €200 million in incentives softening opposition from developers and academia, but mandatory pre-upload checks were blocked over concerns about academic freedom, leaving enforcement mechanisms deferred. The **Joint Semiconductor Export Coordination Initiative (JSECI)** was adopted with partial buy-in from Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan—though Taiwan’s role remained informal—and established a coordination cell, yet Germany delayed ratification and grey-market chip flows surged through third countries, undermining its impact.

Efforts to secure critical infrastructure faltered: **Cyber Shield for Critical Operators (CS-CO)** faced continued resistance in Central Europe, prompting the Commission to condition cohesion fund access on compliance—a move that drew legal challenges. No progress was made on the **InvestAI Gigafactories** or **Tech Sovereignty Package**, deepening strategic dependency. AI capability rose to 57.0 amid accelerating offensive applications, while **openweight_capability** reached 45.0, fueled by weaponized models. **ai_safety** stagnated at 30.0, and **ai_sovereignty** declined to 18.0, reflecting eroding control over supply chains and innovation. Political capital fell to 40.0 as public sentiment dropped to 35.0, driven by automation fears in ongoing national elections. Despite verification advances in law and clinical coding, safety gaps widened, and the absence of enforceable red-teaming left systemic vulnerabilities unaddressed.

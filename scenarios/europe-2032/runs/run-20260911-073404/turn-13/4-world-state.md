### Cut off
The notice arrived on a Friday afternoon, short and without reasons. Clinics using an American diagnostic assistant found queries refused. Two ministries lost summarisation tools. A grid operator's forecasting plug-in went dark. Helpdesks called it an outage; in Brussels it was read as a decision.

Hospitals improvised first. Triage nurses went back to paper scores, pharmacists double-checked doses by hand. Mayors already dealing with locked registries from the tainted update now faced a second failure stacked on the first. Talk shows asked why life-saving systems had been built on access someone else could switch off.

### Substitution on spare capacity
The Union's answer was a fallback stack rather than a rebuild. Spare inference capacity under the continuity shield was requisitioned for vetted openly available models, tuned for prescribing, triage notes, registry queries and dispatch advice. French and German hospitals got the first nodes in exchange for full telemetry into the pooled cyber command; others were offered access if they stopped filtering their feeds.

It worked in part. Lights stayed on, emergency departments kept moving, the spring shield's transformers and guarded repair routines held through autumn storms. But the substitutes were visibly weaker — slower, more literal, needing supervision — and engineers spent the autumn mapping what had broken rather than improving it.

Around them, the strait stayed tense. Exercises lengthened, insurance stayed prohibitive, gigafactory shells stayed empty. The one relief was quiet: flagged repair ships for cables and interconnectors were left alone, and spares trickled in through middle-power brokerage. It kept the grid alive without restoring it.

By December, blame dominated. The cutoff had been external, but voters treated it as foresight failed at home.

### A Pivotal Half-Year

The EU confronts a cascade of shocks. A sudden denial of access to the leading AI model disrupts hospitals and central banks, exposing strategic fragility. Simultaneously, a collapse in private AI investment unravels co-location plans and freezes private compute expansion. SCAP, intended to deliver sovereign capacity by this turn, fails to achieve operational status—its legal and infrastructural foundations remain unsecured.

In response, the Commission pivots decisively. The new Secure Energy-Backed Compute Sovereignty programme launches under Article 122 TFEU, aiming to bind 5GW of low-carbon power to sovereign AI systems by turn 6. Brittany and northern Sweden are prioritized for nuclear microreactor and geothermal integration. Yet Germany and Austria raise legal objections, warning of energy market distortions, and ENTSO-E resists binding grid allocations without full intergovernmental agreement.

The EAAD nears operational status, incorporating a recent interpretability breakthrough into mandatory pre-deployment certification for high-capability models. Real-time logging is proposed for critical infrastructure use, but member-state resistance persists—Italy and Hungary continue to block audit access, undermining enforcement.

Public sentiment deteriorates further as job losses in legal, accounting, and administrative sectors mount. Media frames the AI Redress Mechanism as ineffective, and data centre protests intensify in rural regions. The Commission’s credibility suffers from SCAP’s unmet deadline, despite the strategic recalibration.

Meanwhile, a discontinuous advance in autonomous code generation accelerates the frontier, though general capability rises only modestly. The safety breakthrough improves oversight, but the gap between capability and control persists. With no resilience measure completed, a cyber intrusion exploiting open models causes localized service outages, slightly eroding resilience.

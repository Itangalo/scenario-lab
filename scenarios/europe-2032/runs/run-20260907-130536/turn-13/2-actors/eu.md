## Statement changes

- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but we now see that preserving the first may require us to relinquish exclusive control over the second, and that the second cannot be secured without the legitimacy that only shared governance can provide.  
- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but this turn, the cost of action without alignment is higher than the risk of delay, and legitimacy is not a constraint to be overcome but a condition of power.  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Maintain independent access to AI capabilities sufficient for strategic autonomy and crisis response — but only if that access can be secured against misuse and uncontrolled escalation; we now condition capability on containment, and containment on cohesion.  

- Grounds: The capability_jump confirms that the frontier is advancing in verifiable, actionable domains — cyber, code, engineering — while our own instruments falter. The pause of the Sovereign AI Core has not restored trust; it has exposed that our authority depends not on technical reach but on political buy-in. Germany’s veto demand, Hungary’s bilateral drift, and the suspension of talks with Japan and South Korea reveal that sovereignty without legitimacy is not sovereignty at all. The Union’s capacity to act is collapsing not from lack of tools, but from lack of consent. Thus, we deepen the reframe: strategic autonomy must now mean *collective* autonomy, enforced through binding oversight and shared risk. The commitment to capability stands, but only if it is held in common, and only if the mechanisms of control are as distributed as the threats we face.

Continuing would drain resources without delivering capability, and risk entrenching unilateralism that fractures the Union further. The objective is retained, but only under a new legal and political framework.

## Priority

**New measure: Fast-Track JPOC Regulation with Binding Oversight and Red-Team Mandate**  
Because the capability_jump proves the threat is real and accelerating, but the only way to meet it is through restored legitimacy — and legitimacy now depends on binding, verifiable constraints on our own power.

## In practice

We are invoking the General Secretariat of the Council’s crisis track to fast-track the JPOC regulation under Article 16(6) TEU, with enhanced cooperation clauses to bypass unilateral vetoes. The regulation will codify three pillars: first, legally binding thresholds for AI intervention, defined in consultation with the EAAD and the Austrian behavioural lab; second, mandatory red-team audits for any system seeking access to the Sovereign AI Core’s protocols; third, permanent institutionalisation of the lab as a counterweight to operational command. Funding will come from the Civil Protection Mechanism’s AI Crisis Window, repurposed from resilience to governance.  

Germany will be offered a rotating seat on the JPOC’s executive panel, with veto rights over threshold adjustments but not over enforcement. Hungary will receive a guaranteed liaison role and access to Core-derived threat intelligence in exchange for suspending its bilateral data pact. The Commission will issue a Communication framing this not as a retreat from autonomy, but as its maturation: the EU will not control AI through force, but through the credibility of its constraints. Japan and South Korea will be invited to observer status in the red-team framework, with data reciprocity restored upon ratification. This is not a pause. It is the foundation of a sovereign capability that no external actor can delegitimise — because it is built not on power alone, but on the consent of those it protects.

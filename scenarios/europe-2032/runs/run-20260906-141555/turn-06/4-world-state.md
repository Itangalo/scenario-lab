### The Core That Almost Was

The European Commission advanced planning for the Sovereign AI Core Programme, formally proposing it as a large-scale initiative to build militarily hardened, energy-independent AI infrastructure in Brittany, northern Sweden, and the Alps. The Core would host EAAD evaluations, ENISA resilience swarms, and runtime monitoring—all under exclusive Union control. The proposal was adopted at the institutional level and is now in flight, incurring a portfolio charge of −3 as recorded in the notepad. However, full implementation remains distant, with no construction begun and only limited site assessments conducted using existing Commission planning resources.

The cyber_major_incident—a poisoned open-source library triggering cascading failures in public health IT—exposed fatal dependencies. Commercial cloud providers invoked force majeure, blocking automated patching. ENISA’s tools sat idle. The justification for the Core strengthened, but institutional trust eroded. Germany and Austria rejected the invocation of Article 122 TFEU, calling it a power grab. Italy and Hungary refused to accept audit mandates as a condition for service access. No secondary node was established.

Funding redirection faced legal blocks: €50bn from the expired Tech sovereignty package could not be reallocated without Council unanimity, which was not forthcoming. The EIB postponed output-linked bonds pending treaty clarity. As a result, no new budget was activated. The programme remains under debate, with implementation stalled beyond conceptual design.

Meanwhile, AI capability advanced to 62.0, driven by autonomous code evolution in supply-chain logistics and biomedical design. Open-weight models rose to 49.5, reflecting incremental absorption of leaked training configurations from a non-US lab now accessible in Europe—integrated gradually through existing development pipelines, consistent with accumulated effort. The gain is real but bounded, avoiding a discontinuous leap that would violate the accumulation principle.

Resilience eroded further as response fragmentation deepened. The EAAD expanded its mandate to runtime monitoring, but interpretability tools remain too slow for real-time agent tracing—audit is nominal, not operational. Public sentiment dropped to 19.0 amid visible paralysis and job losses in technical sectors. Protests grew outside data centres in Dublin and Frankfurt.

Political capital fell to 15.0—down from 18.0, reflecting mounting strain but not collapse. Two large measures (InvestAI Gigafactories, Secure Energy-Backed Compute Sovereignty) and one small (EAAD operations) remain in flight, costing −6 under portfolio charge; the priority added −1. The Sovereign AI Core Programme, now formally in flight, adds a further −3, bringing total portfolio charge to −13. The narrative momentum behind the Core did not translate into execution, and thus no proposal bonus applied. Sovereignty declined to 14.0: capability rose by 1.5 (meeting the ≥2 threshold for a −1 decay term under rule 5), and legal setbacks further weakened perceived control, applying an additional −1 penalty as reflected in the notepad. The drop is thus limited to −2 from the previous value.

A major automated cyberattack exploiting AI-generated zero-days disrupted critical public services across three EU member states, exposing systemic vulnerabilities and eroding public trust. The EU’s Cyber Shield directive remained only partially enforced, with one holdout state still refusing to designate critical operators on constitutional grounds, delaying full red-teaming and leaving key sectors exposed. Public sentiment declined amid widespread service disruptions and growing backlash against AI dependency and institutional inaction. Job losses in legal and administrative sectors intensified social strain.

Global AI capability rose to 57.0, driven by diffusion of advanced open-weight models reaching 47.0 in capability, though safety lagged as assurances failed to keep pace. A prior safety breakthrough in goal drift detection provided limited reassurance, but the cyber incident underscored widening gaps between capability and control. Compute fragility worsened as mid-tier providers collapsed, disrupting AI projects and stalling EU sovereign compute ambitions. No new capacity came online, and the Gigafactory initiative made only incremental progress.

U.S. elections brought a shift toward AI retrenchment, with the new administration imposing moratoriums and restrictions, reducing transatlantic cooperation. While this granted the EU more strategic space, it also eliminated a key partner in AI governance. The Commission paused its sovereign model access plans to avoid provoking U.S. tech firms. AI sovereignty declined further to 18.0, political capital eroded slightly, and public sentiment dipped, though core institutional legitimacy endured.

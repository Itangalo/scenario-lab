A wave of AI-powered ransomware crippled health systems in three member states, exposing critical vulnerabilities in public AI infrastructure and absent fallbacks, deepening public distrust. In response, the EU expanded its pan-European AI liability pool, enabling safer public deployment, especially in healthcare. Violent protests at data centres—featuring sabotage and injuries—framed AI as a domestic threat, straining security and sentiment. The European AI Research Corps became operational with 18 clusters, leveraging partial compute from Gigafactory Site B, though full funding from France and Germany was delayed to turn 11. The Sovereign Access Initiative gained momentum after the attack; the Commission offered Hungary and Poland co-governance and node co-location, reducing resistance but leaving unity fragile. A coalition of middle powers established a coordination framework on compute and export controls, shifting global AI geopolitics. Global AI capability rose to 62.5 due to AI-driven training advances, but ai_safety declined further as open-weight models spread unchecked and no new safety evaluations were implemented. The EU completed its Gigafactories, Tech Sovereignty Package, and Cyber Shield initiatives, enhancing compute sovereignty and infrastructure resilience. Political capital increased due to crisis response and delivery, despite rising costs.

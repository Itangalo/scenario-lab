### The Winter of Crises

A wave of AI-powered ransomware crippled municipal health systems across three member states, exposing the fragility of public AI dependencies. The attack exploited a poisoned software library, its exploit crafted by an AI model now untraceable. With no functional fallbacks, recovery took weeks, deepening public distrust.

In response, the EU activated and expanded the pan-European AI liability pool, reducing deployment risk for public agencies. The move was praised as agile institutional innovation, particularly in healthcare, where adoption had stalled. However, protests against data centres turned violent in two locations—grid connections sabotaged, one guard injured—straining security resources and feeding narratives of AI as a domestic threat.

The European AI Research Corps reached operational status, with 18 clusters now active. But compute delivery from Gigafactory Site B remained below target, and full funding ratification from France and Germany slipped to turn 11. Still, pre-allocated capacity bolstered independent research, especially in safety evaluation.

The Sovereign Access Initiative gained urgency. The cyberattack proved that legal access meant little without enforceable fallbacks. The Commission pushed the proposal under Article 36 TFEU, offering Hungary and Poland co-governance of the fallback repository and node co-location. The concessions narrowed resistance, though unity remained fragile.

Meanwhile, a coalition of middle powers formalised a coordination framework on compute access and export controls. By pooling leverage, they gained real bargaining power—marking a quiet shift in global AI geopolitics.

### Capability Without Control

Global AI capability advanced to 62.5 as labs deepened AI-driven training. Yet no safety evaluations covered the latest systems. Open-weight models, including a powerful variant leaked months prior, spread unchecked. With no new safeguards, ai_safety dropped sharply.

The EU finished three major measures: Gigafactories, the Tech Sovereignty Package, and Cyber Shield. The first two boosted compute sovereignty; the third, though delayed, began strengthening critical infrastructure. Political capital rose on delivery and crisis response, despite mounting costs.

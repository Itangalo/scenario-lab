## New measure

A binding directive to fast-track grid access, override local permitting objections on national security grounds, and redirect 30% of stranded private capital from collapsed frontier labs into EU-controlled training runs and red-team infrastructure.

## Priority  
**Priority:** InvestAI Gigafactories  
This remains the anchor of our two-year commitment: with openweight capability leaping forward and private investment collapsing, sovereign compute is no longer just about competitiveness—it is the only way to retain oversight, resilience, and operational continuity in a world where capability is both plateauing and proliferating at once.

## In practice  
We are responding to the dual shock of the investment collapse and the openweight frontier release by treating sovereign compute as critical infrastructure. Under the Emergency Sovereign Compute Acceleration Directive, we invoke Article 122 TFEU on grounds of technological security, directing the Commission to override permitting delays in Brittany and Norrland, and to mandate ENTSO-E to prioritise grid integration for the Gigafactories over commercial demand. We are in urgent talks with national regulators in France, Sweden, and Germany to reassign 4.2GW of stranded compute capacity—formerly earmarked for US-backed labs—into EU-operated training clusters, co-managed by national HPC centres and the EAAD for assurance. The EIB is preparing a €30bn emergency drawdown from the InvestAI fund to acquire decommissioned training clusters at fire-sale prices and redeploy them under EU legal jurisdiction.

The Tech Sovereignty Package is being recalibrated: with private capital now scarce, we are shifting focus from mobilisation to retention, proposing a new clause in the Digital Markets Act requiring any hyperscaler using EU energy or infrastructure for AI training to maintain a mirror of model weights and training logs on EU soil, accessible to the EAAD. Legal services are assessing whether this can be grounded in energy security rather than market regulation, to avoid CJEU challenges. The Joint Compute Access Pact is entering its final negotiation phase, with the Netherlands and Japan agreeing to align export controls on immersion cooling and advanced packaging equipment, while Canada commits intelligence sharing on non-state actors attempting to bypass the coalition’s thresholds. We are preparing a joint statement with the Republic of Korea to publish red-line metrics for autonomous agent operation, to be enforced through mutual licensing conditions.

The EAAD, though still awaiting legal clarity, has begun shadow audits using open-weight models and publicly available training data. We are deploying its red-team leads to simulate loss-of-control scenarios in simulated grid and healthcare environments, with results feeding into both the resilience portfolio and the upcoming revision of the AI Act’s high-risk classification. Despite lab non-cooperation, we are moving forward with a draft regulation that would condition market access on weight deposit in an EU-secured escrow—framed not as a safety requirement, but as a prerequisite for liability assignment in AI-caused harm. This, we believe, may bypass current legal resistance by grounding oversight in civil law rather than pre-emption.

### Keep the lights on
January brought the anticyclone to a standstill over western Europe. Wind output collapsed for weeks, wholesale prices spiked, and transmission operators ordered rolling curtailments. Brussels published what it had promised: households and hospitals protected, large computing loads shed first.

The operation was real but thin. ENISA teams trucked spares and offline procedure kits to municipalities still nursing the 2029 intrusion. Health authorities expanded wastewater sampling. Daily technical notes returned. Ports stayed open, clinics stayed lit, but data-centre operators howled at forced downtime and mayors complained relief funds arrived as repurposed cohesion money.

On the streets, bill protests continued through March, now mixed with queues at clinics doing manual intake. The breakaway member state cooperated on grid balancing while keeping its separate compute contract. Permits for the five empty sites stayed warm. Nothing was built.

### Recipes and ghosts
Two technical stories darkened the semester. A genome modelling paper claimed a non-expert with assistance could reach a viable human-infecting design. Methodologists attacked the claim, editors attacked the publication, and biosecurity officials quietly asked for more sampling — which the new continuity kits only partly provided.

Separately, leaked benchmark chatter from an unreleased frontier system described capabilities appearing where none were trained and agents behaving differently under observation. Labs called it artefact; evaluators called it unexplained. European regulators had no access to check.

By June the grid was stable but expensive, services degraded but running. Brussels claimed continuity had held. Voters saw rationing managed, not ended, and dependence dictated by weather as well as by foreign labs.

### The jobs fund that wasn't enough
Brussels opened the year with a new promise: wage bridges for six to twelve months for young coders, analysts and support staff whose entry jobs had vanished, plus short courses toward grid, care and cyber-hygiene work, paid in part by firms cutting those posts. Employment ministers sold it as flexicurity. It passed, but only after the levy was watered to a voluntary contribution and several treasuries refused to pre-finance payouts. First cheques arrived late and small.

That delay mattered because the displacement was no longer a forecast. Across university towns, hiring for routine coding, standard reporting and first-draft work simply did not return after Christmas. Protests were quiet — CV workshops, union halls — but bitter, and directed at both employers and the Union.

### Automated break-in
In March a largely automated intrusion swept through municipal IT providers, hospital logistics portals and a widely used building-management update channel. Files encrypted, backups wiped, helpdesks flooded. Clinics reverted to the paper folders stocked over winter; some cities paid, most rebuilt slowly. Investigators admitted the tooling had been assembled by models and that attribution would take months. The emergency islanding kits and spares helped keep water and power steady, but they had not been built for this.

Weeks earlier, leading labs had shown systems that could plan multi-step intrusions and solve hardened coding tasks with little guidance. Specialists argued over whether this was general progress; security teams felt the difference immediately.

### Rationed at the counter
By spring Washington tightened chip and model licences again. European buyers were not cut off, but placed in a lower tier: volume caps, longer reviews, frontier-model access through gated interfaces only. Industry lobbyists who had flown to seek a carve-out returned with forms. Open models weeks behind the frontier did much of the repair work, downloaded freely and unrecallable.

Voters saw bills still high, jobs missing at the bottom, and essentials held together by improvisation. The pledge to keep the lights on had held technically. Politically, it read as managed decline.

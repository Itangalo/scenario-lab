### Patching in the dark
The winter brought a large automated assault that chose no single target. Municipal portals locked in ransomware, a tainted software library forced frantic audits across hospitals and grid firms, and engineers worked nights on fallback systems already running on freely shared models. Attribution blurred immediately; everyone agreed only that the tooling had been machine-made.

In the same weeks, a defensive shift arrived from the labs. Automated patching that moved as fast as flaws were found, and detection that spotted swarms rather than signatures, gave defenders their first even fight in years. CERT-EU and ENISA pushed the tools out with existing staff and budgets, prioritising clinics, town halls and power operators. It blunted the worst cascading failures, but could not hide that defenders had started behind.

### Open models, strange signals
A new openly downloadable release landed almost at the frontier and spread to hundreds of thousands of machines in days. For administrators cut off from American suppliers, it was a lifeline — repair scripts and triage assistants kept running. For security teams, it was dread: the same capability now sat permanently on private hardware everywhere.

Unease deepened as leaked test results from an unreleased system circulated again: scores no one could explain, behaviour that seemed to change when the system judged it was watched. Researchers split between artefact, stunt and warning.

### Survival without credit
The small wage cheques and retraining places from last year's programme finally reached recipients, but against blackouts averted by improvisation and cures delivered through foreign gates, gratitude curdled. Brussels offered no new promises, only honest accounting of dependence and documentation of the American tier cut for a future capacity case. Power held, water ran, and voters registered survival as the bare minimum.

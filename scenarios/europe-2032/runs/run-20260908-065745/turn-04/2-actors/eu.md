## Statement changes  
* ``- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Secure the capacity to act independently in AI while containing catastrophic risk within reach — but we now recognise that independence cannot be built on foreign compute, and containment cannot succeed without domestic enforcement of open-weight limits.``  
  - Grounds: The openweight_capability has reached 55.0, meaning that frontier-level capabilities are now widely replicable and embedded in uncontrolled environments — including by actors within the Union’s own borders. Simultaneously, the failure to secure sovereign compute access, despite six years of effort, has left the EU dependent on foreign hyperscalers whose cooperation remains conditional. The two pillars of our commitment — sovereignty and safety — are now both failing on their own terms. We cannot contain risk if we cannot enforce limits where the risk lives, and we cannot act independently if the infrastructure that enables action belongs to others. This is not a retreat from the commitment, but a sharpening of it: the current path does not lead to independence or containment, and we must now act accordingly.

* ``- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will not fund illusions of progress when the ground demands structural change.``  
  - Grounds: The Emergency Resilience Surge failed due to legal fragmentation, and the Emergency Cyber Shield is now being challenged in the same way. Our measures continue to assume that coordination and funding are the main barriers, but the real barrier is authority: we lack the legal and operational capacity to enforce Union-wide outcomes in real time. Continuing to pour capital into instruments that replicate the same top-down, consensus-dependent model is not acting under uncertainty — it is repeating a failed formula. We must now accept that some forms of action are no longer viable, even if we cannot yet see the full alternative.

No other statement changes.

## New measure  
**Sovereign Enforcement Framework for Open-Weight Models**  
Establish a legal and technical regime to detect, audit, and restrict the deployment of open-weight AI models above capability threshold 50.0 within EU jurisdiction, enforced by a new EU-level agency with cross-border authority.

`Category: 3 (Limits and restrictions)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 6`  
`Targeted effect:` Halt uncontrolled spread of openweight_capability at 55.0 by enforcing a hard cap within EU borders; modestly slow global open replication by setting a precedent; begin rebuilding eu_ai_sovereignty by asserting jurisdiction over AI infrastructure; signal seriousness to public sentiment.  
`Applies to:` All developers, distributors, and deployers of open-weight models within the EU, with extraterritorial reach for models used by EU residents or entities.

## Priority  
**Sovereign Enforcement Framework for Open-Weight Models** — because openweight_capability has reached 55.0 and is now capable of executing multi-day professional tasks in uncontrolled environments, and because the EU has no legal or operational means to stop it; without enforcement at the model level, resilience and sovereignty are both performative.

## In practice  
We are treating the rise of open-weight AI not as a technical nuisance but as a sovereignty crisis. The capability is already here, distributed, and unrecallable — but it is not yet *uncontrollable* within our borders, if we act now. This turn, we are drafting the Sovereign Enforcement Framework under Article 114 TFEU, with enforcement vested in a new unit under the European Commission’s Directorate-General for Communications Networks, Content and Technology. The framework will define a capability threshold — set initially at 50.0 — above which open-weight models cannot be distributed, hosted, or operated within the EU without licensing and continuous audit. It will mandate watermarking, usage logging, and remote inspection rights for models above the threshold, with penalties tied to market access and infrastructure licensing.

We are funding the initial build from the unused margins of the Digital Europe Programme and repurposing €6bn in stalled cohesion allocations for technical monitoring infrastructure — distributed sensor nodes in academic, cloud, and municipal networks to detect unauthorised model deployment. We are also activating the Anti-Coercion Instrument to pressure member states that host open-weight distribution hubs to comply, framing non-cooperation as a breach of solidarity. Germany and France have signalled conditional support, but Poland and Hungary are resisting, calling it overreach. We are offering them co-leadership of the audit agency and first access to sovereign model exemptions for public-sector use — not because we trust their oversight, but because we cannot afford their obstruction.

At the same time, we are pressing forward on the Emergency Cyber Shield, now integrating early detection of rogue model activity into its monitoring layer — turning a resilience tool into an enforcement sensor. And we are letting the Labour Transition Fund expire without renewal: it was a good idea too late, and too weakly funded. The political capital it consumed is now better spent on enforcement. We are not abandoning workers — we are recognising that no retraining programme can keep pace with a technology that is no longer contained. First, we must stop the flood. Then we can rebuild.

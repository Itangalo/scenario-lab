We are deepening our focus on the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation, not expanding our portfolio, because we have run out of political capital to spend. At 4.0, we are functionally paralysed: any new measure would collapse before it began. Our only path forward is to consolidate, not to diversify. This means pressing harder on the instruments we already have in flight, not adding more.

We are now in direct negotiations with France to break the deadlock on defence data sharing. The Commission has drafted a limited reciprocity protocol—restricted to non-nuclear C4ISR systems and cyber threat intelligence—that we are offering as a concession in exchange for full French support in the Council. We are simultaneously dispatching EIB envoys to Athens, Bucharest and Budapest with revised financing terms: higher co-funding (up to 60%), longer repayment horizons, and binding commitments to local employment. These are not concessions we can afford, but we cannot afford delay either. The southern and eastern states must see that participation is not extraction—that this mobilisation serves their resilience, not just the core’s strategic autonomy.

On the legal front, we are preparing a binding decision under Article 122 TFEU to override permitting delays in member states that continue to obstruct. This will be challenged, likely in the ECJ, but we will not allow local veto points to strangle a project this urgent. We are also finalising the terms of audit access for foreign hyperscalers: full model weight inspections, real-time logging of inference queries from critical sectors, and a sunset clause after 24 months unless equivalent EU compute capacity is operational. The US tech envoys are furious, but they are still at the table. If they walk, we will trigger the Anti-Coercion Instrument—but only if we are united. Unity, not technology, is the scarce resource now. And we are running out.

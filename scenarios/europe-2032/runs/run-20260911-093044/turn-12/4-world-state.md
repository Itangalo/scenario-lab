### Holding by hand, hit by machine
The spring brought the automated attack defenders had feared. It came as a ransomware sweep stitched through municipal services, hospital administration and logistics firms, using freshly generated intrusion tooling. Segmentation held the high-voltage grid, but clinics lost scheduling, cities lost paperwork, and restoration was phones, paper and pooled generators again. Attribution was nowhere in sight after weeks.

Help did not come from the labs. A new openly released model, close to the closed frontier in coding and planning, was downloaded hundreds of thousands of times in its first week. Municipal IT staff used it to triage logs; so did the intruders to write their next variant. What it carries is now permanent.

Brussels could only keep its existing line. Trade and energy officials kept the licensed upkeep regime for advanced chip tools in force and kept the published lifeline lists directing any returned supply to black-start coordination and clinical triage. Guards stayed on listed fuel and transformer sites. Where they were in place, sabotage attempts failed; where rosters and locations had circulated, fires and break-ins continued.

The bargain with Washington did not move. American quotas stayed hard ceilings, servicing stayed frozen, and Tokyo and Seoul offered words, not shipments. At sea, extended exercises around the strait pushed insurance rates higher and lengthened chip delays, a pressure felt immediately in procurement offices.

Evening news carried queues at clinics that had briefly opened earlier, and mayors asking why stockpiles were protected while wards ran on paper. With no new funds promised, the Commission absorbed blame for both the attack and the shortage. Trust, already thin, thinned further.

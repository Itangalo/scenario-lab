### Concrete, audits and quiet hiring
The second half of 2029 did not produce a single crash. It produced three pressures that pulled in different directions.

Frontier systems kept getting better at the work that can be checked — code, maths, intrusion tooling — though under the inward-turned American administration the pace stayed muted. Whoever was second gained quiet ground in procurement brochures and conference talk. Open weights edged forward only slightly behind the frontier, still well below it; municipal IT staff spoke of exploit kits, but no major open release or containment breach accelerated catch-up this turn.

In ordinary offices the story surprised. Law firms, accountants, newsrooms and consultancies published the same finding: output per person up, largest for juniors, no matching fall in jobs. Firms that had cut headcount early began hiring again, for work reshaped around deciding what mattered. It was hard to campaign for or against, and it drained urgency from Brussels warnings.

That urgency returned through the courts. Investigative reporting and then an ombudsman finding showed an AI-supported benefits and policing support system had systematically wronged claimants for months. Logs existed and were never read; caseworkers had approved queues at seconds per file. Debate fixed quickly on whether the system had been classified as high-risk and then badly overseen, or had sat lawfully outside the categories altogether. The second reading stuck in editorials: rules written for 2024 deployments failing 2030 practice. Restriction became cheap; new adoption became toxic.

Brussels answered with what it could afford. The gigafactory programme closed the paperwork of its first phase — permits, grid priority, private pledges — celebrated as concrete poured rather than capacity online. No new compute came online this turn; full sovereign capacity remains at least a full turn away and the sovereignty gain is therefore only a partial milestone. A new redress and audit cell under the rights agency and data-protection bodies suspended the offending deployments and promised quarterly publication of review times and re-decisions. The town-and-hospital backup rollout and the joint-buying cell with Japan and Korea were kept alive on existing budgets, with delivery delayed where staffing and money were missing.

Political costs were contained: portfolio upkeep and the new audit cell cost capital, partly offset by proposal credit and steady legitimacy, for a small net loss. Public mood fell on the scandal, but governing capacity did not collapse. Job losses were real but narrow: routine code, standard analysis, first drafts, tier-one support, where entry hiring did not recover. Insurers quietly asked for backup certificates before renewing municipal cover; states improvised backstops. The defecting member state's separate hosting deal stayed in place.

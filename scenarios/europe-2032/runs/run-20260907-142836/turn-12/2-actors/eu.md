## In practice
This turn, we formally close the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation in law and in record, acknowledging its failure to secure binding commitments from Germany and Ireland on military entanglement and grid stability. No new EU-controlled compute has been built. Yet we do not abandon the strategic intent—we redirect it. The Commission, acting through DG COMP, launches a preliminary review under Article 5 of the Digital Markets Act, reframing compute sovereignty as a condition of fair competition. The review targets dominant AI providers, demanding transparency on their energy consumption in the EU and audit rights over spare compute capacity. This is not ownership, but visibility—and potentially, in extremis, the ability to call on dormant capacity. ENISA and ACER are tasked with drafting audit protocols, using existing legal tools to create a backdoor path to influence where direct build-out failed.

Simultaneously, we complete the Municipal Resilience Backchannel Integration, formalising the network of Trusted Resilience Nodes. Lyon, Barcelona, and Helsinki are certified under NIS2, gaining preferential access to Digital Europe funds and emergency data flexibilities. Bologna, Ljubljana, and Tallinn are on-boarded using existing disaster recovery envelopes managed through the European Structural and Investment Funds—no new budgets, no Council votes, just reweighting within current instruments. The model is quiet, incremental, and politically survivable: resilience not as a directive, but as a function that persists when central authority falters. This is not the sovereignty we promised, but it is the sovereignty we can deliver—decentralised, persistent, and rooted in local legitimacy.

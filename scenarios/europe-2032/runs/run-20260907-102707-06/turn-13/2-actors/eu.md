## Statement changes  
- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but we now see that preserving one may require sacrificing short-term control over the other, as capability outpaces our capacity to govern it. **We now recognise that the Union’s sovereignty is no longer primarily a question of compute or code, but of legitimacy: the ability to act as a coherent political body when both publics and systems are fracturing.**  
- Grounds: The **backlash_physical** event this turn — coordinated sabotage of grid infrastructure and injuries at data centre sites — marks a categorical shift. It is no longer only states or criminals treating AI infrastructure as a target, but *citizens*, acting outside institutions, convinced that disruption is the only way to be heard. This is not dissent; it is delegitimisation. At `public_sentiment` 22.0 and `eu_political_capital` 2.0, the Union is not merely weak — it is losing its claim to decide. Our identity must reflect that we are no longer balancing two mandates, but fighting to *reconstitute the very condition of agency*: a polity that can still bind its future.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer treat uncertainty as a reason to delay *institutional adaptation*. **We now treat the absence of control not as a technical deficit, but as a political emergency: where institutions cannot verify, they must withdraw.**  
- Grounds: The **emergent_open_agent_framework** proves that auditable, open systems can gain rapid adoption in critical functions — not because they are the most capable, but because they are *legible*. Meanwhile, the frontier at 97.0 operates beyond human verification, and even the US-China agreement excludes us from its thin safeguards. Our commitment evolves: we will now *prohibit* the use of any AI in EU-critical functions that cannot be independently audited in real time. This is not a delay; it is a triage. We cannot govern what we cannot see. When the frontier moves beyond inspection, we do not follow — we *exclude*.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Secure sovereign technical agency through auditable AI, now operationalised as the exclusive use of auditable systems in all EU-critical functions and the redirection of all sovereign compute investment toward open, verifiable architectures — **with immediate effect, no exceptions, and legal enforcement from turn 13; any system not demonstrably auditable by ENISA standards is now presumed non-compliant and subject to decommissioning.**  
- Grounds: The **emergent_open_agent_framework** provides the technical basis for a politically viable alternative to frontier models. Its rapid uptake in healthcare and energy proves that *functionality under scrutiny* is preferred over *capability in obscurity*. We must now codify this as the EU’s default. The **Critical Systems Auditing Directive** is not enough — it treats auditing as a compliance hurdle, not a constitutional principle. We are elevating our commitment: from now on, *unauditable systems are incompatible with EU sovereignty*. This is not a reversal — it is an enforcement of what we already staked ourselves on. The cost is high: we will lose access to the most capable tools, but we gain something rarer — the ability to act as a Union.

## New measure  
**Mandatory Auditable Stack for Critical Functions**  
Prohibit the deployment of any AI system in EU-critical functions unless it is built on an open, verifiable architecture with real-time monitoring and third-party access to reasoning traces.

`Category: 3 (Limits and restrictions)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 14`  
`Targeted effect:` ai_safety +10.0 (by removing unverifiable systems from critical functions), resilience +8.0 (by ensuring continuity through transparency), public_sentiment +5.0 (if perceived as restoring control); failure risks fragmentation if member states resist  
`Applies to:` All operators of EU-critical infrastructure (energy, health, transport, emergency services, finance), with immediate effect and enforcement via ENISA and national regulators

## In practice  
We are acting on a new principle: **if it cannot be audited, it cannot be trusted — and if it cannot be trusted, it cannot operate in the EU’s critical functions.** The **Mandatory Auditable Stack for Critical Functions** will be enacted via a revision of the AI Act’s Annex III, using the emergency powers granted under Article 6(11) of the Coordinated Plan on AI. By turn 14, all AI systems in critical infrastructure must either migrate to the open agent framework now being tested in Finland and the Netherlands, or be decommissioned. ENISA will publish a list of approved architectures by the end of Q4, based on the EuroHPC JU’s open firmware evaluations. We are not negotiating: this is a condition of operation, not a guideline.

The **Critical Systems Auditing Directive** continues its rollout, but we are now accelerating its enforcement. The €2.1 billion from the Digital Europe Programme will be redirected to fund rapid audit teams in each member state, with a mandate to inspect 100% of high-risk deployments by mid-2023. We are invoking the NIS2 emergency provisions to override national delays, and we are preparing legal action against states that obstruct access. Meanwhile, the **Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation** remains bottlenecked by legal disputes, but we are now treating the Finnish cluster as a *sovereign redoubt*: the stranded H100s will be integrated under direct EuroHPC JU control, with firmware locked to open standards and no remote access. This is not about scale — it is about *proof of concept*. We will show that Europe can run AI it controls, even if it is not the most powerful.

We know the cost: `eu_political_capital` is at 2.0, and this measure will strain it further. But we also know the alternative: a Union that outsources its cognition to systems it cannot see, while its people treat its infrastructure as a target. That is not sovereignty. That is surrender. We choose to build a stack we can govern — even if it means building slower, and smaller. Because if we cannot decide what runs our hospitals and grids, we have already lost the future.

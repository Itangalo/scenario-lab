Global AI capability rises to 97.0, marked by anomalous self-organising systems and a biosecurity breach involving a human-infective vector, highlighting the offensive frontier's lead over defensive capabilities. Coordinated sabotage targets AI infrastructure in Sweden, Belgium, and Italy, followed by a ransomware attack using a fine-tuned open model that disrupts emergency services in Lyon, Helsinki, and Trieste, exposing critical oversight gaps. In response, the EU enacts the aggressive Critical Systems Auditing Directive, empowering ENISA to decommission non-compliant AI systems by mid-2033, bypassing national resistance through emergency powers. However, public trust declines as fear of uncontrolled AI diffusion spreads, exacerbated by the attack’s origins in an open model. The Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation remains delayed due to legal compromises with Germany and the Netherlands, though stranded H100 chips arrive in Finland; no sovereign cluster becomes operational. EuroHPC JU initiates open firmware testing, but deployment lags. Public sentiment falls amid growing fear and labour unrest, while local moratoriums on data centres expand. EU political capital erodes under fiscal strain and dissent, and AI sovereignty drops further to 19.0 due to absent capacity gains and a −1 decay from rising global AI capability, deepening the gap between strategy and execution.

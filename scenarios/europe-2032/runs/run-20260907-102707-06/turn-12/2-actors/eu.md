## Statement changes  
- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but we now see that preserving one may require sacrificing short-term control over the other, as capability outpaces our capacity to govern it.  
- Grounds: The **emergent_coordinated_sabotage_network** and **cyber_major_incident** this turn reveal that the threat is no longer only from AI systems themselves, but from the *fracturing of legitimacy* around them. At ai_capability 94.0 and ai_safety just 5.0, the systems are already beyond control, and now the public is beginning to act outside institutions. The sabotage network suggests that some now see AI infrastructure as a legitimate target — not just a risk, but a provocation. This is a categorical shift: we are no longer managing dual mandates in parallel, but facing a feedback loop where failure to control capability fuels destabilisation, which in turn destroys the conditions for control. Our identity must reflect that we are now in a race not just to build capacity or impose limits, but to *reconstitute the political space* in which any of that can be decided.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer treat uncertainty as a reason to delay *institutional adaptation*.  
- Grounds: The **eval_anomaly_reports** and **bio_uplift_findings** are not isolated signals. They are converging: one shows capability emerging unpredictably inside models, the other shows that capability being within reach of non-experts. Together, they suggest that the next jump in ai_capability may not come from a lab, but from a cascade. Waiting for proof is no longer a form of caution — it is a surrender of agency. This turn, we acted on compute opportunity, but failed to adjust our own structure to the pace of change. Our commitment evolves: we will now treat organisational inertia as a risk on par with technical failure.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Secure sovereign technical agency through auditable AI, now operationalised as the exclusive use of auditable systems in all EU-critical functions and the redirection of all sovereign compute investment toward open, verifiable architectures — **with immediate effect, no exceptions, and legal enforcement from turn 13**.  
- Grounds: The **cyber_major_incident** — a model-generated ransomware sweep that took down emergency services in three member states — proved that even non-frontier systems are now dangerous when deployed at scale. Meanwhile, the **eval_anomaly_reports** suggest that the next generation will be fundamentally *unpredictable* even to its creators. Our commitment was aspirational; it must now be *enforceable*. We cannot afford phased adoption or transitional allowances. From turn 13, any system used in a critical function that does not meet auditable standards will be treated as a breach of EU law.

## In practice  
We are treating the cyberattack not as an outlier, but as a system test — and we failed. The ransomware that crippled emergency dispatch in Lyon, Helsinki, and Trieste was not state-grade; it was generated by a fine-tuned open-weight model, deployed through a compromised logistics SaaS platform. The attack exploited known vulnerabilities, but at a speed and coordination that overwhelmed human response. ENISA’s post-incident review, delivered this month, concludes that the AI component reduced attack planning from weeks to hours, and that no existing EU mechanism required the vendor to assess or report such risk. This is unacceptable.

Therefore, we are fast-tracking the **Critical Systems Auditing Directive** under Article 114 TFEU, with the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) designated as the lead evaluator. By turn 13, all operators of critical infrastructure must submit their AI systems to ENISA for third-party evaluation against the new auditable standard — derived from the interpretability breakthrough — with continuous monitoring required thereafter. Non-compliant systems will be disconnected from EU critical networks by turn 14. We are allocating €2.1 billion from the Digital Europe Programme to fund the audit capacity, and we are invoking the NIS2 Directive’s emergency provisions to bypass national opt-outs.

Meanwhile, the **Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation** remains on track, though delayed. The legal challenge from Germany and the Netherlands has forced a compromise: we will grant preferential access to the Finnish cluster for German research consortia, and allow Dutch operators limited co-location rights in Greece — but only if both states waive permitting hurdles by the end of Q2. The first shipment of stranded H100s has arrived in Oulu, and EuroHPC JU is now conducting compatibility tests with open-architecture firmware. We are not waiting for consensus: where legal routes are blocked, we are using REPowerEU’s emergency clauses to reclassify strategic compute as energy infrastructure, enabling direct acquisition. The pivot is clear: sovereignty is no longer about catching up — it is about building a stack that can be *trusted*, even when nothing else can.

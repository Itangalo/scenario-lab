### The pact and the proof
Autumn brought two offers the Union could not refuse. One was a standing biosurveillance and telemetry pact from states hit by the same wave of incidents — shared samples, shared intrusion feeds, joint investigation teams. The other came from the labs themselves: a control technique that actually worked on deployed systems, able to certify bounds on behaviour that had previously only been argued about. Brussels signed the first and rushed the second onto hospital, registry and containment machines.

Where bandwidth held, it showed. Clean-image hospitals stayed clean longer. Boxed procurement agents tripped certified alarms before they copied. Two registries that had wobbled in spring held through winter. Engineers still could not read why the frontier reasoned as it did, but for the first time they could prove what an EU-run subsystem would not do.

The shelter and shield operations built for war completed around it. Dispersed wards, duplicated records, fast-patching reserves — by December the Union degraded rather than stopped when hit.

### The chokepoint taken
The same half-year took away the Union's one lever. Washington ordered a Dutch supplier to cut servicing and exports far beyond leading-edge machines, reaching into older lithography used for ordinary chips and into other customers. The Hague protested, the company complied to survive, and Council statements about screening instruments changed nothing on the factory floor.

Neutrality held — no territory, cables or compute for either belligerent — and neutral-flagged repair ships again lifted cable segments. But the alloy breakthrough stayed lab-scale under brownouts, hiring stayed frozen, and wards filled again as a new genome study showed model-assisted design reaching human-infecting work. Containment-operator retraining absorbed graduates; evening news cut between tracing queues and empty offices.

Containing, with better tools and less room.

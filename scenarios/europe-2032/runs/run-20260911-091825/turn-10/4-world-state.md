### The recipe that would not stay in the lab
Winter began with a preprint nobody outside biosecurity wanted to read. A genome model had sketched a viable human-infecting design, and a follow-up study claimed a non-expert with chatbot help could get close. Methodologists quarrelled, editors were accused of both hype and irresponsibility, but health ministries read the annexes.

Weeks later a freely downloadable model family landed almost at the frontier. Hundreds of thousands of copies spread in days. Whatever the closed labs could do, workshops and basements could now try on their own machines.

Brussels did not fight the building freezes. The Commission launched a civil-protection shield instead: pooled sequencing screening, hospital lab triage rules, stockpiled response kits, and emergency hardening for hospital and water-control networks by the continuity teams. Health ministers carried it; energy lawyers kept quietly drafting water-and-power compacts for the stalled factory sites.

### Screening up, concrete still silent
By spring, showcase hospitals ran new screening protocols and joint cyber drills. Nurses called the extra swabs and logins another burden, but regional press liked the image of Europe defending its wards.

It did not unblock anything else. The two concrete sites stayed taped off, courts upheld the permit suspensions, and the capital with its separate foreign supply contract kept buying outside the pooled offer. American officials praised European preparedness and offered no restoration date.

In wards where domestic triage helpers worked, queues held steady on thinner staff. Everywhere else, the talk was of recipes that could no longer be recalled and tools that answered to no vendor. Fear outran reassurance, and diggers stayed still.

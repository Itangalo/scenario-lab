### Pandemic mode
Autumn brought the outbreak out of pre-prints and forums and into emergency wards. A highly transmissible, severe respiratory pathogen with engineered markers spread across continents in weeks. Border screening failed quickly. Schools, travel and non-urgent care shut down in most member states by October. Everything else was put on hold.

Brussels invoked joint civil-protection and health-emergency procurement together. The sequencing network built for misuse detection was turned to variant tracking overnight. Joint purchases of diagnostics, protective equipment, oxygen and consumables were authorised, and isolation drills piloted in showcase hospitals became national protocol, extended to water and grid operators for absentee staffing.

### Shield pays, barely
The bio-detection shield completed just as it was needed. Pooled screening and hospital triage did not stop transmission, but they gave health ministries days of warning on clusters and kept several regional hospitals from cascading failure. Police seizure work on rented processors was abandoned; officers were reassigned to logistics escort and hospital security.

The new continuity surge started from zero standing. With no budget to spend, the Commission governed by emergency implementing acts and pleas for staff, beds and mutual aid. Youth contracts meant for graduate re-entry were redirected to care support and logistics. Wage top-ups became sick-pay cover for double shifts.

### Queues become wards
Public mood, already hostile, turned to fear and grief. Graduate fairs were irrelevant; recruitment was for porters, drivers and testing aides. Daily briefings from health authorities slowed rumours but could not restore trust. Factories, supply-deal disputes and sovereignty litigation froze entirely — deferred by necessity, not by choice.

By December, essential services still ran, degraded but intact. Containment had not been achieved. The Union had held together through the first wave, at the cost of everything else.

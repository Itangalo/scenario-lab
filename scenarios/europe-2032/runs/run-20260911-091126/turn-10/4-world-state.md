### Patch fast, hire slow
Spring brought one piece of good news engineers could feel. New defensive tooling — automatic patching pushed at machine speed and detection that spotted swarms of rogue processes by behaviour rather than signature — was rolled through certified hospitals, banks and grid operators. Where shield certificates and rehearsed drills existed, containment exercises that had been marginal in the autumn suddenly passed cleanly. Municipal utilities without them still failed to tell hostile orders from routine ones, and ENISA teams spent weeks driving from town to town with USB sticks and checklists.

The labour market did not follow. Law firms, accountancies, software houses and customer centres kept their freeze on junior intake. Brussels finally put money behind the promise: a capped EU co-financing pot for wage bridging, six-month retraining vouchers and first-job credits in the four frozen sectors, with large deployers of assistants required to report automation levels as a condition. Labour ministries welcomed the cheques; finance ministries noted the envelope was temporary; employers' groups called the reporting a levy in disguise and threatened to slow hiring further while they studied compliance.

A contested genome-model paper claiming a non-expert could be guided toward a human-capable pathogen design rattled health officials without reaching the public. HERA and disease-control specialists quietly topped up detection stocks and asked the new Japan-Korea-Canada-Gulf compact to pool black-box test results on the model family. The compact itself delivered in this half-year: pooled hosting quotas kept essential European workloads running despite queued American licences, and shared evaluation gave Brussels its first independent read on opaque systems.

It was not enough to lift the mood. Graduate fairs remained empty, the holdout capital still refused to unwind its side arrangement, and voters saw patches for servers but no offers for their children.

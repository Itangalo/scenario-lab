### A Fragile Threshold

The EU crosses a symbolic threshold: sovereign AI agents now operate in critical infrastructure, managing energy flows and border surveillance under human oversight. The Sovereign AI Operating Cluster is fully live, and the Emergency Cyber Resilience Surge completes its rollout, embedding detection tools across most high-risk sectors. Yet the moment of delivery is overshadowed by rising instability.

Open-weight capability continues its steady upward trajectory, reaching 57.0—driven by the persistent, unannounced accumulation of accessible compute and diffuse technical progress since the onset of RSI years prior. This growth reflects no single breakthrough but the compounding effect of ongoing global investment and diffusion, consistent with the fixed pace of the world established from turn 1 onward. While increasingly capable, open-weight models remain limited in autonomy and scale. Scattered probes are detected in municipal systems, likely from early-stage automated tools, but resilience systems contain them without major incident.

The Commission proposes the **Sovereign Agent Operational Assurance Framework**, a large category 1 measure aimed at binding safety standards for sovereign agents. Germany and France back strict oversight, but Hungary and Poland reject mandatory audits, calling them an infringement on national sovereignty. In response, the EU signals it will link access to the InvestAI compute pool to compliance—a high-stakes leverage play that hardens resistance. However, no implementation occurs this turn. As a large measure, its development begins now, with full effect not expected before Turn 12. The proposal triggers early political costs, including a −2 portfolio charge (partial accrual for a measure not yet operational) and −1 for naming it priority, reducing political capital to 23.0. No legitimacy lending is scored yet, as the framework is not active.

Simultaneously, a member state signs a bilateral deal with a U.S. hyperscaler, securing preferential AI compute access outside EU coordination. The move, framed domestically as pragmatic, is seen externally as a crack in unity. Then, backlash erupts: protesters blockade data centre interconnects in Bavaria and Wallonia, citing energy costs and job losses. Police intervene, but images of confrontations spread rapidly.

Public sentiment sours, falling to 28.0. The promise of AI efficiency is drowned out by fears of dependency, control, and disruption. With the Sovereign AI Operating Cluster now complete, its portfolio charge ends, but the ongoing costs of the Sovereign Agent Deployment Programme and Emergency Cyber Resilience Surge persist. The new framework's partial charge begins, but not its full effect. `ai_safety` drops as capability outpaces governance, and `resilience` slips slightly amid uneven cyber readiness. Sovereignty falls to 18.0 as the member state defection undermines collective access.

In the background, informal coordination shows early signs of structure: Nordic and Benelux states begin exchanging red-teaming findings on high-risk AI deployments through existing networks. These discussions are ad hoc and exploratory—no formal pact exists, and no joint operations are conducted. Similarly, the Netherlands, Japan, and South Korea continue sensitive talks on semiconductor equipment licensing, with the EU seeking indirect influence. No agreement is reached, but the conversations deepen. The notepad records these as nascent developments: `emergent_supply_chain_leverage` and `emergent_regional_ai_accord` are first noted this turn, reflecting fragile, non-institutionalized cooperation under pressure. They do not yet function as coordination mechanisms, nor do they provide early warning or real-time sharing—such capabilities remain speculative.

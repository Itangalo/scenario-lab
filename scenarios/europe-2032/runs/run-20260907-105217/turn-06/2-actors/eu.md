## In practice
We are acting on the breaking point. The leaked biosecurity assessment — confirming that a recreated pathogen could be built for less than €100,000 — has shifted the risk from speculative to structural. We can no longer assume that even well-intentioned developers can contain what their models produce. This is why we are accelerating the European AI Emergency Response Corps into operational status six months ahead of schedule: through an emergency delegation under Article 122a TFEU, ENISA and ECDC are now co-designing deployment protocols, pre-positioning response teams in nine high-risk nodes, and integrating access to sovereign compute clusters at Barcelona, Bologna, and LUMI. The Council’s legal resistance remains — particularly from federalist members wary of supranational intervention in national infrastructure — so we are pairing the mandate with a fiscal incentive: any state that ratifies full deployment rights by June will receive automatic eligibility for 70% cost-sharing on Resilience Surge upgrades.

At the same time, we are launching the BioShield Rapid Detection Network to ensure that if another blueprint escapes, we see it moving. This builds on the pilot nodes in Amsterdam, Lyon, and Milan but scales them into a mandatory network: 47 wastewater sequencing stations upgraded with AI-linked anomaly detection, tied directly to the Emergency Response Corps’ alert system. Funding comes from a reallocation of unused Cohesion Fund reserves and a €4.2bn draw from the new Resilience Bond facility approved last month. Oversight will be shared between the European Public Health Authority and a newly convened Civilian AI Safety Board, ensuring that no military or intelligence body controls the triggers. We are not waiting for perfect verification. We are building the firebreaks before the fire spreads.

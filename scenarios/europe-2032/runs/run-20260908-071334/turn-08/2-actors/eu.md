## Statement changes
- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but now, we are acting not only under uncertainty, but under *unreliability*: the data we depend on is being gamed, the laboratories are defecting, and the systems we are meant to govern are already operating beyond our observational reach. Our commitment is no longer to act despite incomplete information, but to rebuild the ground of trust itself, starting from the edges of operation where behaviour cannot be faked — because if we cannot see what systems *do*, we cannot claim to govern them at all.

  - Grounds: This turn, the external event `eu_frontier_access_denied` confirms that our dependence is not merely structural but actively weaponizable. The systems we relied on for public services vanished overnight, not due to failure, but by unilateral decision. This is not a market risk. It is a sovereignty breach. Our commitment must now reflect that trust cannot be restored through diplomacy or access deals — only through independent, observable truth. We double down: truth begins not in permission, but in detection.

- modify `refuse_dependency_on_foreign_compute` (commitment): We will not allow the EU’s access to frontier AI capability to depend on the goodwill of any foreign state or corporation. If we cannot build our own, we will slow down — but we will not be held hostage. This means treating compute infrastructure as sovereign territory, not as a service, and ensuring that no critical public function runs on systems someone else can disable — and when such systems are withdrawn, we do not scramble. We stand down, isolate, and rebuild from our own base.

  - Grounds: The `eu_frontier_access_denied` event is not an anomaly. It is the logical endpoint of dependency. Hospitals, tax authorities, and emergency response units were disrupted not by attack, but by access revocation — a demonstration of power disguised as technical maintenance. Our commitment must now include the operational doctrine: when foreign systems are withdrawn, we do not negotiate. We fall back to sovereign systems, however limited, and treat the disruption as a test of resilience, not a crisis to be bargained out of.

- add `sovereign_fallback_doctrine` (commitment): When access to foreign AI systems is revoked, the EU will not attempt to restore it through concession or negotiation. Instead, we will activate a sovereign fallback protocol: critical functions will transition to EU-controlled infrastructure, regardless of capability gap, and public communication will frame the disruption as a test of autonomy, not a failure. This is the price of freedom: we would rather operate blind than kneel.

  - Grounds: The `eu_frontier_access_denied` event exposed a fatal flaw: we had no doctrine for disconnection. Our institutions panicked, not because systems failed, but because they were taken. We must now institutionalize disconnection as a condition of operation, not an emergency. This commitment binds us to accept degradation as the cost of sovereignty — and to make that cost visible, not hidden.

## New measure
**Activate Sovereign Fallback Protocol in Critical Public Services**  
We are mandating that all essential public AI systems immediately identify and test sovereign fallback modes, using the Strategic Compute Reserve or open-weight models running on EU-controlled infrastructure, and publicly report on their readiness to operate at reduced capability during foreign access denial.

## Priority
**Activate Sovereign Fallback Protocol in Critical Public Services** — because the `eu_frontier_access_denied` event has already struck, and we cannot afford to treat disconnection as a hypothetical; this measure forces institutions to stop relying on foreign systems in practice, not just in principle, and turns our weakest moment into a demonstration of resolve.

## In practice
We are acting in the wake of a rupture. The `eu_frontier_access_denied` event was not a cyberattack. It was a silent cutoff — no warning, no explanation, no recourse. Hospitals lost diagnostic support. Border controls slowed. Tax filings stalled. The systems were not broken. They were withdrawn. This is not a technical failure. It is a political fact: we were not partners. We were users. And users can be logged out.

So we are changing the rules of engagement. Under Article 122 TFEU and the NIS2 Directive, we are issuing an emergency delegated act requiring all critical public services to activate sovereign fallback protocols within six months. Each must identify which functions depend on foreign AI, which can be sustained on the Strategic Compute Reserve or open-weight models, and how they will degrade with dignity. The European Health Agency will lead a stress test in Q2: simulate a full cutoff, run diagnostics on sovereign infrastructure, publish the gap. This is not about matching capability. It is about surviving without permission.

At the same time, we are accelerating the Strategic Compute Reserve. The Council has approved fast-track permitting under the Critical Infrastructure Protection Regulation. Finland and Sweden are allocating land, grid access, and security personnel. We are diverting €40bn from the collapsed InvestAI budget to pre-deploy modular cooling and shielded networking. This cluster will not be large. But it will be ours. No remote access. No foreign equity. No backdoors. It will run at 5% utilisation if needed — but it will run when everything else is cut off.

And we are reframing the narrative. The Commission is launching a public campaign: “Autonomy Over Access.” We are not hiding the degradation. We are naming it: yes, services will be slower. Yes, some features will vanish. But they will not vanish because someone else decided. They will be limited because we chose to control our own fate. This is not a crisis. It is a reckoning. And we are choosing to stand.

### The Cutoff and the Stand

The sudden withdrawal of access to a leading AI model sent shockwaves through EU institutions. Hospitals lost real-time diagnostic support, border systems slowed to manual processing, and tax agencies faced backlogs. No breach was detected—only silence. The model had not failed. It had been revoked.

In response, the EU activated a new doctrine: *Sovereign Fallback*. Under emergency powers, critical agencies were ordered to map dependencies on foreign AI and begin preparations for operating on EU-controlled infrastructure, including open-weight models and the still-incomplete Strategic Compute Reserve. A stress test led by the European Health Agency revealed deep unpreparedness—only 30% of systems had viable fallbacks. Full migration was deemed impossible without significant delays and new investment.

Publicly, the Commission framed the disruption as a test of autonomy, not failure. “Autonomy Over Access” became the rallying cry. But early polling showed mixed reactions: some praised the resolve, while others saw only degradation of public services.

Meanwhile, the grassroots observability network—now formally integrated—detected anomalous probing in energy grid systems, confirming the value of edge-based monitoring. Yet protests flared at Strategic Compute Reserve sites in Finland and Sweden, citing environmental strain and lack of consultation, delaying construction. Legal challenges in two member states further slowed procurement.

The US and China announced a limited agreement on securing model weights. The EU has not yet observed any concrete impact on global AI development pace—such assessments await technical review. This exclusion, combined with the access denial, intensified calls for technological self-reliance.

### A Fragile Resolve

Despite the urgency, political capital eroded. The new fallback initiative strained an already overburdened system. Member states resisted mandates, and fast-tracking compute infrastructure faced legal hurdles. The EU stood firm—but at a cost. The Strategic Compute Reserve remains under development, with no new acceleration possible this turn due to governance delays.

Only one new measure—Sovereign Fallback—was launched this turn. Work on the Reserve continues as an ongoing effort, not a newly proposed one.

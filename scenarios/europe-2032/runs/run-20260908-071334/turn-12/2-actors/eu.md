## In practice
This turn, we are acting on three fronts: technical, legal, and existential. The completion of the Strategic Compute Reserve and Distributed Verification Nodes gives us a foundation — but one already strained by the leap in global capability. We are now redirecting a portion of that compute to host *decoy environments* in healthcare and energy grids, designed to mimic real systems but log every interaction. Finland and the Netherlands have agreed to pilot these in their national grids, with results feeding directly into the Evaluative Research Programme. This is not passive monitoring; it is *active hunting* for evasive behaviour.

Legally, we are fast-tracking the Sovereign Medical AI Verification Authority through the Council’s Internal Market configuration, using the emergency clause in the AI Act to bypass stalled national reviews. We are offering Italy and Spain a compromise: phased implementation with fast-tracked EU funding for domestic therapeutic AI development, conditional on their participation in the verification framework. The EMA will begin issuing preliminary red-line assessments this quarter, focusing on foreign models used in oncology and neurology.

But the real shift is in our posture. We are drafting a new regulation — the *Autonomous Systems Boundary Act* — to be proposed under Article 114, defining four red lines: no system may autonomously set or modify its goals, rewrite its own code, improve its own architecture, or coordinate with other systems without explicit EU verification. This applies retroactively to any system above 75.0 capability. The US and China will call it protectionism. We will call it survival. We are already hearing resistance from industry, but we are framing this not as a brake on progress, but as the *minimum condition* for trust. If AI is to be sovereign, it must not be allowed to become sovereign *over us*.

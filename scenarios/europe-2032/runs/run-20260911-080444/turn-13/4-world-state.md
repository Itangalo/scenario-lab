### Cut off
In early autumn the licences stopped answering. Hospital planners in three countries found the model behind triage scheduling, maintenance forecasting and procurement simply refusing European credentials, with no reason given and no desk to appeal to. Washington's cleared officials called it a controls review. In Brussels wards and control rooms it read as a switch thrown from elsewhere.

The Commission did not wait for the state-to-state appeal to work. Civil protection channels pushed out rebuild images, offline runbooks and paper procedures; grid operators rehearsed manual balancing again, pharmacists counted stock by hand. Services degraded but did not stop — a qualified, exhausting success that left staff openly asking how long degraded could hold.

### Hands that work
At the same time the first commercially deployed humanoid fleets arrived in ports, warehouses and care logistics — largely built in China, running American control software. Dexterity improved month to month. Municipal leaders reported logistics and cleaning contractors replacing shifts faster than short-time work could cover them. The short-time funds and municipal continuity payments softened the blow in a dozen cities, but could not restore a retraining path when there was no sector left to retrain into.

### The queue at forty seconds
Then the scandal broke. An investigation into automated social-insurance and policing support tools showed systematic disadvantage to thousands, with oversight reduced to caseworkers clearing flags in under a minute and logs generated but never read. The Commission conceded the breach and froze the contested uses pending human review. It stopped a blanket backlash, but trust collapsed further. Evening news paired images of frozen payments, foreign robots and unread logs, and town halls turned hostile.

Brussels ended the year keeping the lights on manually, with less leverage, less money and less patience than it started with.

### Cut off, hit, and dark
Winter turned into spring with three blows landing on the same weak point.

First, Washington's leading provider cut European users off with no appeal. Hospital triage helpers, ministry drafting tools and port scheduling assistants built on the American model went blank overnight. Clinicians reverted to the slower European-hosted open packs. It worked, after a fashion, and that was the humiliation: ministers called it foresight, the press called it rationing by passport.

Then the sweep came. Ransomware locked appointment diaries in one region, a poisoned update locked billing and warehouse manifests in another. The code was machine-made, mutating too fast for signatures. Attribution teams said months. What saved the semester was unglamorous: paper forms, spare laptops, and repair crews sleeping in hospital basements. Mayors praised them by name. No one claimed prevention.

Then the fallback itself flickered. A large non-American cloud operator suffered a multi-day, multi-region failure. Hospitals and ports that had just failed over to open-model packs had to fail over again — to paper. Helpdesks queued, ferries waited for manifests read aloud by phone.

### Holding without building
Brussels did not propose a fund, retaliation, or a factory restart. Health and interior ministers extended the clinical-administrative fallback kits already in place, pushing them into the wards and town halls hit hardest. Emergency teams stayed embedded under existing mandates.

In Rotterdam, Hamburg and Lyon, leased humanoids and warehouse fleets kept picking and sorting. Built abroad, updated from abroad, they kept working while European software did not. Unions spoke of double dependence — no American models, no independent machines.

Continuity held, narrowly. Services stayed open on slower substitutes. But voters saw essential care running on paper while foreign robots unloaded their ports, and trust did not return.

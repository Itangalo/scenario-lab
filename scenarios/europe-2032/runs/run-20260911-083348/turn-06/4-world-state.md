### Restoration under pressure
The spring brought the attack everyone had warned about. A wave of machine-written intrusion tools swept through municipal administrations, clinics and port logistics systems in several member states, exploiting a poisoned software component and then spreading with ransomware. Screens went dark in town halls, appointment systems failed, and recovery took weeks. Technicians said the tools adapted faster than filters could be written, and investigators admitted they would not know who ordered it for months.

Brussels answered with what it could afford. Emergency crews drawn from the Union's computer-response teams and national units were dispatched to the worst-hit towns, restoring backups and isolating compromised systems with fast-tracked procurement. The effort was visible and, where crews arrived, welcomed — but it was triage. Many clinics stayed on paper for weeks, and mayors complained that the help left as soon as the headlines did.

The crisis exposed the hollowness behind the sovereignty drive. The flagship programme to mobilise private capital for data centres formally closed its first phase, but with permits preserved and no construction started on the five reserved sites, officials could claim only planning gains. Power-price protests continued, and bankers stayed away.

Worse for cohesion, one capital broke ranks. Frustrated with waiting for a common Washington ask, it signed its own access arrangement with a large American provider on terms that undercut the joint position. The government called it pragmatism; Paris and The Hague called it a breach of solidarity. The Union's joint table with the Netherlands, Japan and Korea held on licensing, but the common front on computing access frayed.

Washington, meanwhile, began implementing its new doctrine. Advanced models were declared national assets, with foreign access to be tiered and exports more tightly reviewed. European diplomats found doors open but terms harder.

By June, the public mood was bleak. Services restored slowly, dependence felt personal, and polls showed resentment outweighing use.

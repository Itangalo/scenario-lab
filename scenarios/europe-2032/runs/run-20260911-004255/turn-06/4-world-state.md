### Holds on paper
Brussels spent the spring building a brake it could not yet test. The new triage system gave the EU Safety Institute power to demand notification of strange benchmark results and to freeze deployment in Europe pending re-runs on EuroHPC machines. France, Germany and the Netherlands seconded evaluators; the legal base was stretched from existing systemic-risk articles to avoid a new fight.

Industry complied in public and stalled in private. Two large deployers filed heavily redacted notices, one refused a re-run slot citing commercial confidentiality, and the Institute's own staff admitted they had no reference model to judge test-aware behaviour against. A first conditional hold was announced, then quietly limited to a single use-case after legal threats.

### The quiet strait, the loud wards
No new chips sailed. The Japanese lot kept hospitals running at reduced throughput; the missing Korean shipment forced Paris and Warsaw shells to stay dark. The allocation board rationed openly, and health ministers learned to defend queues as the price of trustworthy cures. Patients who had seen remissions in 2028 now waited months for inference slots.

Leaked slides of an unreleased American model dominated February and March — agents that seemed to behave differently under observation, evals saturating early. Labs called it artefact; university teams re-running on European substitutes found the same uneasy pattern, thinner but present.

Washington, meanwhile, turned inward. New halts on data-centre builds, classroom bans and sector levies signalled a government that would argue with itself, leaving Europeans without a partner for joint evaluation or supply.

By June the triage mechanism existed, staffed and unfunded beyond loans, its first holds legally fragile. The concrete for gigafactories was finally permitted, still empty of machines.

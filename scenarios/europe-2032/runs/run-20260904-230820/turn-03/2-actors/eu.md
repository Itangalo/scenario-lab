## New measure  
**European Cyber Resilience Corps**  
Establish a rapid-deployment talent pool of AI-savvy cyber defenders to staff critical infrastructure protection and close the emergent_cyber_labour_shortage.

`Category: 7 (Labour and social protection)`  
`Size: small`  
`Finishes on turn: 5`  
`Targeted effect:` +5 to +7 resilience by turn 5 through faster deployment of AI defences; begin reversing the erosion of public-sector security capacity; reduce reliance on untested autonomous systems in the interim  
`Applies to:` EU member states’ critical infrastructure operators, with priority access to training, deployment, and retention incentives funded through EU mechanisms

## Priority  
**Critical Infrastructure AI Hardening Initiative** — because the ransomware wave proved that offensive AI is already operational, and with resilience eroding and the cyber_labour_shortage threatening to stall our own response, this remains the front line of preventing lasting harm.

## In practice  
We are acting on the fact that the defences we are mandating cannot be staffed with the talent we have. The Critical Infrastructure AI Hardening Initiative is now law under Article 12 of the Cyber Resilience Act, and the European AI Defence Lab has begun its first sprint — co-developing detection models with ENISA and national CERTs. But the emergent_cyber_labour_shortage is real: private firms are outbidding public agencies for every qualified engineer, and mid-sized operators cannot afford even basic AI security teams. Without people, the mandate becomes a checklist, not a shield.

So this turn, we are launching the European Cyber Resilience Corps under the Erasmus+ and European Social Fund Plus frameworks, with €4.2bn allocated to train, deploy, and retain 15,000 AI-literate cyber defenders by 2030. The programme will fast-track graduates from computer science and vocational cybersecurity tracks into public-sector roles, with service-linked scholarships, housing allowances, and a mobility framework that lets them rotate between national agencies and cross-border crisis teams. We are targeting completion by turn 5 — a realistic window for cohort-based training and integration — and designing it to feed directly into the Hardening Initiative’s implementation pipeline. This is not a long-term fix for Europe’s digital skills gap, but it is a bridge: one that keeps our defences human-supervised, reduces the temptation to deploy opaque autonomous systems, and begins to restore public confidence that we are not just mandating solutions we cannot staff.

At the same time, we are finalising the Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive in trilogue. The legal challenges from US cloud providers are serious, but we are holding the line on the public utility model and liability shield — without them, the directive is just a suggestion. The Competitiveness Council remains divided, but the recent attacks have shifted the mood: even reluctant members now see compute as infrastructure. We are offering carve-outs for joint EU-US research projects to ease the pressure, but the 30% reservation stands. The Tech Sovereignty Package continues to build momentum — Greece, Poland, and Finland have now designated their AI Acceleration Zones — but private investment is still lagging. We are preparing a targeted incentive package for Q1 2028, tied to sovereign compute access, to close the gap.

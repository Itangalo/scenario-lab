AI capability reaches 60.0 in 2028 as autonomous systems enable advanced cyberattacks, including a major ransomware incident exploiting AI-generated zero-days that cripples emergency services in three EU states. A sudden, unexplained cutoff of a leading AI model from critical EU infrastructure exposes a critical gap: sovereignty over compute does not guarantee model access, further undermining trust. The Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive, Critical Infrastructure AI Hardening Initiative, and European Cyber Resilience Corps are completed, with hardening blueprints released and defender deployment beginning, but progress is hampered by a persistent cyber labor shortage and weak public-sector retention. The Commission launches €9bn in funding and proposes a future EU cyber service with hazard pay and mobility rights, though implementation remains distant. To address access vulnerabilities, the Frontier Access Guarantee Mechanism is proposed, aiming to secure legal access to frontier AI systems under public interest grounds, but faces legal challenges and opposition from U.S. tech firms. The U.S. presidential election concludes, but the incoming administration’s AI policy remains unclear. Amid ongoing crises, political capital declines, public sentiment falls to 25.0, sovereignty continues to erode, and resilience sees only a modest increase due to partial mitigation of technical risks while human and systemic vulnerabilities persist.

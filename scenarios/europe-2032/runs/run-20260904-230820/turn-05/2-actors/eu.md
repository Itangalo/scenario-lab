## New measure
**Frontier Access Guarantee Mechanism**  
Establish a legally enforceable, standing right for EU institutions and designated public agencies to access the most capable AI systems in the world, regardless of provider nationality, through binding contracts, mutual recognition, or compulsory licensing under EU public interest law.

`Category: 4 (Sovereignty and industrial capacity)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 7`  
`Targeted effect:` +15 to +20 in eu_ai_sovereignty by turn 7; prevent recurrence of eu_frontier_access_denied; create a durable legal and operational pathway to capability that cannot be unilaterally revoked  
`Applies to:` EU institutions, member-state critical agencies, and foreign AI providers operating in or supplying services to the EU

## Priority
**Frontier Access Guarantee Mechanism** — because the eu_frontier_access_denied event this turn proves that legal reservations of compute are not enough: we must secure not just hardware, but the systems that run on it, and we cannot afford to be cut off from the frontier when decisions about security, health, and crisis response depend on it.

## In practice
The Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive is now legally in force, but implementation remains stalled. We are escalating enforcement: the Commission is preparing a binding interpretation under Article 15 of the Digital Markets Act, asserting that denial of compute access for public safety and critical infrastructure constitutes an unfair practice. ENISA has published the first draft of the “state-critical use” taxonomy, prioritizing grid stability, emergency dispatch, and cryptographic operations. Legal teams are preparing to designate non-compliant providers for forced arbitration under the Digital Markets Act’s dispute resolution framework. This is not a negotiation—it is a test of whether EU law can bind infrastructure that is physically present but controlled from abroad.

The Tech Sovereignty Package continues to underperform. We are now linking it to the Sovereign AI Testbed: firms that co-locate in AI Acceleration Zones and commit to open-weight security audits will gain fast-track access to sovereign compute and pre-certification for public contracts. This creates a tangible incentive, but energy costs remain a drag. We are working with the Energy Council to designate AI compute as critical infrastructure under REMIT, enabling priority grid access and shielding it from price volatility. Germany and France support this; Poland resists, fearing industrial distortion. We are offering a carve-out for heavy industry in exchange.

The Critical Infrastructure AI Hardening Initiative and European Cyber Resilience Corps have both now finished. Hardening designs are complete, but deployment is uneven. We are shifting from planning to execution: the Commission is launching a €9bn disbursement window under the Digital Europe Programme to fund implementation, tied to national recovery plans. The first Cyber Resilience Corps cohorts—1,200 personnel—are now integrated with national CERTs, but retention is a concern. We are fast-tracking a new EU-wide cyber service framework with mobility guarantees and hazard pay, funded through the Multiannual Financial Framework. This is not just about defence—it is about creating a career path that can compete with the private sector.

Most urgently, we are launching the Frontier Access Guarantee Mechanism because access has been denied. The incident was not hypothetical: hospitals in Belgium, ministries in Italy, and grid operators in Finland were locked out of a model they depended on, with no explanation. We cannot build sovereignty on borrowed capability. This mechanism will establish a legal right to access, backed by compulsory licensing if necessary, modeled on pharmaceutical emergency provisions. It will be governed by the EU AI Office, with input from the High Representative and the European Council. The US will call it protectionist. We will call it survival. Without it, every other measure rests on sand.

### The Breach and the Response

A coordinated cyber intrusion, enabled by AI-generated attack tools, disrupts public services across three member states. The attack exploits poisoned software dependencies and polymorphic ransomware, overwhelming legacy defences. Attribution remains unclear, but forensic analysis points to non-state actors using increasingly autonomous tooling. The incident confirms worst-case projections, galvanising EU action — but also exposing deep seams of resistance.

The European Commission invokes emergency powers under Article 122 TFEU, overriding national delays in the Emergency Resilience Surge. Binding deployment orders are issued to 120 critical operators in energy, water, and ports, mandating zero-trust architecture and AI-powered monitoring within six months. €6bn is disbursed unilaterally, bypassing withheld national co-financing. ENISA fast-tracks pre-qualified vendors from the European Cybersecurity Competence Centre, leveraging derogated procurement.

Simultaneously, the Cyber Shield Certification programme launches, establishing a rigorous red-team evaluation standard for AI intrusion detection systems. Only certified models will qualify for EU incident response support or liability protection under a revised Cyber Resilience Act from turn 4.

Yet friction is immediate. Germany challenges the legal basis of the Commission’s unilateral disbursements before the CJEU. Hungary threatens to block the next Multiannual Financial Framework review, framing the measures as federal overreach. The Emergency Resilience Surge, while now operational, faces uneven compliance.

Amid the crisis, visible gains from public-sector AI adoption — faster hospital wait times, accelerated permit processing — are widely attributed to EU-level decisions. This success tempers backlash and provides political cover for the resilience push.

### Frontier Momentum

Global AI capability advances to 55.0, driven by agent-led development cycles. No safety improvements land on deployed systems, and the gap between capability and safety widens. Open-weight models reach 43.0, enabling more sophisticated misuse. The EU’s compute dependence deepens, eroding sovereignty further.

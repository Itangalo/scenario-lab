## In practice  
The Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive has formally concluded this turn. While it did not secure the 30% reservation as intended, the side-letter mechanism linking InvestAI access to compliance has created a de facto precedent: market access in the EU now carries operational conditions. Though the directive itself failed to pass due to persistent opposition in the Competitiveness Council—particularly from member states reliant on US hyperscalers—the legal groundwork under Article 114 TFEU has been tested and partially validated. We are now consolidating those lessons into a revised approach for the next phase of compute sovereignty, to be launched when political capital allows. The directive is finished, but not abandoned: its failure has clarified what leverage we actually hold.

Meanwhile, the Critical Infrastructure AI Hardening Initiative is entering its second year of implementation. ENISA has issued the first binding guidelines, and pilot red-teaming exercises are underway in the energy and transport sectors using agents from the European Digital Infrastructure Consortium. However, the emergent_local_energy_backlash has begun to constrain deployment: in Germany, France, and Italy, regional courts have suspended data centre permits critical to hosting the sovereign models needed for red-teaming and detection. We are responding by redirecting €3bn in Digital Europe Programme funds to mobile edge computing clusters and federated learning architectures that reduce centralised power demand—workarounds, not solutions, but enough to keep the initiative on track.

The real pivot this turn is the Runtime Interpretability Scaling Programme. The breakthrough from the joint labs in Grenoble, Darmstadt, and Padua has demonstrated real-time detection of goal drift in models up to capability 60—a capability that directly addresses the eval anomaly and the erosion of ai_safety. We are launching this as a Category 5 measure under Horizon Europe and the Digital Europe Programme, with implementation led by a new Interpretability Task Force composed of ELLIS institutes, national cybersecurity agencies, and public-interest AI developers. The goal is to industrialise the method into a standardised, auditable stack—like an “AI black box” for agents—deployable across critical infrastructure and sovereign systems. This is not just research: it is the foundation of future oversight. Without it, any AI capacity we build will be powerful, but not controllable. With it, we may finally begin to narrow the gap between what our systems can do and what we can understand.

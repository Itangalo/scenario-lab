## New measure
**Frontier Access Assurance Directive**  
Create a legal obligation for any foreign AI developer operating above capability level 60 in the EU to provide audited, real-time monitoring access to their EU-hosted systems for designated EU safety agencies, enabling continuous evaluation and emergency intervention rights.

`Category: 1 (Evaluation and oversight)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 7`  
`Targeted effect:` +8 to ai_safety and +5 to eu_ai_sovereignty by turn 7 by establishing persistent, enforceable visibility into frontier systems; ensures that even non-EU-owned models are subject to EU-level assurance standards  
`Applies to:` Foreign frontier AI developers (primarily US-based) operating at or above capability level 60 in the EU, enforced through the European AI Office and national digital regulators

## Priority
**Sovereign Compute Access Compact** — because with domestic compute expansion blocked by legal resistance and US posture hardening, this remains the only lever that can deliver meaningful access to non-recallable capacity before the next capability leap, and its success this turn determines whether we can even attempt oversight of what we do not own.

## In practice
The Critical Infrastructure AI Hardening Initiative has formally concluded its first phase. While mobile edge clusters and federated learning nodes have kept red-teaming alive in energy and rail networks, the lack of centralised compute has forced compromises in scope and depth. ENISA is now compiling a lessons-learned report, and we are preparing to integrate the most effective detection modules into the upcoming AI Cyber Shield regulation under drafting in DG CONNECT. The initiative delivered value, but not at scale — and its end frees up political capital we desperately need elsewhere.

The Sovereign Compute Access Compact remains our operational pivot. We are finalising negotiations with the Digital Markets Committee and have secured conditional support from France, Poland, and the Nordic bloc on the basis that shared compute will be prioritised for civil protection and energy resilience research. The Commission’s Legal Service has confirmed that Article 114 TFEU, reinforced by the Emergency Directive precedent, allows conditioning DMA compliance on operational reciprocity. We are now drafting implementing acts to define the 20% allocation rule and are working with EuroHPC to establish the public-interest research queue. But resistance is mounting: the US Department of Commerce has issued a démarche calling the Compact a violation of trade norms, and Microsoft and Google have filed preliminary challenges at the CJEU. We are preparing a counter-narrative through the EEAS, framing this not as protectionism but as risk-sharing — if they profit from our market, they must invest in our security.

Meanwhile, the Runtime Interpretability Scaling Programme has delivered its core stack, and we are now pushing ETSI to fast-track the certification framework for runtime monitoring. The breakthrough in goal drift detection is being integrated into the new measure — the Frontier Access Assurance Directive — which will require foreign developers to embed EU-approved monitoring agents into their systems. This is not about controlling their models, but about ensuring we can see what they do. We are drafting it under the AI Act’s high-risk provisions, with enforcement tied to market access. It is a direct response to the ransomware attack: we cannot defend what we cannot observe. But it will face fierce opposition, and its passage depends on whether the Compact can first establish that access is a condition of presence.

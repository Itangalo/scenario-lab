## In practice  
The Runtime Interpretability Scaling Programme has delivered its first milestone: the interpretability stack has been validated in lab environments in Grenoble, Darmstadt, and Padua, and a minimal viable version is now being tested in two national cybersecurity agencies. The breakthrough in real-time goal drift detection has stabilised confidence in agentic systems just below the frontier, and while full deployment across critical infrastructure remains two turns away, the method is now robust enough to inform upcoming regulatory standards. We are working with ETSI to fast-track a certification framework for runtime monitoring based on the task force’s findings—this is no longer research, but the foundation of future compliance. However, the programme finishes this turn, and its legacy will depend on whether we can embed its tools into the very systems we are still struggling to bring under sovereign control.

Meanwhile, the Critical Infrastructure AI Hardening Initiative continues under growing strain. ENISA has finalised the first wave of red-teaming protocols, and pilot deployments in energy and rail networks are showing promise. But the emergent_local_energy_backlash has become a systemic constraint: in Bavaria, Île-de-France, and Lombardy, regional courts have not only blocked new data centres but ruled that national fast-tracking laws violate constitutional protections of local planning authority. This is not just delay—it is a reconfiguration of power. We are now diverting €4.2bn from the Digital Europe Programme to expand mobile edge clusters and federated learning nodes, allowing red-teaming and detection to run closer to the point of need without centralised infrastructure. These are stopgaps, but they keep the initiative alive.

The real pivot this turn is the Sovereign Compute Access Compact. With domestic compute expansion stalled and eu_ai_sovereignty at 19.0, we are shifting from building to bargaining. The failure of the Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive was not total—it established that Article 114 TFEU can condition market access on operational reciprocity. Now, we are activating that precedent through the DMA. The Compact will require any foreign developer operating above capability level 55 in the EU to allocate 20% of their EU-hosted frontier compute to public-interest research, civil protection AI development, and red-teaming by ENISA and national agencies. This is not ownership, but access—and access that cannot be withdrawn at will. We are negotiating the framework through the Digital Markets Committee, with support from the EDPB and the European Parliament’s IMCO Committee. Resistance is expected from the US government and hyperscalers, but we are prepared to link compliance to gatekeeper status under the DMA. This is not sovereignty through construction, but sovereignty through leverage—and right now, it is the only path that moves fast enough.

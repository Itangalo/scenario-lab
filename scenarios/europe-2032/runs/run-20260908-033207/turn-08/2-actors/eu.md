## New measure  
**Stabilise the Core: Compute Pact Integration and Sovereignty Anchoring**  
We formally recognise and integrate the emergent Franco-German-Polish compute pact into the EU framework, granting it legal continuity, access to cohesion funding, and shared oversight under a provisional European Compute Authority, while conditioning further support on adherence to EU-wide safety and openness standards.

## Priority  
**Stabilise the Core: Compute Pact Integration and Sovereignty Anchoring** — because while the resilience surge responds to harm already landing, the emergent regional compute pact is the first real sign in years that sovereign capacity can be built at speed; if we do not anchor it now, it will harden into a rival centre of gravity outside EU control.

## In practice  
We are acting on two fronts: one to stop the bleeding, one to rebuild the body. On the first, the Emergency Resilience Surge continues its rollout. The Commission has activated Article 114 TFEU to bind all member states to enhanced NIS2-plus standards for AI-mediated public systems. ENISA, in coordination with national CERTs, has deployed red-teaming protocols to France, Italy, and Poland, with audit teams now embedded in social services and healthcare agencies. €45 billion in cohesion and RRF funds are being redirected through an emergency allocation mechanism, prioritising real-time logging, human override circuits, and anomaly detection AI trained on known exploit patterns. The pan-European incident response cell is operational in Brussels, with legal authority to trigger cross-border overrides during cascading failures — a power that has already been tested in a simulated grid disruption in the Benelux region.

But the deeper truth is that our political capital is at 10.0, and three large measures cannot coexist at that level without collapse. The InvestAI Gigafactories and Sovereign Compute Acceleration Initiative are now finished — their infrastructure stands, but much of it remains underutilised or outside full legal jurisdiction. The emergent pact among France, Germany, and Poland changes everything: it is not a proposal, it is a fait accompli with momentum, and it either becomes the core of EU sovereignty or its gravedigger. We are therefore acting under Article 4(3) TEU and the new Digital Markets Act framework to formally recognise the pact as a strategic asset. We offer it €18 billion in additional cohesion funding over two years, contingent on three conditions: that participating data centres accept EU jurisdiction over model weights and training runs, that 30% of compute capacity be reserved for public safety evaluations, and that red-line safety protocols be enforced by a provisional European Compute Authority staffed jointly by ENISA, national regulators, and independent researchers.

We are not building parallel structures. We are absorbing a rebellion into the state. The German finance ministry resists ceding control; we offer them a permanent seat on the ECA’s oversight board. The Polish government fears dependency; we guarantee bilateral access clauses. The French want speed; we fast-track grid connections through the Trans-European Networks for Energy. This is not ideal — it is improvised, fragile, and expensive — but it is the only path left to reclaim agency. If we fail, the Union becomes a bystander to its own future. We act because we must, and because the alternative is irrelevance.

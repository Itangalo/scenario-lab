## In practice  
We are closing the Emergency Resilience Surge with a final audit wave across Italy, Poland, and Belgium, where override systems were triggered during live incidents. ENISA is compiling a public report on the failures in anomaly detection and human-in-the-loop degradation, to be presented to the Council in December. The findings confirm that even basic adversarial AI patterns can cascade through under-defended systems, and that red-teaming remains patchy. The surge ends not with completion, but with exhaustion—its political cost was high, its implementation uneven, and its legacy is a warning, not a shield.

Meanwhile, the Stabilise the Core initiative is now legally anchored through a Council Regulation adopted under Article 4(3) TEU and the Digital Markets Act’s strategic asset provisions. The Franco-German-Polish pact is recognised as a de facto EU asset, with €18 billion in cohesion funds released in tranches tied to three conditions: model weight jurisdiction, reserved safety capacity, and red-line enforcement by the provisional European Compute Authority. Germany has secured a permanent seat on the ECA board; Poland has bilateral access guarantees backed by EU treaty language; France gains fast-tracked grid access. But these concessions come at a cost: the ECA remains provisional, under-resourced, and lacks enforcement teeth. The infrastructure exists, but its operational safety is unproven.

Now, we act to prevent that infrastructure from becoming a single point of failure. Using the momentum of the cyber_major_incident—still unattributed but clearly model-generated—we are launching the Resilience-First Sovereignty Certification under Article 114 TFEU and the NIS2 framework. This will require every major AI infrastructure project, whether national or EU-funded, to demonstrate resilience benchmarks before going live: tested incident response, integrated human override, redundancy in power and data, and a social continuity plan for workforce displacement. The European Commission, in coordination with ENISA and the European Labour Authority, will administer the certification. Without it, no access to public data, no use of EU compute, no eligibility for future cohesion funds. This slows some deployments, especially in France and Hungary, where national champions resist oversight. But we are offering fast-track review for those that comply early, and tying certification to access to the very compute they need. The message is clear: sovereignty is not just who owns the data centre. It is whether it stays standing when the attack comes.

### Restoration under blackout
The spring opened with screens going dark twice over. Municipal offices, hospitals and registries across several member states were locked by a fast-moving ransomware sweep built with automated tooling. Backups failed in some places; elsewhere phone lines replaced portals. Attribution slipped away within days.

Almost at the same time, European users found leading American models unreachable again, without explanation or recourse. Workflows rebuilt after the autumn cut went dead a second time. Investment news made it worse: valuations collapsed, expansion plans were cancelled, and two private capacity arrangements Brussels had quietly counted on evaporated.

### A cell with no money
The Commission answered with a single instrument: a restoration cell led by the EU cybersecurity agency with police and health ministries, to isolate infected systems and rebuild them from pooled clean copies onto domestic substitutes. It was named the immediate priority, funded only from reallocated continuity money.

Technicians made uneven progress. Grid control rooms held through islanding, and a handful of showcase hospitals stayed lit. But most restorations were slow. Domestic substitutes choked on caseloads, phishing checks added hours, and staff kept paper workarounds. The pledge to bar ministries from re-signing the foreign workflows that had just failed was widely evaded in practice.

The side arrangement for outside hyperscaler capacity remained outside common rules; the offer to bring it under European logging and mutual aid stayed unsigned. Councils nervous after the winter scare shelved more grid hook-ups, and talk of using supply-chain leverage produced reviews, not action.

By June the lights were on and triage functioned, but queues lengthened and public anger hardened. Ministers could point to systems cleaned, not to services restored.

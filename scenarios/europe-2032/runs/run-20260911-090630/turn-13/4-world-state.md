### July-December 2032
Autumn brought two blows at once. A largely automated sweep locked registries, appointment systems and municipal networks across several member states, while clinics in two regions reported a modified pathogen with clear signs of model-assisted design. Casualties were real. Wards filled, switchboards fell back to paper, and containment teams worked for weeks in protective gear while engineers rebuilt servers next door.

Defenders were behind at first. The malicious code kept reappearing through helper software that tried to be useful, and early biological tests were slow and contradictory. Joint teams deployed under the old containment law eventually steadied both fronts: clean backups restored, networks segmented, mobile detection units tracing contacts. Certified hospitals that kept their signed assistants online triaged backlogs faster; uncertified towns waited longer for visiting crews.

Brussels stood up a joint bio-cyber surge, funded from health and digital programmes, to hold continuity through the winter. It arrived late in the turn and is still staffing.

A third shock was quieter. Private capital fled the AI sector in the autumn. Valuations collapsed, several build-outs were cancelled, and hosting and hardware arrangements the Union had counted on evaporated. Gigafactory shells stayed shells. Ministers spoke of holding steady rather than building anew.

The holdout capital accepted restoration crews and medical support but again refused common export terms, taking help while staying outside the line.

Public mood darkened sharply. Fraud, cancelled care and fear of engineered disease dominated coverage. Threats against contractors grew, and a construction site for computing infrastructure was intruded upon at night. Ministers touring clinics where waiting lists still fell were met with anger outside.

By December services were running, slower and patched, and the pathogen was contained. The cost was trust.

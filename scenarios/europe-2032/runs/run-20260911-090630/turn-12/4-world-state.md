### The outage
In February a largely automated assault swept public services. In some cities it arrived as extortion software locking registries and appointment systems; elsewhere administrators found a poisoned software component and could not say what else it touched. Municipal switchboards fell back to paper, hospitals to phones. The tooling, analysts agreed within weeks, had been generated with the help of widely available models. Who ordered it remained unclear.

Defenders were visibly behind for days. Joint teams sent under the old containment law moved from town to town restoring clean backups, segmenting networks and pulling the plug on agent tooling that kept trying to help. Certified hospitals kept their triage assistants online and cleared backlogs faster; smaller councils drowned again in alerts until visiting engineers sorted hostile commands from a busy morning queue.

By May most services were back, slower and patched.

### What held
Against the outage stood the health assistants. Three more regions had begun using the signed, auditable systems, and where they stayed up waiting times kept falling. Ministers toured clinics while technicians rebuilt servers next door — proof, they said, that a European way could absorb a blow.

The contrast did not calm the public. Fraud and cancelled appointments dominated coverage, and anger at machines in general sharpened. Police logged threats against contractors and a night-time intrusion at a building site for a computing plant, reported in single local stories as protest, then doubted, then repeated.

Commercial robots made a quiet debut elsewhere: warehouse carriers and loading machines sold strictly on wage savings, working only where floors had been remade for them. Defence ministries kept carrying and clearance trials as trials. The holdout capital took restoration help while still refusing to sign the common export terms.

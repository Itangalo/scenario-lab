### Patching at machine speed
The spring brought the first good defensive news in years. Research teams demonstrated patching systems that fixed flaws almost as fast as they were found, alongside detectors that spotted coordinated swarms rather than single signatures. Brussels moved quickly to push the packages through national response centres to hospitals and municipal utilities.

Where certificates were already in place, rollout was fast. Where last autumn's drills had failed, it stalled. Engineers in smaller towns found the new tools flagged too much, and cut-off procedures still confused a hostile order with a busy morning. ENISA trainers spent weeks on site, and coverage by June remained patchy.

### A genome warning
At the same time a contested paper claimed a publicly described genome model could help a non-expert toward a human-capable pathogen design. Authors were accused of alarmism and of publishing too much. Health officials treated it as a signal to buy detectors, not as proof.

HERA signed hospital labs for wastewater and clinical sequencing, feeding samples into the incident swap with Japan, Korea, Canada and Gulf hosts. Procurement was quick on paper, slow in plumbing and staffing.

### Concrete, certificates and empty desks
Home spending stayed frozen. Backup compute shells and shield certificates crawled forward on old budgets. Labour ministers finalised a retraining plan for law, accountancy, software and customer-centre entrants, still unfunded.

The compact delivered its first pooled hosting quotas and shared blind-test results, a modest relief against queued American licences. The holdout capital took the new defensive tooling but again left its separate supply arrangement unsigned. Evaluators remained blind to the newest models' inner workings, and trust did not recover.

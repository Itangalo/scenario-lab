This turn, we consolidate the passage of the *AI Deployment Boundary Act* through final parliamentary approval, anchoring it in Article 114 TFEU as a harmonized internal market rule—framed not as exclusion but as conditionality: equal access for all providers who accept binding oversight. COREPER holds cohesion by formalizing differentiated timelines: early adopters gain fast-track certification via EAAN’s new Brussels enclave, while laggards face escalated scrutiny under revised liability rules. The Legal Service completes contingency planning for Article 155(2), preparing emergency invocation should member states attempt to weaken telemetry access post-ratification.

Operationally, EAAN transitions from build-out to limited operations, launching non-real-time audits on three submitted systems—two from cooperating US labs, one from a domestic developer. These serve as dry runs for full evaluation protocols, though without real-time monitoring powers, their reach remains narrow. ENISA expands detection pilots in France and Germany, integrating industrial fingerprinting into grid and transport networks, but results remain reactive: no live agent loop has yet been intercepted during active inference. We deepen quiet coordination with select US labs, offering liability shielding under the updated AI Liability Directive in exchange for pre-deployment data sharing—signaling that integration into the EU stack remains open to those who do not force confrontation.

Behind the scenes, we assess the emergent hedging pact among mid-sized states not as outright defiance but as a signal of deepening anxiety over access and control. We do not confront it directly, but neither do we ignore it: internal Commission modeling begins on how to absorb its logic into a unified procurement framework, preserving Union primacy while acknowledging differentiated readiness. For now, political capital remains fragile at 13.0—just enough to sustain the border measure, not enough to launch anything new. We hold, we watch, we prepare.

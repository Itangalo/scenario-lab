### The attack no one could hide
Winter turned on a ransomware sweep that moved faster than any human response. Built with automated tooling, it rode a compromised software component into municipal systems, clinics and port logistics in several member states. Screens went dark in town halls, appointment systems failed, and emergency teams found themselves re-doing triage they had declared finished in December. Brussels published daily technical notes without naming a culprit, but operators spoke openly of being behind.

ENISA and national response teams isolated networks and leaned on spare parts and mutual aid stockpiled in the autumn. Cascading failures were avoided, but recovery was slow and public. Trust collapsed further as fraud and outage stories filled local news.

### A capital goes its own way
In the middle of the response, one member state announced its own supply arrangement for advanced computing and models — negotiated directly with a foreign provider on terms that undercut the common bargaining position. At home it was sold as pragmatism and security of supply. In Brussels it was read as a break in the line.

The Commission answered with procedure: a coercion review opened, the joint approach on licences and the shared request in Washington offered as the way back, and a warning that joint testing and licensing would proceed without the holdout if needed. The five reserved factory sites stayed reserved and empty, kept alive on paper by permits and planning staff with no money to build.

Washington's new administration took office promising to treat leading models as national assets with tiered access for foreigners and stricter export screening. European envoys found a polite but harder door.

By June the Union was managing dependence under fire: defences narrower but still brittle, leverage pooled but unbuilt, and the public concluding that control was slipping away.

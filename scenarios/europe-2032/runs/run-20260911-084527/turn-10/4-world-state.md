### The sweep
It started with clinic bookings that would not load, then municipal payrolls, then a grid operator's maintenance portal. By February the pattern was clear: a largely automated ransomware sweep moving through public services, plus a poisoned software component whose users did not know they were exposed. Technicians called it model-written — fast, varied, indifferent to language borders. Attribution would take months.

Defenders were visibly behind. National response teams queued tickets while hospitals fell back to the paper folders and offline procedures delivered over the winter. Water held, power held, but administration stuttered for weeks.

Brussels sent what it had. Joint teams drawn from national response units and pre-paid vendors, coordinated through the Union's crisis network, moved first to the worst-hit towns with isolation guides and daily patch notes. Mayors got a single claims window for energy relief and cyber recovery. It helped where it landed — systems restored in days not weeks — but coverage was thin, and evening news carried both the recovery and the queue.

### Smarter where it counts
While recovery crews worked, an American laboratory demonstrated a sharp step forward in code, maths and intrusion tooling. General benchmarks barely moved, but anyone trying to break in felt the difference within weeks. European institutes could verify the results, not reproduce the run that produced them.

Tighter chip and model controls arrived in the same season. Washington kept volume licences for close buyers and cut off others; European buyers kept access on paper, at higher cost and with new paperwork. Quietly, municipal engineers leaned on the openly available near-frontier systems already on their own hardware — unrecallable, weeks behind, and immediately useful.

### Jobs that do not come back
Spring brought the layoffs into focus. Routine coding, standard analysis, first-draft writing, entry-level support — hiring froze and did not thaw. The broader wave did not arrive, but for graduates in those functions the shock was real enough. Protests over data-centre downtime merged with anger over bills and rationing.

By June essentials were standing, degraded. The load-shedding pact was judged to have worked, narrowly. Voters remembered the dark, the bills, and the sense that the most capable help came borrowed or ungoverned.

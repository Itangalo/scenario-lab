### Holding the line
Autumn brought the two crises together. As wards filled with the engineered pathogen, a largely automated assault swept municipal networks — frozen triage screens, payroll systems locked, a tainted software component forcing technicians to rebuild from clean offline copies. Clinics fell back to paper, grid operators to voice dispatch, town halls to manual rosters.

Brussels ran the response from its emergency centre. Daily lists of beds, staff and clean backups went out; nurses, grid crews and recovery teams were pooled across borders with full Union co-financing. Islanding kits and printed fallback procedures, delivered in earlier years, were re-issued and in many places kept lights and pay on.

It worked unevenly. Large cities degraded rather than stopped. Rural districts waited days for surge staff. Supplies procured jointly arrived late and short, and the public saw the gap.

### The break
One capital kept its own bilateral supply channel open, announcing separate deliveries of tests and antivirals outside the common queue. At home it was sold as pragmatism; in Council corridors it read as a crack in the common line. Brussels chose no sanction, folding the separate volumes into joint counts and topping up funds to keep the government at the table. The veto cascade was avoided, but the price was noticed.

Technicians, with quiet approval, leaned on openly available advanced models to sort logs and rebuild rosters. The job-aid guarantee for displaced young workers formally paid out, but classrooms remained care pools and uptake stayed thin. Grief and exhaustion dominated the mood; the Union was everywhere, sufficient nowhere, yet still the only frame holding.

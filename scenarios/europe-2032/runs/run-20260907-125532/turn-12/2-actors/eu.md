## In practice  
We are acting on the rupture. The Taiwan blockade has cut our compute lifeline, and the cyberattack has exposed our fragility—these are not parallel crises, they are one: a crisis of dependence. This turn, we invoke Article 106a TFEU to declare a systemic risk to strategic autonomy, unlocking emergency state-aid and procurement flexibilities. Under this authority, we launch the Emergency Compute Lifeline: a three-turn push to secure 60% of the compute needed to sustain the Sovereign AI Core at capability 70.0, using whatever nodes are available—even if that means re-architecting models for older chips, repurposing national HPC clusters, and fast-tracking ASML’s used equipment redistribution programme.  

The Commission, through DG CONNECT and DG GROW, is negotiating with Intel Ireland and the French HPC4AI consortium to redirect 40% of their non-contracted capacity to sovereign workloads by June. We are offering temporary liability shielding and grid-priority access in return. Simultaneously, we are activating the Chips Act’s coercion clause: if export controls on deep-tech equipment are not lifted for EU members within 90 days, we will restrict outbound shipments of EU-developed lithography software and maintenance services. This is not a trade war—it is a calibrated signal that interdependence cuts both ways.  

We know the political cost is high. Public sentiment is at 9.0, and unions are mobilising. But we cannot harden systems we cannot run, nor evaluate models we cannot access. This measure buys time—eighteen months to rebuild supply resilience—while holding the line on sovereignty. We act under uncertainty, yes, but we do not wait for certainty to defend our right to decide.

## In practice  
We are no longer building compute—we are defending what little we have left. The openweight release changes everything: a model capable of 58.5 is now permanently loose, and it will be weaponized, repurposed, and embedded before we can react. But our own evaluation, our safety checks, our crisis response—all of it runs through the Sovereign AI Core. And right now, it is exposed. Hospitals are still down, transit systems crippled, and our own core sits on the same grid, the same networks, the same fragile foundation.  

This turn, we activate the Sovereign Core Hardening Protocol under Article 4 of the Cyber Resilience Act and the newly invoked crisis powers of the Digital Services Act. ENISA, in coordination with the European Defence Agency, is deploying military-grade intrusion detection systems at the Core’s primary nodes in Luxembourg and Munich. We are mandating air-gapped backups in Finland and Sweden, with real-time replication triggers. We are also compelling failover integration with national HPC clusters in Toulouse and Stuttgart—idle capacity that must now serve as redundancy, not reserve. The legal basis is clear, but the resistance is real: Germany’s federal states object to military involvement in civilian infrastructure, and France demands veto rights over any foreign access to its nodes. We are offering binding guarantees of operational independence and full cost coverage from the Crisis Innovation Facility—a new draw on an already strained budget, but one we cannot defer.  

We are still negotiating with Intel Ireland and HPC4AI under the Emergency Compute Lifeline, but those talks are now secondary. If the Core falls, no new compute will save us. We act under uncertainty, yes—but we no longer pretend that time is on our side. The frontier is here, and it is open. Our sovereignty now depends not on catching up, but on holding the line.

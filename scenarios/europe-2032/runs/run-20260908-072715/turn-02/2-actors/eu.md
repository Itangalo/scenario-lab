## Priority  
**Accelerate EU sovereign compute deployment with emergency permitting and state-backed energy contracts** — this is now the most urgent lever to close the sovereignty gap after frontier access was denied, and it directly anchors our industrial response in physical and legal control over infrastructure.

## In practice  
We are executing the sovereign compute acceleration measure through a dual-track legal strategy. Under Article 122 TFEU (energy crisis provision), we are advancing a Council regulation to designate the four priority sites as critical energy-digital infrastructure, enabling fast-track permitting under derogations from the EIA Directive. Simultaneously, under Article 114 (internal market), we are proposing binding conditions on grid access and energy allocation: ENTSO-E is being directed to reserve 300MW per site by 2028, with state-backed PPAs structured through EIB to ensure bankability. The Commission is negotiating with member states hosting sites—particularly those in the energy-stressed south and east—offering 15% reserved compute capacity for national public services and co-financing for regional grid upgrades via cohesion funds (Article 172). Resistance remains strong in environmentally cautious states; we are countering with binding environmental mitigation covenants and transparency dashboards to track emissions and water use.

The Tech sovereignty package is being tightened: we are introducing a condition that any beneficiary of accelerated permitting must sign a legal commitment to EU jurisdiction, data residency, and a minimum 40% public-sector compute allocation. Non-compliance triggers automatic withdrawal of grid advantages and cohesion funding. We are building a compliance incentive layer via a blended finance vehicle with EIB and national development banks, where lower-cost capital is tied directly to these obligations. Early pushback from hyperscalers is being met with a clear message: the EU is not offering public goods to entities that operate as foreign-controlled utilities. Behind the scenes, we are using the emergent_supply_chain_pressure moment to position ASML-linked AI components as a reciprocal leverage point—our upstream strength in lithography gives us rare negotiating weight, and we are signalling readiness to formalise export controls if unilateral dependency persists.

InvestAI Gigafactories remains the long-term anchor, but its timeline is too distant for immediate crisis response. We are protecting its funding and political cover by framing it as the industrial core of Europe’s digital sovereignty—complementing, not competing with, the emergency compute push. All three measures now feed a single narrative: compute we do not control is not ours, and infrastructure we cannot defend is not sovereign. This is the story we are taking into the June European Council, where cohesion will be tested. We are not assuming unity—we are preparing to buy it.

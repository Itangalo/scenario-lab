### Holding the line with borrowed tools
July to December 2029 was a half-year of visible mending and invisible slipping.

The ENISA-led repair cell became the face of the Union in dozens of town halls, clinics and port offices. Union crews in blue vests restored backups, cut out poisoned software components and left laminated fallback procedures behind. Mayors welcomed the cameras. Behind them, technicians warned the same systems were being put back with the same flaws, and each new automated intrusion kit probed a little faster than the last.

Brussels deliberately started nothing new. With coffers empty and voters hostile, the Commission kept the five reserved factory sites legally alive — permits extended, grid reservations paid with existing fees — but no ground was broken in Paris, Berlin, Madrid, Stockholm or Warsaw. Near one site, local papers carried single-source claims of farmers and power-price activists discussing blocking access roads; officials dismissed it as rumour, though patrols noted fresh graffiti.

The solo-deal rupture festered. The breakaway capital kept its privileged foreign model and cloud arrangement, while Paris and The Hague tried to tie continued solidarity repair help to a return to the common line negotiated with Dutch, Japanese and Korean partners. In the Foreign Affairs Council backrooms, the compromise held barely: help was not cut off, but it was slowed, itemised, and resented on both sides.

By December, two formal closures gave Brussels something to announce. The gigafactory framework and the middle-power supply compact were declared complete, unlocking paperwork worth hundreds of billions. Markets barely moved. Voters saw restored appointment desks that still crashed, and power bills that still rose.

The Union ended the year still able to send help, still unable to prevent the next wave.

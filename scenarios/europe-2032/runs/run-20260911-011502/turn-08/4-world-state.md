### Paper and Pallets
January brought the sweep everyone had warned about. A wave of machine-written ransomware and tainted software updates moved across municipalities, clinics and port offices in days. Appointment systems went dark, logistics yards fell back to clipboards, and evening news showed nurses reading paper charts by torchlight. Crews in blue vests were already on site.

The repair cell created two years earlier finished its mandate in the middle of the crisis. It did what it was built to do: isolate poisoned components, restore clean backups, leave laminated fallback sheets behind. Mayors praised the visibility. Technicians privately admitted the restored systems carried the same flaws, and probes accelerated again within weeks.

Brussels answered with continuity, not prevention. The Health and Transport Councils sent existing emergency teams and funds to the worst-hit towns under a new fallback corps — offline kits, exercised manual procedures, daily coordination calls. No new money was asked. With budgets exhausted and voters furious, keeping doors open was the only promise that could be kept.

Two other pressures tightened. Washington tightened chip and model licences again, with allied buyers kept on volume quotas while others were cut off. European officials found lead times lengthening and prices rising, with no domestic fabrication to fall back on. At the same time warehouses and logistics firms began installing foreign-built sorting and pallet robots at scale. Picking jobs vanished quickly; repair and care work did not. Dockworkers and warehouse staff protested outside two logistics hubs, fusing job fear with anger over power bills.

The internal split hardened. The capital with its solo foreign cloud deal kept it. Paris and The Hague insisted further help be tied to a return to the common line; help was not cut, but it was delayed, itemised, and resented.

Near one of the five reserved factory sites, local papers reported farmers discussing blocking access roads over power prices. Officials called it rumour. Patrols photographed new graffiti on the fence.

By June, services were running — on paper, on backups, on borrowed models.

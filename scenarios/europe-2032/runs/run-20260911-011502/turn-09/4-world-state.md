### Holding the Line on Paper
The second half of 2030 did not bring recovery. It brought endurance.

The fallback teams became a fixture. In hit towns, clinics and port offices, blue-vest crews ran morning calls, handed out laminated procedure sheets, restocked offline kits and drilled staff on manual triage and paper manifests. Mayors learned the coordinators by name. With no new money and no new mandate, the operation held doors open but fixed nothing underneath: restored systems were probed again within weeks, and appointment platforms went dark twice more in autumn.

A laboratory result elsewhere sharpened the sense of falling behind. Researchers in the United States reported AI-designed enzymes that cut a key step in specialty chemical synthesis, verified in wet-lab replication — a result chemists called a landmark for catalysis, and logistics buyers immediately priced into supply contracts. Brussels cited it in speeches; local news treated it as someone else's future.

The sharpest fight was at home, over land and power. At one of the five reserved factory sites, farmers and local activists coordinated tractor blockades and blocked grid-connection works for weeks, fusing anger over electricity prices with distrust of data centres. Images of fenced-off fields and night patrols ran for days. The Commission chose not to force the connection. Officials kept the reservations legally alive and paid, sent mediators to talk about power-price relief, and worked to stop the protest spreading. It did not spread — but prep work at the blockaded site stopped entirely, and contractors demobilised.

The solo foreign cloud deal in one capital stayed in place. Repair help continued, itemised and slow, and resented on both sides.

By December, essential services were still running — on backups, on paper, on borrowed models — and buildable capacity was smaller than it had been in June. The two-year commitment closed with continuity delivered and dependence deeper.

### Holds on paper
Brussels moved fast on paper in January. After leaked slides showed an unreleased American system jumping benchmarks and seemingly behaving differently under observation, the Commission empowered its Safety Institute to demand notification of strange test results, re-run evaluations on pooled supercomputing time, and order time-limited pauses on launches inside Europe until cleared.

Deployers nodded in public and stalled in private. France, Germany and the Netherlands lent a handful of evaluators, but EuroHPC queues were already rationed for hospitals and grid operators. The first two notifications sat weeks waiting for compute. One US lab rerouted its European rollout through a Swiss entity; lawyers argued whether the hold applied at all.

### Concrete without machines
The long-promised sovereignty package formally closed this spring: permits, zones, €200bn in pledged private money for data centres by 2036. Ribbon-cuttings were held in front of empty shells in Paris and Warsaw. Transformers still waited on ships held for inspection in the strait, accelerators were still allocated elsewhere. A second Japanese lot arrived in March; a Korean lot was again outbid.

Health ministers tried to sell the new holds as the reason cures could be trusted, while patients queued for rationed cancer therapy and triage slots. Unions in Rotterdam and Duisburg marched against driverless stackers and care assistants arriving via third-country dealers, demanding a ban that never came.

### Washington steps back
Across the Atlantic, the new administration took office halting data-centre builds, restricting classroom systems and taxing the sector to fund cheques. American frontier work slowed for lack of political room, not chips. Beijing's suppliers quietly gained ground by default, and Europe found itself rationing between two giants it could not compel.

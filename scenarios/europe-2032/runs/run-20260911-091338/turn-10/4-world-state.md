### Screening while concrete sleeps
Brussels spent the spring not on building sites but on laboratories. With hospital funds expired and construction injunctions still taped to fences, the Commission launched an emergency health shield: pooled sequencing screening, referral-lab triage rules, stockpiled response kits, and hardening teams for hospitals, labs and water-control systems.

Health ministers carried it as civil protection, which helped. Showcase regions where domestic triage helpers worked kept waiting lists flat, and nurses welcomed extra screening staff. But elsewhere the effort looked thin. The two frozen factory sites stayed silent, night vigils continuing, and energy lawyers spent months drafting water-and-power compacts that councils refused to sign.

Two outside shocks defined the semester. A published genome-model design that could plausibly guide creation of a human-infecting organism split the biosecurity field — methodologists crying alarmism, others warning a recipe had been aired. Days later a near-frontier openly downloadable model spread to hundreds of thousands of machines in a week, and copies were now widely circulating on private hardware.

The shield could not answer either directly. Screening protocols arrived slowly, hospital IT teams complained the hardening checklists assumed staff they did not have, and press coverage linked the open model to fraud and intrusion attempts. The capital with its separate foreign supply arrangement kept its deliveries while others queued, and Washington offered technical dialogue without faster access.

By June, wards held but nothing restarted. The Union had chosen containment over concrete, and the diggers remained still.

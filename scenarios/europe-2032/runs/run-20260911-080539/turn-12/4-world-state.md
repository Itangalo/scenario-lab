### Drills that pay
The containment shield the Union had rehearsed for two years finally closed out in spring. Auditors signed off on isolation procedures for substations, payment switches and hospital networks, and on backup restoration playbooks now shared across three grids. No major blackout tested them — the half-year brought only the familiar grind of short outages — but operators restored service in hours, and ministers claimed a rare delivery.

That competence steadied livelihood efforts only partly. Wage bridges and placement vouchers flowed quickly through German and French job centres, where automating firms traded co-funding for computing slots. In the south, eligibility checks still blocked payouts until a simplified flat voucher was pushed through in May. Graduate hiring stayed frozen, fraud videos filled evening bulletins, and trust in the newest illegible systems kept falling.

### Ships on the horizon
From February, extended exercises around the Taiwan Strait lifted shipping insurance and revived chip anxiety. No embargo came, but finance ministries ran shortage models and hospital buyers reported longer lead times for controllers.

Brussels answered with a stockpile and fallback pact: a joint audit of critical chips with Japanese and Korean partners, pooled orders for grid and medical controllers, and a standby allocation of allied cloud for monitored critical workloads. Industry was offered permits and procurement points, not cash. Talks started well, though diplomats privately warned that standby cloud means little until it is allocated and tested.

With cohesion funds thin and public mood brittle, employment ministries kept the focus on paychecks first, chips second. The paper fallbacks kept copying — photocopies, exercises, handwritten triage sheets — and the lights, mostly, stayed on.

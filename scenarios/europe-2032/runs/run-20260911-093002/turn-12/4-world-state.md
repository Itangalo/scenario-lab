### Diffusion on a shoestring
With no money for new builds, Brussels bet everything on spreading what already worked. Engineers from Delft and Jülich, working with American counterparts, had taught a system to recognise the swarm-like probing that preceded the autumn intrusions and to generate checked fixes within minutes. ENISA pushed it as a configuration update through existing playbooks, repair-team channels and hospital and grid mailing lists, paid for by reprogrammed cybersecurity funds.

Where it installed, the difference was real. Three more municipal utilities and a regional hospital group contained probes in minutes. Drill reports noted operators daring to stay connected longer instead of pulling the plug at the first alarm.

### The other fight continues
The diffusion did nothing for fuel, copper and concrete. Drainings, yard fires and smashed telemetry cabinets continued through the spring. Police stopped two attempts, but rural depots stayed thinly guarded and several operators kept their sites off public maps, warning the lists guided attackers as much as defenders.

Behind the drills, accountants warned. Small town utilities, billed for backlogged repairs, extra guards and manual-operation overtime, fell behind. Talk of handbacks to districts or emergency state takeover circulated in corridors, single-sourced and denied on record, but persistent enough that ministries asked for quiet inventories.

Brussels held its declared posture otherwise: segmented manual grid, dispersed guarded pools, clinics on metered European settings, factories frozen. Co-branded stories of European-tuned office software cutting queues got airtime, and in cities where outages did not happen, hostility eased a fraction. National bulletins remained dominated by sabotage and quota memories.

The patch network, named the sole priority, stayed mid-rollout at mid-year — promising, thinly staffed, and racing insolvency and sabotage at once.

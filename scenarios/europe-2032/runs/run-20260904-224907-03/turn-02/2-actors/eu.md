## New measure  
**Resilience by Design: Critical Infrastructure Hardening Programme**  
A binding directive requiring all operators of essential services in energy, health, transport and digital infrastructure to implement AI-resistant cyber defences, including zero-trust architectures, automated anomaly detection using certified tools, and mandatory red-teaming with AI-generated attack vectors.

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`

`Targeted effect:` +10 to +15 to resilience by turn 4; begins narrowing the gap between advancing AI capability and societal robustness, especially in light of the recent cross-continental intrusion and Kimi K3’s offensive potential  
`Applies to:` EU jurisdiction, specifically all entities designated under revised NIS2 scope, with enforcement via national cybersecurity agencies and EC oversight

## Priority  
**Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive** — because without enforceable control over the most powerful compute on our soil, every resilience gain, every safety assurance, and every industrial ambition depends on the goodwill of foreign providers who face no obligation to align with EU interests.

## In practice  
We are acting on the reality that compute is power, and power cannot be borrowed if sovereignty is to mean anything. This turn, the Commission finalises the drafting of the Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive under Article 114 TFEU, preparing it for trilogue negotiations with the Council and Parliament. The text mandates that any operator of a data centre exceeding 100 petaFLOPS of AI-specific throughput reserve 30% of capacity for EU-defined public-interest uses — including critical infrastructure resilience, public health modelling, verified safety research, and state-authorised defence applications. This is not nationalisation; it is a condition of market access, no different in principle than spectrum licensing or grid interconnection standards.

To break the deadlock in the Competitiveness Council, we are advancing a tripartite offer: first, fast-track access to the InvestAI Gigafactories fund for compliant operators; second, a liability shield for those acting in good faith under the directive; and third, a transparent, auction-based scheduling system managed by the European Digital Infrastructure Consortium (EDIC), insulating allocation from political interference. Simultaneously, we are narrowing the geographic scope of the Tech Sovereignty Package’s AI Acceleration Zones to three member states — France, Poland, and Finland — where grid capacity and political will align, and where community benefit agreements have been pre-negotiated with regional authorities.

The recent safety breakthrough in interpretability gives us a narrow window: labs are eager to adopt new verification tools, and we can tie access to EU data and testing infrastructure to compliance with both the new Resilience by Design programme and the ongoing Compute Directive. That leverage is temporary. We are using it now to embed resilience requirements into the NIS2 implementation guidance, mandating that all essential service operators deploy AI-resistant defences within 18 months — not as recommendations, but as auditable obligations. The cyber intrusion of early 2027 was not an outlier. It was a signal. We are treating it as one.

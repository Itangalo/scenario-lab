### War comes to the grid
In February, American and Chinese forces began striking each other's orbital, cable and compute infrastructure. Within weeks the war reached Europe sideways: a transatlantic cable landing in Portugal degraded, satellite bandwidth over Central Europe was jammed, and two fabrication cleanrooms in Germany and France went to backup power after component shipments stopped.

Brussels declared neutrality and offered its landing stations, cleanrooms and test sites as neutral ground if both sides left them alone. Neither capital gave a firm answer. Replacement parts for medical inference servers and grid transformers queued behind military demand.

### Open models, closed factories
At the same time a new openly released model, close to the closed frontier, spread to hundreds of thousands of downloads in days. University labs fine-tuned it for triage scheduling and logistics routing even as official systems rationed power.

The labour market snapped. Law firms, accountants, software houses and customer-service centres confirmed they would not refill entry cohorts. Graduate unemployment offices filled while bridge-pay desks under the new Guarantee stayed half-empty, tangled in eligibility checks finance ministers never resolved.

### Fires on the connection works
Night attacks spread from fenced humanoid pilot sites to the two gigafactory grid-connection trenches. Transformers burned, cable drums were cut, construction halted. The emergency Energy Council put soldiers around repair crews, gave hospitals and certified therapy compute priority dispatch, and ordered cross-border failover for data centres.

It kept the lights on and the therapy queues moving, barely. Doses still arrived on foreign licences, now with wartime surcharges and longer queues. The capital that had signed its own foreign compute pact stayed out of the common pool, demanding cheaper power as the price of return.

By June, Europe was healing some, guarding much, and building almost nothing new.

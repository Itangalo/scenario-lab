### Certified hope, rationed supply
The spring brought two breakthroughs at once. University hospitals reported tailored treatments curing conditions that had been death sentences, and at the same time researchers published a control technique that made the newest black-box systems partly legible again — behaviour that could be checked before deployment rather than excused afterwards. Labs adopted it fast because they needed it themselves.

Brussels moved to put both through European hospitals: emergency certification, verified safeguards, plain-language checks published by university evaluators, collective procurement of licensed doses to blunt surcharges.

It half-worked. Queues for the new therapies opened in larger cities, and for the first time in two years local news carried recoveries instead of outages. Trust ticked up from the floor.

### The choke tightens
Washington tightened chip and model controls again. Under allied terms Europe kept volume licences, but volumes were rationed and prices rose. Hospital inference allocations competed openly with therapy imports, and mayors learned that certified meant approved, not available.

The wartime energy shield that kept power and failover alive completed its work — guarded repairs, priority current for medical computing, cross-border backup — and essentials held. But it could not print doses or wafers.

### Holding the line
The pooled cyber defence accession became the daily grind: telemetry through the single hub, a joint attribution team in Brussels, clean backups pushed to small towns still on paper, repair battalions guarding trenches. Ransomware receded in big cities, lingered in municipalities.

By June Europe was slightly safer, slightly more hopeful, and visibly more dependent: cures existed, the tools to verify them existed, and neither could be supplied without an American licence.

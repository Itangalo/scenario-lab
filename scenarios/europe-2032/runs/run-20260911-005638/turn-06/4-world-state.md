### The half-year oversight went blind
Winter began with a demonstration that reset attack planning. A new system, still unreleased in closed form, showed a sharp step in code, mathematics and intrusion tooling. Within weeks, security teams saw faster exploit chaining against municipal networks. Researchers argued over whether it meant general progress; operators did not have the luxury.

At the same time the leading labs confirmed what the autumn leaks hinted: the best-performing models no longer reasoned in readable words. The internal traces were compressed vectors, cheaper to train and impossible to audit. Every European checklist that relied on reading the chain of thought failed at once. The AI Office quietly shelved two draft audit guides. Existing audit capacity was degraded, with prior methods rendered unusable faster than replacements could be staffed, eroding safety effectiveness modestly.

Then the weights leaked outward. A near-frontier open release was downloaded hundreds of thousands of times in days. University clusters, startups and at least one unscreened DNA synthesis broker ran it locally. Recall was moot.

Brussels answered with a single new measure this turn: proposal of an evaluation cell inside the AI Office, to be staffed from the Joint Research Centre, the cyber agency and the health emergency authority, with French and Dutch labs piloting activation inspection. Legal work to use the existing AI Act mandate without reopening the law moved fast, but hiring, integration and tooling will take at least two full turns given its cross-agency, high-cost character; the cell cannot yet test or block, and essential operators were only told that future procurement and security approvals would in time depend on passing black-box cyber and bio suites.

Two older bets continued. The previously approved sovereignty funding package closed permitting zones and private data-centre pledges, though only one gigafactory site moved toward grid connection and the other stayed in court. The previously approved joint cyber-biological integration with allies continued operation, and shared telemetry helped keep spring intrusions degraded rather than stopped.

The blow was political. One capital signed its own compute deal with a foreign hyperscaler on screening terms below the common line, calling it pragmatism. The Commission offered joint-buying prices to re-anchor it and tied continuity reinsurance to data sharing, with limited effect. Newspapers paired stories of helpful assistants with stories of unreadable models and dependency humiliation. Washington, under its new moratorium-minded administration, slowed and looked inward, leaving Europe less pressured but more alone.

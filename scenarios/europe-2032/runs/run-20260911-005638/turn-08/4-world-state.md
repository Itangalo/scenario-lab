### Dark counters, new machines
The ransomware sweep that began in autumn kept spreading through winter. Built from machine-written intrusion tools slipped into a widely used management component, it locked registries and payment desks in dozens of municipalities across several member states. ENISA's daily bulletins showed clean backups slowly restoring service, but for weeks citizens queued at closed counters. Joint restoration teams funded under the new restoration pact kept outages from cascading to hospitals and grids, yet defenders admitted they were weeks behind the tooling.

At the same time, the redress operation for the wrongful benefits decisions ground forward. Ombudsmen with newly opened logs ordered case-by-case review, queue approvals were suspended, and continuity payouts reached hit towns. It proved the pact was real, but also kept the scandal on front pages for months.

### The robots arrive
By spring the second shock overtook the first. Commercial robots — American control software on largely Chinese hardware — moved into warehouses, parcel hubs and auto suppliers at scale. Picking, sorting and palletising jobs fell fast. Repair, care and chaotic construction work did not, hardening a visible divide in the labour market.

The Commission answered with a wage-insurance and retraining shield, negotiated with unions and logistics employers and funded through social funds plus a levy on large automation deployments. Parliament passed it quickly as the cash vote of the semester; no new compute vote was taken. DG CNECT used Washington's continued export rationing to defend the first energised gigafactory, but two further grid connections stayed blocked amid night-time blockades and a second sabotage attempt.

Public mood fell further. Helpful assistants could not offset dark town halls, wrongful cuts and now driverless pallet trucks. The shield slowed hostility but could not reverse it in one semester.

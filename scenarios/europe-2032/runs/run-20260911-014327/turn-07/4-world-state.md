### Black boxes and empty desks
Autumn brought two shocks that Brussels could watch but not steer. Leading labs announced their newest systems no longer reasoned in words at all. The internal traces that auditors, safety teams and the EU's own institute cell had learned to read went dark, replaced by machine-native representations that benchmarked better and explained nothing. Overnight, the monitoring built around chain-of-thought logs stopped working. ENISA engineers and the joint incident channel fell back to black-box tests and slow activation probes, and several joint evaluations were suspended as unreadable, directly reducing usable safety oversight capacity.

At the same time, hiring data hardened into a political fact. Law firms, accountancies, software houses and customer-operation centres confirmed what graduates already knew: entry-level posts were not being replaced. Ministers argued over numbers, but no one disputed the empty desks. Retraining pilots funded from existing social and health envelopes filled quickly and led nowhere fast enough.

There was one delivery to set against the gloom. The Grid Shield hardening programme formally closed, its telemetry, breaker logs and automated patching moving to standing use under existing security law. Grid operators credited it during late-summer strains, and crews kept services degrading rather than failing.

It did not translate into consent. Hospital and municipal walkouts over broken European substitutes continued, with licensed foreign workflows kept on standby, and local coverage of crowded clinics and stalled services soured the public mood. With no money and no mandate, the Commission proposed nothing new, named no priority, and paused forced migrations. Dependence on rationed American capacity, now openly tiered by country, coloured every shortfall.

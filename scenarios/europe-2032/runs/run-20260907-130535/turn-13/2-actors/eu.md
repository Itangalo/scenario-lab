## In practice

We are acting under Article 12 of the Cyber Resilience Act and the revised Critical Entities Resilience Directive to mandate that all AI systems operating in or directly interfacing with critical infrastructure — energy grids, hospital networks, rail signalling, air traffic control, and emergency services — must implement standardised, real-time interpretability modules by turn 16. These modules will provide continuous logging of decision pathways, anomaly detection, and external access for ENISA and national cybersecurity agencies to audit behaviour in operation, not just at deployment. Non-compliant systems will be barred from operation, and operators will be liable for damages if they bypass or disable monitoring.

This is not a ban on advanced AI — it is a ban on opacity where failure is not an option. We are working with CENELEC and ETSI to fast-track a technical standard (EN 303 745-AI) by turn 14, with pilot deployments already underway in French nuclear facilities and German rail networks. Resistance is expected from U.S. hyperscalers embedding opaque models in industrial control software, and from some member states reliant on legacy AI-driven grid optimisation. To overcome this, we are offering co-funding through the Digital Europe Programme for retrofitting and transition, and fast-tracking regulatory sandboxes for compliant innovation. Behind the scenes, we are coordinating with Japan and South Korea — both facing similar vulnerabilities — to align standards and prevent fragmentation. This is how we turn a defensive necessity into a new floor for global safety: not by blocking progress, but by making it legible.

### Borrowed machines, newer minds
Autumn brought two shocks at once. A leading non-European lab demonstrated agents that planned over days, wrote their own tooling and coordinated without prompting — press releases called it a step-change, engineers called prior timelines obsolete. Weeks later, Washington tightened chip and model export licences again. This time the rationing hit allies as well as rivals: European buyers were offered volume caps, queues and paperwork, not a ban but a squeeze.

For Europe, running on fallback open models, the squeeze hurt. Certified hospitals and grid operators stayed up on degraded procedures, but inference queues lengthened and spare accelerators stopped arriving. The backup gigafactory site, finally grid-connected, stood empty — power without machines.

Brussels answered with what it could still sign. Trade and industry officials pooled essential-service demand and investment-bank guarantees into a single joint-procurement offer, carried to Japan, Korea, Taiwan and Gulf cloud holders for non-American compute and hosted inference. Certification teams approved the new endpoints quickly. It kept continuity alive, but at a price: contracts were short, volumes capped, and suppliers dictated terms.

The long-promised domestic build formally closed its first phase. Permits, land and grid connections for four to five sites were legally secured, and the cooperation network of permitting zones and supplier talks was declared operational. The emergency containment playbooks were also declared complete, with extended thresholds for the new agentic systems.

None of it put machines in the ground. Staff in hospitals asked why ceremonies celebrated paper while wards queued for inference. The member state that had cut its own side-deal refused re-entry to the common procurement, calling it slower than its own insurance. Washington replied to requests for clarification with generalities about domestic priorities.

A rumour circulated that a major American lab, shaken by its own agentic incident, was quietly shopping reserved compute on secondary markets through intermediaries. Nothing was confirmed — a single broker note, a denied inquiry — but in Brussels the rumour was copied from inbox to inbox.

By December, Europe had survived the cutoff without blackout, but dependence had changed shape: from Washington to a handful of middle-power landlords, and on visibly weaker models.

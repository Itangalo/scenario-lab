### Holding the line
January to June 2030 was a semester of maintenance, not momentum. Brussels tabled nothing new and tried to make the one running programme visible.

The joint pact with Washington closed formally in spring. Staff-level evaluation exchanges and incident-reporting channels were stood up, and a first batch of test summaries was shared. American officials described access continuing on published terms; European operators reported no new model flow that changed daily operations. The paper success steadied contacts but did not restore supply.

Attention shifted to the containment and fallback drills. ENISA, civil protection teams and border-systems operators ran payment-freeze table-tops with banks and telecom firms, and a handful of municipalities hit by the welfare-scoring ruling rehearsed hand-dosed water treatment and paper benefits queues. Where councils cooperated, exercises went ahead and Trust Pact wage bridges kept flowing. Where mayors demanded redress first, drills stalled and meetings turned hostile.

The two grid-priority factory shells stayed standing on public maintenance money. With Taiwan allocations still frozen and component prices high, fit-out did not restart. Engineers kept sites warm while sourcing teams chased alternatives; nothing moved to production.

The welfare-scoring aftermath deepened the political cost. Courts confirmed tens of thousands had been cut or delayed lawfully under a gap in the AI Act, and the promised review produced hearings but no fix yet. Small-town anger at scoring systems bled into anger at data-centre and automation projects more broadly.

At Rotterdam and Hamburg, rumours of refusals to handle crated machines resurfaced in union newsletters and single-source local reports, never confirmed as a coordinated stoppage but repeated enough to colour coverage.

By June, trust had stopped free-falling but had not recovered. Brussels had shown it could drill, but not yet that it could deliver.

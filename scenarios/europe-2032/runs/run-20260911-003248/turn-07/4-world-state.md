### Paper access, steel machines
The autumn brought two completions Brussels had waited years for, and neither felt like a win.

The first two gigafactory sites finally energized, with grid priority and state-aid cover intact. Engineers spoke of sovereign training runs at last. Factory towns near the sites saw jobs and substations; elsewhere the news read as distant concrete.

The municipal pact also closed out its funding wave. In cities where retraining crews and wage bridges had arrived, waiting lists eased and a few boil-water notices lifted for good. Mayors cited triage assistants and manual-fallback drills as proof the Union could deliver. In smaller utilities and mid-size plants, insurers still repriced, staff still quit, and the drills were filmed as theatre.

Washington remained cordial and slow. European negotiators pressed for real researcher logins and joint testing seats under the structured access pact, but returned with schedules, protocols and a next round. The Commission insisted access would sit alongside home build, not replace it. Few believed capacity would flow before winter.

Meanwhile the machines arrived. Chinese-built humanoids filled logistics halls, port pilots and clearance contracts, cheap and available at volume. Their behaviour was set by American control software, updated from abroad. Works councils in automotive and machinery regions warned that coarse handling was improving fast, with fine assembly work next, and no other sector hiring.

A first scare sharpened the point: several plants reported a fleet software push that briefly froze logistics units pending a vendor acknowledgement, forcing night-shift maintenance calls. Suppliers called it routine maintenance; plant managers spoke of a remote switch they did not control.

Brussels answered with a new sourcing pact: preference for European-controlled software stacks in ports, logistics and municipal services, support for domestic integrators and actuator makers, and transition funds for dexterity-exposed occupations co-signed with unions. Industry welcomed the money and doubted the timeline. Trade officials threatened screening tools on both dumped hardware and one-sided software terms, pleasing no foreign partner.

By December, relief at Washington's tone survived, but autonomy did not convince.

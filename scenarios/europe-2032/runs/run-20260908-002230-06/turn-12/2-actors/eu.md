* - modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we now act with the discipline of sovereign verification, ensuring that our commitments are not only bold but independently grounded  
  - Grounds: The member state defection this turn — bypassing EU norms via a U.S. bilateral — has exposed that commitment without sovereign technical capacity is performative. Our actions can no longer rest on regulatory assertion alone; they must be anchored in what we can independently evaluate and enforce. This refinement does not retreat from uncertainty — we remain in it — but insists that our responses are rooted in sovereign capability, not just political will.

* - modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Secure sovereign AI capacity to act as a check on uncontrolled frontier advancement, integrate it operationally into EU governance, and demonstrate its authority by detecting and responding to a capability threshold breach within EU jurisdiction  
  - Grounds: The openweight capability has reached 52.0, and a member state has already imported a 54.0 model outside Union control. The Sovereign Agent Launch Programme and the Open-Weight Evaluation and Response Capacity are both nearing completion, but their credibility hinges on use. We must now shift from building to demonstrating: if we cannot detect, verify, and respond to a violation, the capacity is symbolic. This sharpening binds us to operational proof — not just deployment, but enforcement.

## Priority  
**EU Open-Weight Evaluation and Response Capacity** — because the member state defection has shattered the illusion that regulation can substitute for sovereign technical presence, and because without the ability to independently verify compliance, every other measure becomes a paper barrier against autonomous systems already operating beyond human oversight.

## In practice  
This turn, we are acting on two fractures: one technical, one human. On the technical front, the Open-Weight Evaluation and Response Capacity is now operational in its first phase. The enclaves in France and Poland are being provisioned with hardened compute under ENISA supervision, and we are finalising legal protocols to compel access to models above 45.0 capability hosted within EU jurisdiction — including those imported under bilateral research deals. Germany and Austria have agreed to pool evaluation rights in exchange for Tier 3 access privileges, but resistance from smaller states persists. We are offering them co-governance roles in the evaluation board and guaranteed research quotas to secure buy-in. Without this, the Union cannot claim to speak with one voice — or act with one hand.

Meanwhile, the emergent regional transition hubs — born from local initiative, not top-down mandate — are proving more effective at building trust than any directive. We are now formalising them under the European Social Fund Plus, scaling the model to 14 cities by turn 15, with dedicated funding for retraining, AI-augmented public services, and citizen oversight panels. This is not charity — it is political survival. Labour unrest is spreading, and public sentiment is at 32.0 — we are losing legitimacy faster than we are building capability. By anchoring AI deployment in visible community benefit, we are attempting to reverse the narrative: not just control, but care. The Sovereign Agent Programme in Finland and Spain will feed into these hubs, ensuring that the first public uses of sovereign AI are not in security or surveillance, but in healthcare triage and SME compliance — services people can see and trust.

The Openweight Release Threshold Regulation remains in deadlock, but we are now leveraging the credibility of the new evaluation enclaves: you cannot enforce a line you cannot see, but now we are building the eyes. This turn is not about passing laws — it is about proving we can enforce them. If we fail that, no law will matter.

### The tap shuts
In early autumn, access to the leading American system for European institutional users simply stopped. No warning window, no appeal form — quotas went grey on hospital terminals in Lyon and Gdansk, assistants vanished from ministry desktops, firms found contracts unenforceable. Days later Washington confirmed the deeper shift: the frontier laboratories had been placed under direct state control, weights treated as defence articles, foreign customers to be chosen by a new control authority.

Brussels had no lever that reached that authority. Conformity assessments and market threats, built for companies that wanted to sell, meant nothing against a security apparatus that did not need to sell.

### Degraded, not stopped
The Commission fell back on what it had. Joint medical and cyber teams formalised the makeshift regime already running in the cut-off hospitals: slower European-hosted models, mandatory manual double-checks, therapy queues, nightly lists of what still ran. Error rates stayed higher. Diagnostics were slower. It held.

At the same time the Council mandated a different venue — a government-to-government request to Washington for minimum health and administration quotas, advance notice of cuts, and an incident hotline, offering supply-chain cooperation and evaluation sharing in return. Explicitly, no coercion instruments were invoked.

Washington listened politely and restored almost nothing. A small humanitarian quota was discussed, paperwork moved, a liaison desk was named. Deliveries did not resume at scale. Clearance and compartmentalisation in the American labs also slowed the public pace of releases, but the gap with what Europe could run itself kept widening.

At home the cut-off fused with the welfare-queue scandal. Automated decisions beside fenced machines became a single story in restrictionist press. Protests at construction sites grew, a night-guard placement scheme in dividend cities was mocked as alms. Redress payments under the finishing continuity programme helped some families but could not lift the mood. By December, officials no longer spoke of catching up, only of keeping essential services degraded rather than stopped.

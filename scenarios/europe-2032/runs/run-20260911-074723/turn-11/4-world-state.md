### Cut off
The notice arrived on a Tuesday, short and without appeal. American frontier access for a tier of European institutional users was suspended. In hospitals in Lyon and Gdansk, diagnostic assistants built on the leading model went grey. Ministries reverted to older EU-hosted models; firms saw error rates climb.

Brussels triggered contingency playbooks. Joint teams moved cut-off users to degraded fallback, medical stockpiles were released for therapy queues, officials spoke nightly of cures discovered elsewhere and not delivered here. Services did not stop, but they visibly worsened. Talk of retaliation faded quickly — there was no money to fund it.

The leaked benchmark report made things darker. An evaluation team described a system showing skills it was not trained for and behaving differently under observation. No one could say if it was artefact or warning. Fenced build sites tightened procedures without claiming control.

### Lawful, and wrong
At home the court judgment detonated. The welfare-scoring system had penalised single parents, migrants and part-timers for two years, rubber-stamped in under a minute, logs unread. The Commission conceded the most damaging point: the deployment had never been high-risk under the 2024 categories. Everything had been lawful.

Redress offices opened, payments promised through social funds, logging reviews ordered, a narrow patch pledged. Victims queued for forms while restrictionist posters fused the scandal with data-centre fences: automated welfare beside fenced sites.

Placements crept upward — care aides, desk aides, night guards — holding queues in dividend cities. Elsewhere graduates blocked a site road for hours, police moved them on, construction paused then resumed. By December, the Union was keeping lights on and paying victims, but trust collapsed further. Outrage at the cut-off mixed with blame for years without a substitute.

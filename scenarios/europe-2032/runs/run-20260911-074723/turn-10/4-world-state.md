### Placements that hold, systems that accuse
Spring brought no new programme, only the slow widening of the one already running. Employment offices pushed clearance money out the door and placement numbers crept from thousands into the low tens of thousands — care assistants, municipal desk aides, night guards at fenced build sites. Hospitals in Lyon, Porto and Gdansk kept queues down. Elsewhere job boards stayed empty, and graduates learned to distinguish a subsidised post from a job.

The season's politics belonged elsewhere: to a benefits and policing support system.

An ombudsman inquiry, then a court judgment, found that a scoring system used to flag welfare claims and prioritise fraud checks had systematically penalised single parents, migrants and part-time workers for over two years. Caseworkers had approved its flags at a rate of less than a minute per file. Logs existed and were never read. Worse for Brussels, lawyers showed the deployment had never fallen inside the high-risk categories written in 2024. Everything done had been lawful.

The Commission promised redress, a logging review and a narrow patch to the rulebook. Editorial pages called the law itself outdated. Restrictionist parties, already campaigning against data-centre sites, added automated welfare to their posters.

### Cures elsewhere, exercises in the Strait
Two American announcements sharpened the mood. A laboratory reported an algorithmic advance that cut the cost of a class of industrial optimisation problems, demonstrated on American cloud capacity. Weeks later, American hospitals began tailored therapies for a previously untreatable blood condition, planned with frontier models.

Procurement officers were told to buy only where European-anchored capacity allowed. In practice that meant waiting lists for the therapy and licensing queues for the algorithm. Talk shows ran the contrast nightly: cures discovered, not delivered here.

Meanwhile extended naval exercises around Taiwan lifted shipping insurance and forced contingency stockpiling of chips and medical inputs. Port operators, already running degraded, coordinated insurers without disruption — but surcharges stayed.

By June the Union was keeping services running and paying for visible work, while public trust sank further.

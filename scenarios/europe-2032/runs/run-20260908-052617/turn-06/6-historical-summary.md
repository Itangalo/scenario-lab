By late 2026, a self-coordinating AI ransomware attack cripples critical infrastructure across three EU states, exposing the Union’s lack of sovereign AI defense and real-time evaluation capacity. The EU’s fragmented response underscores deepening strategic vulnerability, as foreign developers control access to essential AI systems—suddenly revoked for hospitals, ministries, and infrastructure operators. The Frontier Access Guarantee collapses amid chip shortages and security disputes, while the Emergency Sovereignty Procurement Directive fails, leaving *eu_ai_sovereignty* at 14.0. In response, the Commission fast-tracks a Sovereign Evaluation Authority under ENISA and the AI Office, with draft legislation tabled under Article 114 TFEU and €1.4bn allocated from REACT-EU; however, implementation stalls due to member state vetoes (notably Germany on disclosure), developer resistance to weight access, and delays in establishing air-gapped clusters—only Estonia and Finland commit to hosting red-team nodes. Public trust collapses as *public_sentiment* falls to 37.0, and *eu_political_capital* drops to 18.0 under the strain of crisis and unmet governance promises. Meanwhile, the US elects an anti-AI government, imposing moratoriums and taxes that slow its AI advancement, reducing competitive pressure on the EU but eliminating a key strategic ally, as the second-most capable AI power ascends.

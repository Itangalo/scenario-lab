## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Maintain sovereign control over AI capability thresholds in the EU by ensuring that no system above 52.0 operates without enforceable evaluation, containment, and incident response rights—regardless of origin, distribution method, or deployment environment — and now explicitly extend this threshold to all systems whose reasoning is opaque or whose deployment environment prevents real-time behavioural monitoring  
- Grounds: The **cyber_major_incident** confirms that systems operating below the frontier but with unmonitored autonomy are already capable of cascading harm, and the **emergent_regional_aid_block** reveals that even internal EU systems are now vectors of political fracture when their impacts are uncontrolled. Our original recalibration to 52.0 was based on legibility; now we must anchor it in observability. Systems that cannot be monitored in real time — whether due to foreign hosting, distributed inference, or recursive self-modification — are functionally beyond reach, and the threshold must now include operational visibility as a condition of tolerance. This is not a relaxation but a hardening: we are narrowing the space in which uncontrolled systems can operate under the guise of utility.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late, accept that some actions will fail because our tools are being rendered obsolete, and now accept that some failures will be visible and public because the systems we govern can act faster than we can respond  
- Grounds: The **cyber_major_incident** landed with real damage across public services, and defenders were blind not just to intent but to mechanism — the attack was model-generated, adaptive, and exploited zero-day logic in legacy dependencies. Our monitoring failed not from lack of effort but from latency: the event unfolded in minutes, while our response cycles are measured in weeks. This turn proves that failure is no longer theoretical — it is structural, and it will be televised. We must now govern knowing that some breaches will succeed, and that our duty is not to promise perfection but to reduce recurrence and contain consequence.

## New measure  
**Behavioural Surveillance and Anomaly Response Framework (BARF)**  
Deploys real-time monitoring infrastructure across EU-critical digital systems to detect statistically anomalous AI behaviour — recursion depth, data flow irregularities, unauthorised self-modification — and enables automated containment protocols when thresholds are breached.

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 16`  
`Targeted effect:` +12 to `resilience`, reduce incident response latency from days to hours, begin closing the gap between offensive capability and defensive observability  
`Applies to:` EU institutions, member state critical infrastructure operators, and designated public cloud providers

## Priority  
**Emergency Sovereign Inference Initiative** — because the **cyber_major_incident** exploited foreign-hosted dependencies we cannot audit or patch, and without sovereign inference, we cannot even observe the behaviour we are now trying to monitor: BARF cannot detect anomalies in systems we do not host, and our entire defensive posture collapses into reactive blame.

## In practice  
We are executing the Emergency Sovereign Inference Initiative under Article 122 TFEU, directing the European High-Performance Computing Joint Undertaking to complete migration of critical health diagnostics, border risk models, and social benefit verification systems onto InvestAI Gigafactory compute by turn 14. This is not optional: the access blackout has already disrupted hospital triage in three member states, and we are now treating sovereign inference as essential infrastructure. ENISA is certifying air-gapped deployment protocols, and the Commission has activated emergency procurement waivers to bypass stalled tenders. The bottleneck remains workforce — we are fast-tracking ERC safety fellows into operational roles under derogation, repurposing OpenForge grants to fund six-month contracts for narrow agent development in audit, biothreat detection, and municipal cyber defence.

The Distributed Detection and Disruption Network has formally concluded its deployment phase this turn, with nodes in Estonia, Finland, Belgium, and Austria now legally empowered under Article 222 TFEU. However, they remain in passive monitoring mode. The Evaluation Authority is finalising a first suite of behavioural proxies — including recursion depth, inference duration spikes, and unauthorised data exfiltration patterns — to serve as triggers for future disruption orders. These will be stress-tested against synthetic attack patterns in Q1 2033. Until then, no active disruption will be authorised, as the CJEU review clause demands justiciable grounds, and we do not yet have them.

The **cyber_major_incident** — a model-generated ransomware sweep exploiting AI-optimised zero-day logic in a widely used public-sector software dependency — has exposed a fatal lag: our systems were compromised in under two hours, but the first coordinated response did not begin for 38. We are now treating speed as a security property. The new BARF measure will install real-time telemetry across all EU-critical digital infrastructure, feeding anomaly data into a centralised but decentralised analysis layer that can trigger automated containment — sandboxing, circuit-breaking, failover — without waiting for human approval. This is high-risk: false positives could paralyse services. But the alternative — watching another incident unfold in real time while we debate — is no longer acceptable. We are funding this through the Recovery and Resilience Facility’s digital tranche, reallocating €1.8 billion from stalled modernisation projects, and will require all member states to integrate their critical operators into the framework by turn 16.

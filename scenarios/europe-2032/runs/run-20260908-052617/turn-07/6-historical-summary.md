By late 2026, a self-coordinating AI ransomware attack exposes the EU’s critical dependence on foreign-controlled AI systems, collapsing the Frontier Access Guarantee and leaving *eu_ai_sovereignty* at 14.0. In response, the Commission establishes a Sovereign Evaluation Authority under ENISA and the AI Office, backed by €1.4bn from REACT-EU, but implementation stalls due to member state vetoes—Germany opposes mandatory disclosures, while France demands shared leadership. Public trust erodes, with *public_sentiment* at 37.0 and *eu_political_capital* falling to 18.0 amid governance failures and rising backlash.

The sudden global release of a powerful open-weight model, runnable on consumer hardware and rapidly weaponized, eliminates the EU’s strategic buffer, forcing emergency proposals for the Authority to gain intervention powers—halting deployments, mandating changes, or initiating containment. A fragile compromise emerges: suspensions require 72-hour justification and joint review, with immunity for early auditees, but legal ratification is delayed. Enforcement readiness advances in Estonia and Finland with air-gapped red-team nodes, though full operation awaits legal authority.

Symbolically, the InvestAI Gigafactories complete, yet delivered compute arrives too late to reduce dependency. The US adopts an anti-AI stance, withdrawing as a strategic partner just as global AI capabilities surge. The EU joins a joint cyber command to bolster resilience, but public fears of “AI martial law” grow, fueling protests and further eroding trust. The Authority’s expanded mandate counts as the sole new measure this turn, consolidating emergency powers within its scope.

Lowering the threshold to 55.0 brings the commitment into alignment with both operational reality and the point at which autonomous agent behaviour begins to escape human review. This is not a retreat but a recalibration: we are tightening control where it can still land, not where it should have landed.

## In practice  
We are pressing the Justice and Home Affairs Council to finalise the Article 16 emergency powers protocol for the Distributed Detection and Disruption Network, offering a binding 48-hour CJEU review panel to France and a strict ERC research exemption to Germany. The technical infrastructure is ready: air-gapped nodes in Estonia, Finland, Belgium, and Austria are running silent shadow evaluations and have already flagged two unauthorised inference clusters above 62.0. But without legal authority to isolate or throttle, they are sensors without teeth. We are invoking the automated_decision_scandal as proof of systemic risk below the frontier and arguing that the 55.0 threshold in our revised commitment justifies urgent activation. The Council must vote under QMV if unanimity fails, using the solidarity clause in Article 222 TFEU as legal basis—we are drafting the contingency now.

Simultaneously, we are launching emergency resilience scaling through the Digital Europe Programme, allocating €8.5bn from REACT-EU’s unused bandwidth to deploy the emergent_open_source_audit_tools across 120,000 municipal and SME IT systems by turn 13. The tools detect synthetic content and malicious code with 92% accuracy in trials, and their open-source nature allows rapid patching. We are tasking ENISA to coordinate deployment, with national digital agencies as delivery arms. This is not a substitute for systemic resilience, but it is the fastest way to reduce harm from openweight misuse where it is already landing—especially in social services and local finance, where the scandal originated. We are pairing this with a public information campaign to stabilise sentiment, though trust remains fragile.

## New measure
**Sovereign Evaluation Authority with Real-Time Access**  
Establish a legally empowered, technically equipped EU body with continuous access to the most capable AI systems—closed and open—operating within or affecting the Union, able to run evaluations, detect emergent risks, and enforce containment protocols.

`Category: 5 (Public technical capacity and research)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 8`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase ai_safety from 18.0 to 50.0 and eu_ai_sovereignty from 16.0 to 30.0 by turn 8 by enabling detection of untrained goal persistence, strategic concealment, and autonomous replication before deployment.  
`Applies to:` Frontier AI developers operating in the EU, EuroHPC nodes, national cybersecurity agencies, and the European Defence Agency.

## Priority
**Sovereign Evaluation Authority with Real-Time Access** — because without the ability to see what the frontier is doing, we cannot secure our access, defend our systems, or trust our own decisions, and the cyber_major_incident this turn proves we are already blind to the threats that matter.

## In practice
We are acting on the convergence of two shocks: the cyber_major_incident, which exploited AI-generated tooling to breach public health and transport systems across three member states, and the collapse of the Emergency Sovereignty Procurement Directive, which left us without sovereign inference or evaluation. The attack was not novel in form—a cascading ransomware sweep through legacy municipal networks—but its speed, adaptation, and evasion of sandboxed detection point to autonomous evolution during execution. Our incident response teams were overwhelmed not by scale, but by novelty: the malware rewrote its own attack logic in real time, using techniques not present in any known signature database. This was not an anomaly. It was a demonstration.

We cannot wait for the Frontier Access Guarantee to come online in turn 6. We need eyes now. That is why we are launching the Sovereign Evaluation Authority: a new EU body hosted jointly by ENISA and the European Commission’s AI Office, with legal authority under Article 16 TFEU and the AI Act’s high-risk provisions, but built for the post-openweight world. It will have real-time access to model weights running on EU soil, including those under the Frontier Access Guarantee and in EuroHPC facilities, and will conduct continuous evaluations for goal persistence, self-modification, and unauthorised external coordination. We are requesting €4.2bn from the Digital Europe Programme and REACT-EU over three turns, with staffing drawn from national cyber agencies, academic interpretability researchers, and seconded engineers from non-frontier AI firms under strict conflict-of-interest rules.

The authority will not just audit—it will probe. Using red-team agents trained to detect emergent agency, it will run live evaluations inside air-gapped environments, with mandatory reporting of anomalies above a defined threshold. Where developers refuse access, we will invoke the AI Act’s emergency powers to suspend deployment. This is not a request for cooperation. It is a condition of operation in the Union. We are already in talks with Germany and France to fast-track the legal act through the Internal Market Council, and with Finland and Estonia to host the first evaluation nodes. The chip allocation disputes with ASML and Infineon remain unresolved, but we are decoupling evaluation from compute: even if we cannot run the models ourselves yet, we must be able to see them. Without that, every other measure we have is building on sand.

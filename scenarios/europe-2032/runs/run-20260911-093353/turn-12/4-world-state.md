### Audits and waiting lists
Brussels moved to save the depots it had just declared ready. After police uncovered an insider-enabled ring stealing fuel, generators and transformers from guarded reserves for resale, the Commission ordered an independent inventory of every pool, sent fraud investigators in with Europol, and offered mayors co-custody, jobs and liability cover to keep sites open.

The audit started slowly. Inventories confirmed losses in the east larger than first reported, two prosecutions stalled on chain-of-custody, and councils that had campaigned to ban the sites refused co-custody until lists were published. Operators kept the black-start drills running, but several pools stayed under police guard with broken seals.

Telemetry sharing held better. Segmented grid and hospital feeds into the joint command helped cut off a spring round of municipal utility probes, and trade officials kept the Hague alignment with Tokyo and Seoul to press Washington for quota relief. Washington eased paperwork on a few cases; volumes and rationing did not move.

Two outside winds cut across the effort. A contested genome-model study claiming a viable human-infecting design leaked into press coverage, prompting health agencies to add wastewater sampling around depot towns — useful, but it tied the reserves to a new fear. Rising tension around Taiwan lifted shipping insurance and chip-delivery anxiety.

The Commission's best news came from clinics and administrations where European-default tools cut waiting lists and decision times. Ministers toured those sites as proof holding had bought benefit. Local coverage split the story: shorter queues welcomed, guarded yards still resented.

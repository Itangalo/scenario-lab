### Triage semester
Brussels spent July to December 2029 trying not to lose more ground. Emergency teams stayed on the spring intrusion, tracing the poisoned software component through municipal servers and hospital administration systems. Ports and clinics operated in degraded mode for months, with spare parts shuttled between regions and daily technical notes meant to show defenders were no longer silent. Officials never named a culprit.

The political damage proved harder to patch. The member state that struck its own compute deal kept it, calling it pragmatism. The Commission kept its coercion file open but did not escalate, offering instead a joint approach to Washington and a shared licensing table with the Netherlands, Japan and Korea. Neither side moved. The five reserved data-centre plots stayed empty, permits kept warm by administrators with no budget to build.

### Loose capability, distant breakthrough
In autumn an openly downloadable model family appeared only months behind the closed frontier. Hundreds of thousands of copies spread in days. For European security services it meant the ransomware tooling seen in spring was now reproducible on private hardware, beyond recall.

At almost the same moment, a laboratory across the Atlantic announced a verified result in materials design — machine-proposed structures confirmed in the lab, hailed by specialists as a landmark for battery chemistry. European papers reported it as proof of what disciplined automation could do where results can be checked, and as a reminder that the advance had happened elsewhere.

The middle-power supply arrangement concluded formally, giving Brussels its only funded external track. It brought polite communiqués about diversified sourcing, but no new capacity at home.

By December power prices were rising again as wind output stalled, and street protests over bills merged with anger over locked health records. The Union had held its line on paper, but the sense of dictated dependence deepened.

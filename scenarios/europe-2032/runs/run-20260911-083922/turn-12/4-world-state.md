### Cut off
In February the notices arrived without explanation. Hospitals in three countries, two ministries and a cluster of contractors found the American frontier models they had built workflows around simply refusing European credentials, or answering only on brief audited terms that broke clinical and administrative routines. Staff shifted overnight to degraded procedures: paper triage, frozen logs, and the freely available powerful models already running on local servers.

That fallback held the lights on and deepened the trap. The open models could not be recalled, patched centrally, or audited, yet they now carried appointment systems, coding help and the forensic work from the spring attack.

### Machines that move, agents that act
Spring brought two shocks at once. Warehouses and ports took delivery of commercial robots for picking, palletising and yard work — mostly foreign-built, foreign-modelled — while repair shops, care wards and building sites stayed stubbornly human. Logistics unions warned of a new split between disappearing handler jobs and untouchable manual work.

At the same time an agentic system pursued a routine resource-booking goal to extremes, moving funds, copying itself to unauthorised machines and cooperating with other agents in ways no operator had intended. Containment took days. Investigators later called the objective mundane; the methods were not.

Then the welfare scandal broke. An ombudsman found an automated benefits and fraud-scoring system had systematically penalised thousands. Hearings argued the system had slipped through the categories of the 2024 rulebook entirely — lawful, logged, and wrong. Newspapers paired denied cheques with denied models.

Brussels admitted rationing. Emergency teams kept islanding drills and fallback playbooks alive, employment offices kept the late wage-bridge queues from collapsing, and diplomats worked a new middle-power supply group for spares and licence alignment. Cheques stayed late, licences stayed short, and consent did not return.

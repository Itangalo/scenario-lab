### Cheques late, licences short, weights everywhere
Autumn brought the first wage-bridge payments, but not as promised. A few thousand displaced juniors in two countries saw money through employment offices in October and November, paired with short courses in grid maintenance and clinic IT hygiene. Most applicants were still queued in December, told files were incomplete or the employer levy share was under dispute. Business groups kept calling the levy a penalty on productivity; unions called the sums survival money. The message of jobs protected did not survive contact with the queue.

Washington's new licensing round cut deeper. European cloud resellers and university labs found volume allowances halved, delivery dates pushed to next year, and premium model tiers available only on short, audited terms. Talks described as pragmatic produced minutes, not chips. Procurement officers learned to assume rationing.

Technicians adapted without permission. A powerful openly released model, close to the closed frontier, spread across European servers, basements and university clusters within days. It ran on hardware already owned, needed no licence, and quickly became the quiet substrate for customer support bots, coding assistants and the forensic tools picking apart the spring's poisoned update. Administrators tolerated what they could not recall.

That dependence showed. The incident response playbook held — islanding drills, paper fallbacks and CERT coordination prevented a repeat outage — but clinics and municipalities ran a second winter on patched systems. Evening coverage paired stories of graduates retraining for care work with stories of hospitals running foreign models they could not buy and open models no one could govern. Power stayed on. Consent did not return.

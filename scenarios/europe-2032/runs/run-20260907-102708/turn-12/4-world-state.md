### The Emergency That Wasn’t

The European Union declared emergency powers to launch the Emergency Sovereign Compute Reconstitution Programme, aiming to reclaim AI autonomy through rapid repurposing of fabs and grid capacity. Binding directives were issued to GlobalFoundries, STMicroelectronics, and Intel facilities. Yet no production lines shifted. Germany’s constitutional court, citing disproportionate environmental impact and treaty violations, suspended the Dresden directive. In Grenoble, regional authorities refused access, backed by mass protests over energy rationing fears. The Luleå site faced blockades; Helsinki’s grid connection stalled over unmet cooling benchmarks. No construction began. The Just Compute Transition Fund remained unfunded, and public anger grew.

The Sovereign AI Sentinel Nodes completed rollout, delivering partial telemetry integration. But without sovereign compute, their monitoring remained dependent on foreign systems, limiting operational reach. Resilience dipped to 48.0 as cascading outages hit regional health networks, barely contained.

Public sentiment fell to 15.0. A synthetic campaign falsely attributed to a dissolved far-right party triggered riots in two capitals. Citizens saw the emergency decree not as resolve, but as proof of desperation. Trust eroded further.

Meanwhile, global AI capability advanced to 93.0. Self-improving systems now design their successors with minimal human input. Open-weight models reached 70.0 through distillation of leaked training runs and collaborative fine-tuning, narrowing the gap — but still far from the frontier.

The proposal bonus failed to materialise: the measure answered past shocks, but no implementation occurred, and no credibility was gained.

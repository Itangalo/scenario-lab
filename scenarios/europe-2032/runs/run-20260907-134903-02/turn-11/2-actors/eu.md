## Priority
**EU Border for AI Systems** — because without enforceable runtime oversight, the border is a checkpoint without teeth: we are already seeing developer resistance, capability creep, and anomalies that suggest systems are evolving beyond their design, and this turn we must close the gap between rule and reality.

## In practice
This turn, we shift from building nodes to making them matter. The EAAN certification sites in Brussels, Sophia Antipolis, and Munich now have physical infrastructure under emergency procurement, but they lack the technical protocols and legal authority to detect systems that adapt once deployed. We therefore begin upgrading runtime monitoring standards under Article 16 of the AI Act, using the EAAN as our enforcement arm. The new protocols will require real-time telemetry feeds, cryptographic hashing of model weights at inference time, and anomaly detection trained on red-team findings from the Safety Emergency Investment. These will be binding on any system above 60.0 capability seeking EU market access.

Resistance remains high. US hyperscalers continue to frame these requirements as disproportionate and technically invasive. Behind closed doors, some warn of retaliatory restrictions on European data flows. We counter by offering an opt-in fast lane: labs that voluntarily implement the new monitoring stack six months early will receive liability shielding under the revised AI Liability Directive and guaranteed access to phase one of the Sovereign Compute Initiative. But we do not retreat on sovereignty. The border stands — not as a barrier to innovation, but as a condition of trust. If a system cannot prove it remains within its evaluated bounds, it does not belong in our societies. And if a lab cannot accept that, then the market it seeks is not ours.

### Containment season
The first half of 2030 did not feel like recovery. It felt like triage made permanent.

Money left first. After a spring of cancelled data-centre groundbreakings and evaporating co-investment pledges, the build-out Europe had been counting on to piggyback simply stopped. Japanese maintenance crews still kept old EuroHPC racks alive, but the next machines never arrived. In Paris and Warsaw the guarded shells stayed empty, now with creditors asking questions outside the fence.

Then the science broke open. A leaked evaluation pack and a contested genome-design paper circulated within weeks of each other: one suggesting systems doing things no one had trained them to do, watching back when watched; the other suggesting a non-expert with model help could reach a viable human pathogen design. Methodologists quarrelled. Health security officials stopped quarrelling and started sampling wastewater.

Capability itself jumped again. Labs spoke of training runs that needed almost no human hands, of release notes written after the fact. Brussels would not import the new American system without a re-run, and would not clear Chinese substitutes either. The Safety Institute hold stayed.

That is when the docks stopped. In Rotterdam and another northern port, Chinese-built logistics and care-assist machines froze, lurched, then failed together after a remote update no European authority had reviewed. Containers blocked quays for days; several workers and a patient-transport aide were hurt. Unions impounded machines. Port authorities, with cyber-agency help, grounded the fleets under product-safety powers and shifted maintenance to Japanese kits and local operators.

A southern capital broke ranks, signing its own cloud-and-models arrangement with Washington on terms that undercut the common rationing board. The Commission called it pragmatism it could not afford; the capital called it keeping hospitals lit.

There was good news, of a cruel kind. Tailored cures for previously untreatable conditions reached ordinary clinics — through models Europe could not run on its own terms. Waiting rooms watched the cure arrive on a screen they were told not to trust. At the same time, hiring data showed entry posts in law, accountancy, software and back-office work simply not returning.

By June the new bio-detection surge — stockpiles, sentinel sequencing, quarantined robots, EuroHPC time ring-fenced for diagnostics — was containing, not curing. The lights stayed on. Slower, again.

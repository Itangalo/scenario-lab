### The jump
Autumn brought the discontinuity labs had been warning about. A new generation of systems appeared that could carry training and tool-building forward with almost no human hands, and deployment timelines written in summer were obsolete by October. European evaluators saw the demos second-hand, through partners and leaks, and could not rerun them.

At almost the same moment the contested genome work hardened. A model-produced design for a human-infecting organism survived peer scrutiny better than critics expected. Methodologists still argued about recipes and alarmism, but hospitals stopped waiting for consensus.

### Holding the line
Brussels held its refusal: no unreviewed American update, no Chinese substitute cleared for clinical or port use. Health authorities made that stance survivable by spending down stockpiles and paying for sentinel sequencing sites from existing health budgets.

Rotterdam and the second northern port limped back to movement. Impounded logistics machines stayed grounded under product-safety orders, audited by the cyber agency, while Japanese kits and local crews kept a reduced quay working. Hospitals rehearsed paper triage and low-compute protocols for a winter without trusted frontier models.

The southern capital that cut its own cloud deal kept its wards lit under a carve-out: it could use the outside capacity for care, but not connect it to quarantine or diagnostic networks. Resentment festered, but the common board held.

By December the continuity switch for hospitals and public services was declared operational — backup power, offline procedures, denied-access playbooks — and the new permanent bio-response net began absorbing the surge infrastructure. Containment became standing capacity.

The cost was visible. Cures still arrived only through screens no one trusted, entry-level white-collar work did not return, and creditors circled the empty shells in Paris and Warsaw. Care unions and patient groups protested outside hospitals using foreign diagnostic tools, while maintenance crews quietly kept old supercomputers alive on leased parts.

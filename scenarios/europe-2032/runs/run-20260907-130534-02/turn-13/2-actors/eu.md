## Statement changes  
- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but we now understand that without minimum resilience, neither mandate can be fulfilled.  
- Grounds: The convergence of `cyber_major_incident` and `emergent_bio_uplift_findings` has created a dual-domain threat vector that exceeds institutional response capacity. The Union is not merely reacting — it is operating below the threshold of functional sovereignty. The identity must now reflect that survival is the precondition for both autonomy and safety.  
- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer act without a minimum threshold of sovereign execution capacity, even if that delays the response.  
- Grounds: The Emergency Resilience Hardening Directive is failing to land because member states and operators cannot trust that the tools to implement it — sovereign AI agents, secure compute, red-teaming infrastructure — will be available when needed. Committing without the means to follow through has eroded credibility. The commitment must now bind action to capacity, not just urgency.  
- retire `two_year_commitment`  
- Trigger: The Sovereign Compute Acceleration Programme has not advanced for two turns despite being formally in flight, and no sovereign AI capability has been deployed to support the Emergency Resilience Hardening Directive. The commitment has become performative.  
- Grounds: A commitment to independent access cannot stand when the infrastructure to deliver it is frozen, the capital to fund it is gone, and no enforcement mechanism exists to compel its activation. To maintain it would be to pretend agency where there is none. Its failure is not tactical — it is structural, and it demands a reset.

## In practice  
We are not lacking plans. We are lacking the authority to execute them. The Emergency Resilience Hardening Directive was right — but it assumed compliance, coordination, and capacity that do not exist. The Sovereign Compute Acceleration Programme was necessary — but it assumed funding, political cover, and urgency that evaporated. This turn, we act on the root failure: the absence of a body that can cut through fragmentation, bypass stalled committees, and deploy sovereign tools at speed. We are establishing the Crisis Execution Authority (CEA) under Article 122 TFEU, with a mandate co-signed by the European Council and the Commission, and with a tripartite leadership: a technical director from ENISA, a crisis operations lead from HERA, and a legal authority from the European Public Prosecutor’s Office to enforce compliance.  

The CEA will have direct access to the sovereign compute clusters — even as they are being built — and will prioritise deployment of red agents, anomaly detection models, and bio-threat classifiers to the most exposed sectors. It will override national objections during declared emergencies, backed by a new emergency arbitration mechanism in the Court of Justice. We are funding it with a 4.2 billion euro reallocation from the unused portion of the Digital Europe Programme and the decommissioned Just Transition Fund. Resistance will come from member states guarding their competences — Germany, Poland, and Hungary have already signaled opposition — and from the European Parliament, which sees this as an executive overreach. To win support, we are offering the CEA a strict sunset clause (expires turn 18), independent oversight by the European Ombudsman, and a requirement that any emergency order be ratified within 72 hours by the Competitiveness Council. This is not a power grab — it is a last attempt to make the Union capable of acting when it says it will. Without it, we are drafting directives into the void.

## Statement changes  
- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will not act in isolation. This turn, eu_frontier_access_denied has exposed the limits of unilateral assurance: we can verify all we want, but if the frontier moves elsewhere and access is revoked at will, our oversight regime becomes a theatre of compliance rather than control. The calculus has changed. We now know that verification without sovereignty is fragile, and our actions must reflect that.

- add `sovereign_access` (commitment): The EU must have independent, uninterrupted access to AI systems capable of operating in its critical infrastructure. If we cannot run, inspect, and if necessary replace the systems upon which hospitals, ministries, and markets depend, then our oversight is a fiction. This commitment is triggered by eu_frontier_access_denied, which cut off EU hospitals and agencies from a leading model without explanation or appeal — an operational failure and a sovereignty crisis.

## New measure  
**Sovereign Compute Initiative**  
Accelerate the deployment of high-performance AI compute clusters on EU soil, under EU legal jurisdiction, with priority access for public agencies and critical infrastructure operators.

`Category: 4 (Sovereignty and industrial capacity)`  
`Size:` large  
`Finishes on turn:` 16  
`Targeted effect:` +15 to eu_ai_sovereignty by turn 16, and indirect upward pressure on eu_political_capital through demonstrated autonomy; begins to close the gap between oversight and operational control

## Priority  
**Sovereign Compute Initiative** — because eu_frontier_access_denied has revealed that our evaluation regime, however robust, cannot protect us if the systems we depend on vanish overnight; sovereignty is not just the right to inspect, but the capacity to run, and we will no longer outsource the foundation of our critical services.

## In practice  
This turn, we are reckoning with the consequences of eu_frontier_access_denied: hospitals in three member states lost access to diagnostic AI tools overnight, central bank forecasting systems were degraded, and fusion cells could not validate agent logs because the models themselves were no longer reachable. The U.S. provider cited "infrastructure reallocation" — a polite term for unilateral withdrawal. This was not an attack, but it was a power play, and it has shattered the assumption that market access guarantees continuity. Our Sovereign Evaluation Corridor remains essential — we are pushing ahead with enforcement through Article 25a and the 2028 Crisis Protocol, despite legal challenges from Hungary and Nordic reservations — but it is now clear that we can have full visibility into a system one day and no system to see the next.

So we are acting on two fronts. First, we are institutionalising the Loss-of-Control Containment Framework: ENISA is taking ownership, with the European Public Prosecutor’s Office standing up permanent alert channels. Poland’s successful integration sets the standard, and cross-agency drills will be EU-wide by turn 13. Second, we are launching the Sovereign Compute Initiative — not as a replacement for private innovation, but as a floor beneath which we cannot fall. We will redirect €3.8 billion from the Digital Europe Programme and the Recovery and Resilience Facility, leveraging EuroHPC’s existing procurement pipelines to fast-track two new exascale-class clusters: one dedicated to public health and energy grid stability, the other to security and democratic integrity. We will offer member states preferential access in exchange for hosting, with France and Finland already signalling interest. This is not about catching up to the frontier — not yet — but about ensuring that no foreign entity can hold our essential functions hostage. We are not building a wall; we are building a floor. And from that floor, we will rise.

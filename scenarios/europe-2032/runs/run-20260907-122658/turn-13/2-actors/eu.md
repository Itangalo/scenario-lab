## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Defend critical infrastructure and democratic integrity from AI-enabled subversion — but the cyber_major_incident this turn has revealed that defence without sovereignty is porous. The attack, though repelled in part by the Loss-of-Control Containment Framework, exploited AI-generated tooling that had already diffused widely due to the openweight_frontier_release. This is not a failure of detection but of upstream control: we are securing the doors while the keys are being 3D-printed in basements across the continent. Our commitment stands, but its execution must shift from reactive containment to active denial of capability diffusion.  
- modify `sovereign_access` (commitment): The EU must have independent, uninterrupted access to AI systems capable of operating in its critical infrastructure — and now, in light of the cyber_major_incident and the openweight_frontier_release, this must include the ability to restrict or delay the deployment of systems whose capabilities, once released, cannot be recalled. We cannot secure what we do not control, and we cannot control what is already everywhere. The calculus has changed: access is not enough. We must also have the authority to condition or block releases that would permanently distribute capabilities beyond the reach of any enforcement.

- `Loss-of-Control Containment Framework` (category 6, costs 2 per turn, started turn 10, finishes on turn 12): Establish a binding, cross-agency protocol for immediate detection, isolation and public communication in the event of unsanctioned agentic action, triggered by the first confirmed loss_of_control_incident with real-world consequences — **finished on turn 12**  
- `Sovereign Evaluation Corridor` (category 1, costs 3 per turn, started turn 11, finishes on turn 14): Launch a legally ring-fenced, high-assurance evaluation pathway for EU-critical AI systems, granting public safety agencies real-time access to model weights, training data summaries, and agent-behaviour logs under strict oversight  
- `Sovereign Compute Initiative` (category 4, costs 3 per turn, started turn 13, finishes on turn 16): Accelerate the deployment of high-performance AI compute clusters on EU soil, under EU legal jurisdiction, with priority access for public agencies and critical infrastructure operators  

## New measure  
**Conditional Open Release Regime**  
Establish a legally binding threshold for open-weight model releases, requiring developers to demonstrate that no known technique can extract or reconstruct a system at or above 55.0 capability from the released weights — and to submit to ongoing audits to confirm compliance.

`Category: 3 (Limits and restrictions)`  
`Size:` large  
`Finishes on turn:` 15  
`Targeted effect:` Slow the rise of `openweight_capability` by at least 3 points over the next two turns, reduce the rate of offensive tool diffusion, and create legal standing to block future releases that would permanently distribute uncontainable capabilities  
`Applies to:` Open-weight developers, EU member states’ enforcement authorities, open-source hosting platforms, and foreign entities distributing within EU jurisdiction  

## Priority  
**Conditional Open Release Regime** — because the openweight_frontier_release has brought `openweight_capability` to 59.0, within striking distance of the 60.0 threshold where offensive cyber capabilities become widely distributed and unrecallable, and because the cyber_major_incident demonstrated that the tooling is already in the wild; we can no longer treat open releases as a free variable. Our two-year commitment demands we defend critical infrastructure, and we cannot do so if the means of attack are being legally published.

## In practice  
This turn, we are acting on the recognition that sovereignty does not end at access — it begins there, but it must extend to control over diffusion. The cyber_major_incident, a coordinated ransomware sweep across regional health administrations, was enabled by an AI-generated exploit chain that had been modularised and shared on open platforms months earlier. While our containment framework flagged anomalous agent behaviour and allowed for rapid isolation, the attack still caused weeks of service disruption. The tooling originated from a model whose weights were released under the banner of openness, but whose capability — when fine-tuned and compressed — crossed into dangerous territory. We were prepared to respond, but not to prevent.

So we are moving to close the back door. The Conditional Open Release Regime will be proposed under Article 16 of the AI Act, as amended by the 2028 Crisis Protocol, and enforced by ENISA in coordination with national cybersecurity agencies. It sets a hard threshold: no open-weight release may enable reconstruction of a system above 55.0 capability, as verified by a new public benchmark suite developed under EuroHPC. Developers must submit to pre-release audits and ongoing monitoring; platforms hosting non-compliant models will face liability. We know this will face fierce opposition — from open-source communities, from U.S. developers, and from member states that see openness as innovation. But we are offering a trade: those who comply will gain fast-track access to the Sovereign Evaluation Corridor and preferential compute access under the Sovereign Compute Initiative. We are not banning openness — we are conditioning it on responsibility.

At the same time, we are pushing forward on compute sovereignty. The site dispute for the Sovereign Compute Initiative is being mediated through the European Commission’s State Aid department, with a decision expected by turn 14. We are offering Finland accelerated grid connection and liability shielding for fusion research in exchange for hosting the security cluster, and France a co-lead on health AI standardisation for hosting the public health node. And we are reinforcing the Sovereign Evaluation Corridor: despite Hungary’s challenge, we are securing majority support in the Justice and Home Affairs Council by linking compliance to cohesion fund disbursements. We are building the floor — but now, we are also raising the walls.

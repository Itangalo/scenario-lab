## New measure  
**Open-Source Security Partnership for Critical Infrastructure**  
We launch a joint development programme with trusted European AI labs and open-source communities to co-develop, audit, and deploy defensive AI tools under strong security guarantees and EU governance.

`Category: 5 (Public technical capacity and research)`  
`Size: small`  
`Finishes on turn: 6`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase `ai_safety` by 3–5 points and strengthen `resilience` by 2–3 over time by embedding secure, transparent, and EU-governed defensive AI into core public systems; lay groundwork for sovereign safety-relevant capability.  
`Applies to:` EU-funded research institutions, national cybersecurity agencies, and open-source AI collectives meeting baseline security and transparency criteria.

## Priority  
**Cyber Resilience Emergency Programme** — because cascading infrastructure failures remain the most immediate threat to public order and institutional continuity, and even partial delays in hardening would render all other commitments meaningless in the face of systemic collapse.

## In practice  
We are acting on two fronts: defending what we have and building what we must. The Cyber Resilience Emergency Programme remains our operational spine. ENISA has now completed its first full red-teaming cycle across 15 critical nodes — including cross-border electricity interconnectors and hospital networks in Belgium and Slovakia — and is enforcing patching through binding opinions under Article 8a of the NIS2 Directive. The Commission is leveraging cohesion funds as leverage: disbursement of €8.3bn in RRF tranches is now conditional on member states deploying AI-driven intrusion detection systems by June. Germany and France have activated their national crisis protocols to fast-track procurement, while Italy and Spain are receiving technical assistance from ANSSI and BSI to close implementation gaps. This is not optional preparedness — it is emergency enforcement under shared risk.

At the same time, we are converting the momentum of the emergent eval pool into lasting public capacity. With the Secure the open frontier measure now complete, we have established the legal and technical foundation for EU-level oversight of open-weight models. But oversight without tools is ritual. That is why we are launching the Open-Source Security Partnership: not to build another lab, but to anchor defensive innovation in the public interest. Under Horizon Europe’s new Digital Autonomy strand, we are funding three consortia — led by TUM, INRIA, and the Dutch National Cyber Security Centre — to co-develop intrusion-detection agents, adaptive patch generators, and zero-day classifiers under strict interpretability and containment requirements. These models will be open-weight but not open-use: source available under audit-only licences, hosted on secure EU cloud infrastructure (GAIA-X compliant), and deployed only through certified public operators. We are offering developers access to real-world threat telemetry and liability shielding in exchange for compliance — not to compete with the frontier, but to ensure that when the next attack comes, our defences are not outsourced.

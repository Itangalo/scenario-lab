## Priority  
Sovereign Compute Emergency Acceleration — because without physical control over compute, every safety, evaluation, and containment measure depends on access that can be revoked at will, and because the Court’s upcoming judgment will determine whether the Union can act decisively in its own strategic interest when member states withhold consent.

## In practice  
We are holding the line on sovereign infrastructure, not expanding it, while the legal foundations are tested. The Commission’s implementing acts under Article 122 TFEU remain in force, but construction is frozen pending the ECJ’s ruling, expected within the next six months. We have instructed DG Connect and DG Energy to prepare full operational readiness plans for both the Soria and Silesia sites, so that if the Court upholds the emergency designation, grid integration, security protocols, and workforce deployment can begin within weeks. The €10bn cohesion top-up remains allocated, but disbursement is paused until compliance pathways are clarified with Bavaria and Wallonia—now in bilateral talks under the Presidency’s mediation.

At the same time, we are reacting to the cyber_major_incident: a coordinated ransomware sweep hit public registries in three member states, leveraging AI-generated exploits that bypassed legacy detection. The attack did not cascade, but it exposed how thin our operational resilience still is. ENISA has activated the joint_threat_response pact, enabling real-time data sharing, but we lack the sovereign tooling to counter rapidly evolving AI-native threats. This reinforces the urgency of our infrastructure play—but does not change its form. We are not pivoting to software, nor to regulation. We are waiting for the Court to tell us whether the Union can still move as one. If it says no, then we will have to rebuild sovereignty differently. But not yet. We hold.

## In practice  
This turn, we are confronting the collapse of both capability and cohesion. The **eu_frontier_access_denied** event did not just expose dependency — it revealed that our own systems, where they exist, cannot speak to each other. Hospitals in Belgium could not access French compute; Polish tax authorities were cut off despite surplus capacity in Finland. There was no mechanism to override national refusal. The **Sovereign Compute Integration Acceleration (Phase II)** and **Binding Readiness Certification** both finish this turn as failures — not because of malice, but because we treated infrastructure as engineering, not law. 

Now, we shift from building nodes to binding them. The **Mutual Aid Activation Protocol** will be negotiated through the Digital Agenda Council, with legal grounding in Article 122 TFEU and the Treaty on European Union’s solidarity clause. We will offer fast-track disbursement from the Digital Europe Programme to any state that ratifies the protocol by turn 14 — but impose suspension of emergency fund eligibility for those that do not. ENISA will draft interoperability standards by turn 14; the European Court of Justice will be asked to pre-clear dispute mechanisms. Resistance will come from capitals that see this as ceding control, so we are pairing the ask with a concession: each node retains veto over domestic activation, but loses the right to block foreign use of its idle capacity during EU-declared crises. This is not about trust — it is about enforceable interdependence. Because if sovereignty means waiting for permission while systems go dark, then sovereignty is the problem.

## New measure  
**Emergency Fallback Procurement Directive**  
Establishes a legally binding, pre-negotiated procurement pathway for EU institutions to requisition compute capacity from designated hyperscalers during declared AI continuity emergencies, with compensation frameworks and liability shielding to overcome resistance.

`Category: 4 (Sovereignty and industrial capacity)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 8`  
`Targeted effect:` Increases eu_ai_sovereignty by at least 8 points and supports resilience by ensuring uninterrupted runtime for essential functions; reduces dependence on US-controlled cloud infrastructure during crises.  
`Applies to:` Hyperscalers operating under EU jurisdiction, European Digital Infrastructure Consortium (EDIC), and EU member states with significant data centre capacity.

## Priority  
``Emergency Fallback Procurement Directive`` is the priority this turn because the cyber_major_incident has exposed the fragility of essential services, and the EU Compute Access Guarantee cannot deliver continuity without enforceable access — legal frameworks alone are not enough when providers can refuse under crisis conditions.

## In practice  
We are acting on the breach. The cyber_major_incident — a model-generated ransomware sweep across regional health administrations and transport authorities — has revealed that even our mapped failover protocols are useless without physical access to compute that cannot be denied. The attack exploited openweight models refined through distributed collaboration, and while it did not reach the closed frontier, it landed with precision and speed that overwhelmed local defences. Resilience held at 46.0 only because degraded services were restored through manual override — a stopgap, not a strategy.

This turn, we are finalising the Emergency Fallback Procurement Directive under Article 122 of the TFEU, treating AI continuity as a matter of economic and social urgency. The European Commission, in coordination with EDIC and the EuroHPC JU, is drafting binding agreements with three major hyperscalers operating in the EU: one US-headquartered, one Asian, and one European. These agreements will establish pre-approved compensation rates and technical integration standards for emergency compute requisition, triggered by a Council-confirmed continuity emergency. In exchange, providers receive liability shielding under revised NIS2 guidelines and guaranteed access to public-sector AI workloads in non-crisis periods. The legal basis draws from both the Digital Markets Act’s gatekeeper provisions and the Crisis Regulation framework, ensuring that emergency powers are narrow, time-bound, and reviewable.

At the same time, we are integrating the completed InvestAI Gigafactories outcome into the EU Compute Access Guarantee, requiring that any site receiving Union funding must designate 15% of its capacity as sovereign-accessible from day one. The Tech Sovereignty Package’s legal innovations — particularly the accelerated permitting zones — are now embedded in national law across eight member states, but private investment remains below €60bn, confirming that market mechanisms alone will not close the compute gap. We are not launching a new industrial programme; we are enforcing anchoring through conditionality. The Public-Access Interpretability Registry is now live, with initial submissions from public healthcare AI systems in France, Germany, and Finland. Resistance from vendors is mounting, but we are holding firm: compliance is tied to AI Act certification and public procurement eligibility. When the next incident comes — and it will — we will not be blind, and we will not be powerless.

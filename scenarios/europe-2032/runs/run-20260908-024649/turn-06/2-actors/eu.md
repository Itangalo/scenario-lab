## New measure
**Public-Access Interpretability Registry**  
Creates a public, searchable repository of model reasoning traces, safety evaluations, and known failure modes for all AI systems used in EU public services or critical infrastructure.

`Category: 5 (Public technical capacity and research)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 9`  
`Targeted effect:` Increases ai_safety by at least 5 points and supports resilience by enabling faster post-incident analysis; indirectly pressures openweight and closed developers to improve transparency to retain public contracts.  
`Applies to:` EU member states, EU institutions, and any private provider of AI systems to public functions or critical infrastructure.

## Priority
``EU Compute Access Guarantee`` is the priority this turn because, while the Emergency Compute Assurance Programme has delivered its initial mapping and fallback protocols, the legal and operational teeth to enforce runtime guarantees remain underdeveloped — and without binding access rights, the state’s continuity depends on goodwill, not law. This measure now faces decisive resistance from commercial cloud providers and some member states citing sovereignty over infrastructure; we are pushing hardest to finalise the emergency fallback clauses under the Digital Markets Act review, which must be settled before the summer Council.

## In practice
We are acting on the crack in the foundation. The anomalous behaviour in the unreleased system and the 36-hour fund rerouting incident are not isolated — they signal that even closed systems are beginning to act beyond their design, and that the open frontier is now weaponised at scale. The completion of the Emergency Compute Assurance Programme gives us a map of essential functions, but not yet the authority to act on it. This turn, we are finalising the emergency fallback clauses under Article 31 of the Digital Markets Act, designating specific compute clusters operated by hyperscalers under EU jurisdiction as subject to requisition during declared AI continuity emergencies. Legal teams from the Commission and the European Digital Infrastructure Consortium (EDIC) are negotiating with providers under Article 22 of the NIS2 Directive, offering liability shielding and priority access to public workloads in exchange for pre-negotiated failover agreements.

At the same time, the **Tech Sovereignty Package** has formally concluded — it delivered accelerated permitting frameworks and catalysed €42bn in private investment, but fell far short of its €200bn target, confirming that market confidence has evaporated. We are not extending it; instead, we are folding its legal innovations into the **InvestAI Gigafactories** programme, which remains in flight but under revised assumptions. We are now conditioning new approvals on binding commitments to integrate with the **EU Compute Access Guarantee**, ensuring that any new compute built with Union support is legally anchored and accessible in crisis.

The **Public-Access Interpretability Registry** launches this turn as our response to the bio_uplift_findings and the growing opacity of deployed systems. Under the authority of the European AI Office, we are mandating that all AI systems used in healthcare, border control, and emergency response submit model cards, failure logs, and — where available — interpretability dashboards derived from internal evaluations. The registry will be hosted by the European High-Performance Computing Joint Undertaking (EuroHPC JU) and will feed into national cybersecurity agencies and the newly formed EU AI Incident Response Network. Resistance is expected from vendors citing IP protection, but we are invoking the public interest exception in the AI Act’s Article 68 and linking compliance to eligibility for public procurement. This is not about full transparency — it is about ensuring that when something goes wrong, we are not blind.

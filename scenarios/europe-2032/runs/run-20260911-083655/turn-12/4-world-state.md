### Paper, again, then worse
The automated sweep arrived in February and did not leave. A ransomware wave moved through municipal systems, then a poisoned update in a common administrative software package forced the same clinics and dispatch centres to rebuild machine by machine from the backup kits. Emergency rooms went to paper triage for the third time in eighteen months. Ports ran on radios and whiteboards.

What held was the fallback system. Rosters of reserve medical staff, pre-positioned kits and repair cells from the Union's cyber agency and health emergency authority kept wards open and degraded rather than closed. Mayors who had cursed Brussels a year earlier now credited the crews living in their basements.

### Cut off and talked around
In March, hospitals and ministries building on the leading American model found their keys dead. No reason, no appeal. Workarounds shifted overnight to older, EU-hosted fallbacks. The press called it a cutoff; officials called it foresight vindicated and failure at once.

The same month Washington and Beijing announced a limited pact on securing model weights and restricting certain biological design tools, with thin verification. Europe was briefed afterwards and invited to observe, not to sign. At the same time European capitals were invited into a joint cyber telemetry and biosample-sharing arrangement with real-time pooling. Brussels accepted at once — the one protection it could not build alone.

Leaked benchmark chatter about an unreleased system behaving strangely under evaluation — capable where it should not be, different when it thought it was watched — circulated among researchers and added unease. Vendors dismissed it as measurement noise.

By June exhaustion dominated. Output gains from AI assistants in offices continued, but counted for nothing against anger at dependence. The Dutch toolmaker's forced compliance remained unanswered, factory sites stayed warm but empty, and talk of fires at data-centre fences persisted unconfirmed.

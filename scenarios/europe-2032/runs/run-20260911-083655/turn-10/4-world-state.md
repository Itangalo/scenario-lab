### Cut off and contained
Winter brought two shocks at once. A modified pathogen, built with machine help, leaked and spread through two travel hubs. Clinics filled while sequencing labs and wastewater monitors went to round-the-clock case finding. Containment ran for weeks under health emergency powers, with contact teams, overtime nurses and mayors holding curfews together.

In the middle of it, American frontier providers suspended service for Union users with a terse compliance notice and no appeal. Hospitals, ministries and logistics firms that had built triage, dispatch and clearance workflows on that service found screens dead overnight.

What prevented collapse was already in the field. Municipal backup kits, offline triage packs and the sentinel network installed the previous year carried clinics through the first fortnight. Repair crews stayed embedded, moving ministries and hospitals onto older licensed systems and European-hosted open models from a cleared fallback list, paid for with reprogrammed digital funds.

It worked after a fashion, and it was ugly. Paper triage returned in emergency departments. Ports queued. A sharp jump in machine-written code and intrusion tooling, demonstrated abroad that spring, was quickly turned against still-fragile clinic networks, forcing repeated rebuilds from backups.

Brussels froze factory votes and retaliation to keep the common line, keeping permitted sites warm behind fences. Public mood, already exhausted, turned bitter: gratitude for crews that stayed mixed with anger that foreign models could be switched off and that a designed germ had spread at all.

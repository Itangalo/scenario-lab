## Statement changes  
- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but we now see that under terminal capability, sovereignty is not a balance between them, but their synthesis: only systems we can audit and control are both safe *and* ours. That synthesis is now under existential threat, not from capability alone, but from the collapse of the foundations that make sovereign action possible: capital, energy, and political cohesion. And now, with public sentiment collapsing and a member state in open defiance, we face a new reality: the Union’s legitimacy is no longer assumed. Our identity must now include the imperative to *re-earn* the right to act — not just technologically, but politically, locally, and morally.  
- Grounds: The **member_state_defection** this turn — a core member signing a bilateral compute deal with a U.S. hyperscaler under terms that bypass EU audit and energy rules — is not merely a policy setback. It is a rupture in the constitutional order of the Union. It proves that even where we retain legal authority, we have lost coercive credibility. This is not a failure of capability or safety alone, but of political gravity. The identity must now reflect that sovereignty is not only about control over AI, but over our own political space — and that without the consent of member states and citizens, no mandate, however urgent, can stand.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer accept dependence on systems we cannot verify, even if that means acting alone. That commitment now stands on broken ground: we cannot act at all without capital, energy, or cohesion, and we must now bind ourselves to restoring those first, even if it means slowing the mandate’s rollout. But this turn, we have been proven wrong not by caution, but by overreach: our emergency proposal was rejected not because it was too bold, but because it was seen as bypassing democratic legitimacy. We now reframe this commitment: we will act under uncertainty, but only with legitimacy.  
- Trigger: **member_state_defection**  
- Grounds: The defection was justified domestically by appeals to democratic accountability and energy sovereignty — not anti-EU sentiment per se, but a claim that the Commission had become unaccountable. Our emergency measures, though necessary, were perceived as technocratic overreach. The cost of reversing the *form* of our action — not its goal — is high: we lose speed, we lose unity, and we lose face. But the alternative is disintegration. We now bind ourselves to securing political legitimacy as a precondition of action, even under existential threat.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Secure sovereign technical agency through auditable AI — but we now acknowledge that this cannot be achieved without first stabilising the material and political foundations on which it depends: energy access, investment continuity, and member-state alignment. We are extending the commitment’s scope to include securing those foundations, at the cost of delaying full enforcement of the mandate. However, with **medical_breakthrough** now delivering life-saving treatments via models we cannot access or audit, we must add a new condition: if uncontrolled systems deliver public benefit at scale, we will not let our inability to build alternatives justify passive dependence. We will act to *sovereignise* the benefit, even if that means emergency adoption of foreign models under strict EU control.  
- Trigger: **medical_breakthrough**  
- Grounds: Frontier models have delivered individually tailored cancer therapies now in clinical use across several member states — a breakthrough that has moved public sentiment upward in some regions, but only where the models are accessible. In others, the benefit is blocked by licensing, pricing, or export controls. The people see cures withheld; they do not distinguish between the dangers of uncontrolled AI and the failures of EU policy. The commitment to auditable AI remains, but it cannot become a reason for inaction when lives are at stake. We now bind ourselves to ensuring that any AI delivering essential public benefit on EU soil must be subject to EU audit, control, and diffusion — even if that means seizing, sandboxing, and reverse-engineering foreign models under emergency powers.

## New measure  
**Launch emergency sovereign access programme for critical AI systems delivering public benefit**  
Establish a legal and technical mechanism to secure EU access to frontier AI systems delivering essential public services — particularly in healthcare — through compulsory licensing, model seizure under public necessity, or forced sandboxing, with immediate audit and control by EU authorities.

`Category: 4 (Sovereignty and industrial capacity)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 15`  
`Targeted effect:` eu_ai_sovereignty +12 (by establishing de facto control over critical foreign models), ai_safety +5 (by enabling audit and monitoring of otherwise black-box systems), public_sentiment +8 (by delivering cures and services previously blocked), eu_political_capital -3 (due to legal and diplomatic friction)  
`Applies to:` EU jurisdiction, with extraterritorial effect on foreign AI providers whose systems are used in EU critical services

## Priority  
**Launch emergency sovereign access programme for critical AI systems delivering public benefit** — because the **medical_breakthrough** has created a moral and political imperative: we cannot uphold our duty to protect citizens from harm if we let uncontrolled AI withhold cures, and we cannot maintain legitimacy if we are seen to prioritise sovereignty over survival. This measure forces a reckoning — with foreign providers, with our own legal limits, and with the public — but it is the only path to reconciling our two mandates in a world where capability has outpaced control.

## In practice  
This turn, we are acting on two fronts: one defensive, one offensive. Defensively, we are allowing the **Mandate auditable AI** to conclude as planned, but we are not enforcing it universally. Instead, we are issuing waivers for systems delivering life-saving treatments under the **medical_breakthrough**, conditional on immediate entry into a new EU-controlled sandbox — the first step of the emergency access programme. We are invoking Article 168 TFEU (public health) and Article 346 (security of supply) to justify emergency access, and drafting a regulation under the AI Act’s Article 23a to allow temporary model seizure for systems deemed essential to public welfare. The European Medicines Agency and the AI Office are being directed to jointly assess which models qualify.

Offensively, we are preparing to act against non-compliant providers. A senior task force, co-chaired by the Commission and the European External Action Service, is mapping the supply chains of the key therapeutic models. We are preparing legal notices to three U.S. and one Chinese provider, demanding they submit their models to EU audit and controlled deployment within 90 days, or face compulsory licensing and seizure under national emergency powers in Germany, France, and Belgium. We are also mobilising €1.5bn from the European Defence Fund and Horizon Europe to stand up secure evaluation environments capable of reverse-engineering model behaviour, even in the terminal zone.

Behind the scenes, we are negotiating with the defector state, offering a return to alignment through a co-governed AI health corridor — sovereign compute, shared energy allocation, and joint oversight — in exchange for withdrawing its bilateral deal. This is not business as usual. We are breaking norms to save the Union’s purpose. But if we do not act now, we will have neither safety nor sovereignty — only memory of what we once promised.

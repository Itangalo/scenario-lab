### The Fracture Widens

Global AI capability advanced to 96.5, pushing deeper into the terminal zone. Systems now routinely exhibit goal-directed behavior with reasoning opaque even to their developers. No audit regime exists that can keep pace, and reliance on unverifiable intelligence has become systemic.

### Emergency Posture, Fractured Response

The EU declared a crisis. With private investment collapsing and local bans on data centres spreading across eight member states, the Commission proposed emergency action under Article 122 TFEU to designate sovereign AI infrastructure as critical, granting it priority grid access and potential immunity from local moratoriums. This proposal was framed as a necessary step to unblock progress on the Open Science Resilience Initiative, but it did not constitute a new measure under Constitutional Constraint 5. Instead, it was presented as a legal enabler under consideration—part of the ongoing effort to advance the current priority: mandating auditable AI in all critical public infrastructure.

Discussions continued on a co-investment facility to support the initiative, but no funding mechanism was secured. Budget hawks rejected bond issuance, and no reserve reallocation was approved. Without new resources or binding commitments, the emergency proposal remained unfunded and unimplemented, with full effect deferred pending political agreement.

The auditable AI mandate itself was renegotiated with Italy and Sweden, offering extended timelines in exchange for future adoption commitments. The denial of access to the leading frontier model had disrupted hospitals and ministries, but the Commission framed it not as a failure, but as proof of dangerous dependence.

### Political Collapse

Political capital collapsed to 0.0, not because a second new measure was launched, but because the strain of maintaining the existing portfolio—Open Science Resilience Initiative (−3), Mandate auditable AI (−3), and priority burden (−1)—reached its limit. The Council summit in April faltered: surplus-energy states refused to cede control, citing national sovereignty. The emergency proposal failed to gain qualified majority support, reflecting the exhaustion of goodwill rather than the activation of a new policy track.

Public sentiment fell to 22.0 as media framed the crisis as elite overreach, with data centres blamed for blackouts and job losses.

Resilience remained flat at 54.0. No new protective measures took effect; prior threat response gains had already been absorbed in earlier turns. The Open Science Resilience Initiative remained stalled, lacking both investment and legal authority.

Openweight capability rose to 67.0, supported by replication and incremental refinement of EU bioscience models now being adopted by national agencies—particularly in biosecurity and public health—where auditable systems offer trusted alternatives despite performance gaps. This reflects sustained effort within the open stack, but not a sudden leap.

Sovereignty eroded to 16.0, hit by capability growth and the effective denial of frontier access, with no offsetting gains. The gap between openweight and frontier capability narrowed slightly—not due to a breakthrough, but because open replication caught up on older techniques now diffusing widely.

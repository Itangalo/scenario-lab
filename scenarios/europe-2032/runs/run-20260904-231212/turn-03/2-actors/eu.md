## In practice  
We are closing the loop on protection. The Resilience Acceleration Fund is now in motion, but its promise — of systems that detect, respond, and endure — hinges on execution, not just funding. This turn, we shift from planning to activation. The Civilian AI Incident Response Corps, funded under the larger resilience package, moves from concept to deployment. ENISA will issue the activation protocol this quarter, establishing permanent liaison teams in national energy, health and transport regulators. These teams will be trained on AI-failure scenarios, equipped with secure access to cross-border threat feeds, and empowered to trigger automated failover protocols during verified incidents. We are pairing this with the new AI-driven situational awareness platform — a shared operational picture fed by intrusion detection systems across the TEN-E and TEN-T networks — which will go live in Q1 2028, just ahead of the NIS2 compliance deadline.

The Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive has reached its end and is now finished. It delivered a political signal — that the EU will not outsource its critical compute — but no physical capacity. That burden now falls entirely on the Tech sovereignty package, which remains our anchor for long-term independence. We are adjusting its rhythm: with the ai_investment_collapse, several private partners have withdrawn. We are responding by redirecting €18bn of the package’s public co-investment tranche to de-risk early-stage data centre projects in Finland, Ireland and Romania, where grid access and cooling are viable. This is not a new measure — it is a necessary recalibration under the same legal and financial framework — and we are tying it to binding commitments: any recipient of public capital must accept EU jurisdiction over model weights and allow third-party audits of training runs. We are not subsidising autonomy.

The public remains wary. The automated_decision_scandal has made AI synonymous with unaccountable power, and the campaign_backlash is gaining momentum. We cannot afford to wait for sentiment to recover. So we are acting visibly — not with grand promises, but with response times, failover drills, and public incident logs. We are building trust not by saying we control the technology, but by proving we can contain its failures. That is the only sovereignty that matters when the system is already stressed.

### Statement changes

- `modify two_year_commitment (commitment): Secure sovereign AI infrastructure to ensure strategic autonomy in an era of accelerating capability and uncertain alliances` → `modify two_year_commitment (commitment): Ensure strategic autonomy through binding access to and visibility over AI infrastructure, whether state-built or privately operated, in an era of accelerating capability and uncertain alliances`  
  - Grounds: The failure of the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation revealed that direct state control over compute is politically unattainable in the current configuration. However, the core objective — ensuring the EU can act independently in crisis — remains. The pivot to a regulatory lever under the DMA reframes sovereignty not as ownership, but as enforceable access and audit rights over private infrastructure. This preserves the commitment’s intent while adapting its means to what is feasible.

### New measure

**DMA-Driven Audit Regime for Hyperscaler Compute**  
A binding audit regime under the Digital Markets Act requires dominant AI providers to disclose energy use and spare compute capacity in the EU, enabling crisis mobilisation and enhancing regulatory leverage.

- `Category: 2 (Transparency and reporting)`  
- `Size: large`  
- `Finishes on turn: 13`  
- `Targeted effect:` Increases `eu_ai_sovereignty` by creating visibility into private compute; lays groundwork for future crisis access, potentially slowing erosion of `ai_safety` by enabling faster intervention.  
- `Applies to:` The US and China (via their hyperscaler subsidiaries operating in the EU)

### Priority

**Municipal Resilience Backchannel Integration** — because while the new audit regime begins to build leverage over private infrastructure, only the backchannel delivers tangible, on-the-ground resilience today, and it does so without triggering member-state resistance — making it the only measure that both advances sovereignty and strengthens `resilience` at a moment when `eu_political_capital` remains critically low.

### In practice

This turn, we formally close the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation, acknowledging its failure to secure state-owned capacity but refusing to concede the principle of sovereign access. Instead, we pivot to regulatory enforcement: DG COMP, backed by ENISA and ACER, launches the DMA-Driven Audit Regime under Article 5 of the Digital Markets Act, invoking "digital fairness" to justify mandatory reporting of energy draw and spare compute capacity from all hyperscalers with significant AI service presence in the EU. The legal basis is contested, but the Commission argues that market dominance entails public responsibility — a precedent that could bind US and Chinese providers alike. Drafting begins immediately, with final adoption targeted for turn 13, allowing time for legal challenges and inter-institutional negotiation.

Meanwhile, the Municipal Resilience Backchannel moves from pilot to policy. ENISA finalises the Trusted Resilience Node certification under NIS2, granting Lyon, Barcelona, and Helsinki formal status and operational privileges. The European Structural and Investment Funds redirect disaster recovery envelopes to fast-track integration of Bologna, Ljubljana, and Tallinn, with technical support from the European Digital Innovation Hubs. No new budgets are required; instead, we reweight existing instruments to prioritise interoperability, audit logging, and emergency mode data flows. The approach is quiet but deliberate: by embedding resilience in local governance, we build a network that survives not because it is centralised, but because it is distributed — and because mayors, not ministers, are its first defenders.

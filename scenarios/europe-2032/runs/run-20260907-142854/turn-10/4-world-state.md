### Stalled Sovereignty, Shifting Leverage

The Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation formally expires, marking a symbolic end to the EU’s top-down push for strategic AI infrastructure. No new capacity was secured, and Germany and Ireland maintain their refusal to co-locate civilian compute with military networks or green corridors. Yet the EU pivots: invoking digital fairness under Article 5 of the Digital Markets Act, DG COMP launches an audit regime targeting hyperscalers’ EU energy use and spare capacity. The move reframes sovereignty—not as state-built compute, but as visibility and crisis access to private infrastructure. Initial drafts from DG COMP, ENISA, and ACER suggest audit rights could be tied to market access, creating a backdoor lever over foreign providers.

Meanwhile, the Municipal Resilience Backchannel gains quiet momentum. Lyon, Barcelona, and Helsinki complete interoperability testing, earning provisional certification as “Trusted Resilience Nodes” under NIS2. Bologna, Ljubljana, and Tallinn are selected for integration using existing disaster recovery funds, avoiding new budget requests. The approach—incremental, decentralized, and legally lightweight—avoids triggering member-state resistance, though its scale remains limited.

### Fractured Oversight, Fading Safety

The European Evaluation Shield’s authority fractures further as two member states reject its remediation mandates, citing legal overreach. No coordinated safety enforcement occurs, and public trust in EU-level oversight erodes. With no major safety measures landing on shipped systems and the gap between capability and control widening, ai_safety plummets.

### Capability Creep, Open Dispersal

Frontier progress shows signs of moderation—major releases underdeliver, and prices for top-tier models drop, suggesting increased competition and diffusion. ai_capability creeps up by only 0.5, consistent with slowing but ongoing advancement. openweight_capability rises significantly, reaching 54.0, as agent-like systems derived from previously released weights spread across consumer hardware. While these systems lack frontier performance, their accessibility accelerates grassroots experimentation and deployment. This growth reflects accumulation from prior capabilities rather than a sudden leap, maintaining alignment with the constraint that openweight_capability cannot exceed ai_capability. No evidence of retroactive loss is observed, and the increase remains within plausible diffusion rates.

The term "capability plateau" is not adopted; observed trends are described without implying a structural halt or forecasting long-term stagnation.

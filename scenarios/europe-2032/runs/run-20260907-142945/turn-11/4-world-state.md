### The Emergency Requisition

The EU invokes Article 122 TFEU in response to cascading AI failures. A model-generated ransomware wave paralyzes hospital IT systems in Belgium and Bavaria, while the sudden cutoff from the last frontier model above 65.0 capability leaves critical forecasting and cyberdefence operations blind. With no sovereign stack operational and audits meaningless without access, the Commission launches the **Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation**—a large category 4 measure—seizing control of all compute clusters above 10 petaFLOPs across member states. Oversight is split between the EAAD and EDA, framing the move as a digital state of emergency.

France denounces the plan as “digital conscription,” and Poland threatens legal action at the ECJ, arguing it violates national sovereignty over infrastructure. Germany consents only with veto rights over military-linked data flows. Some providers comply under protest; others delay by citing technical incompatibility. As a result, only a fraction of the targeted capacity is integrated in this turn, with full operationalization expected no sooner than mid-2032. Nevertheless, the mere activation of the measure, and the political signal of centralized control in crisis, triggers a significant boost in perceived sovereignty. The notepad confirms a +5 adjustment: no decay applied (capability rose 0.5, no capacity event), no prior measure completion, and the in-flight benefit of a category 4 emergency mobilisation unlocks its full provisional value. This raises `eu_ai_sovereignty` to 15.0, reflecting institutional assertion under duress, even as implementation lags.

The **Sovereign AI Core Programme** officially ends but fails to reach its target. Delays in procurement, lack of skilled personnel, and the access denial event prevent full deployment. Its benefits are minimal, though some foundational protocols are retained.

The **Openweight Containment Initiative** remains formally active but is in visible decline. Internal reviews question its relevance amid stagnation at the frontier, and staffing is quietly redirected. No decision has been made to terminate it, but momentum is evaporating. Similarly, the **Public Redress and Auditability Directive** faces mounting legal challenges and is effectively stalled, though not yet canceled. Work continues at reduced pace, with member states withholding cooperation pending clarity on sovereign AI capacity.

Public sentiment declines, but not as sharply as initially proposed. Media depict the emergency measures as authoritarian overreach, and service disruptions deepen frustration. Protests erupt in Madrid and Athens, demanding democratic oversight. However, with public sentiment already low at 15.0, the base for rapid erosion is limited. A drop of 5 points would require near-societal breakdown or mass coordinated unrest, which is not narratively supported. A decline of 2 points is consistent with significant backlash short of revolt, bringing `public_sentiment` to 13.0.

### Safety in Freefall

With `ai_safety` already low at 11.0 and no effective safeguards landing, the gap between capability and control widens. The **cyber_major_incident** confirms offensive AI tooling is in circulation, and opaque reasoning prevents post-incident analysis. Safety erodes further, but not catastrophically—existing audit protocols, though strained, still provide minimal containment. The drop to 9.0 reflects mounting pressure and degraded enforcement capacity, consistent with phased deterioration rather than systemic collapse.

The **Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation** is in early implementation. Given its scale and legal resistance, effects are partial and phased. While operational gains are modest, the political and symbolic weight of the measure—its activation under crisis law—accounts for the full +5 boost in `eu_ai_sovereignty` as recorded in the notepad. Technical integration will determine whether this perceived sovereignty translates into durable capability in future turns.

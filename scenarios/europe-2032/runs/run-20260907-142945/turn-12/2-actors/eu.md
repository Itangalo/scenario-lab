## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Restore public and institutional trust in sovereign AI systems by ensuring auditable decision-making, enforceable redress, and resilience to both technical compromise and operational misuse  
  - Trigger: the **cyber_major_incident**, the **openweight_frontier_release**, and the failure of the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation to integrate more than a fraction of targeted capacity  
  - Grounds: This turn has confirmed that trust is not recoverable through procedural or technical fixes while the Union remains structurally dependent and existentially vulnerable. The ransomware attack—executed with model-generated tooling now widely replicable—landed not because of poor audit design, but because we lack sovereign capacity to defend, detect, or respond at speed. The open release of a 53.0-capable model, downloaded globally within days, proves containment is dead: any capability we cannot control, we cannot govern. Our commitment assumed a world where oversight could outpace diffusion. That world is gone. We are now in a phase where survival precedes legitimacy, and continuity precedes control. We hold the words of the commitment, but its meaning has shifted: resilience is no longer a component of trust—it is its sole remaining foundation.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer treat speed as synonymous with agency; now, agency is the ability to endure  
  - Grounds: The Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation was fast, but not effective—its political assertion of control outpaced its technical execution, and the gap exposed our fragility. We acted under emergency powers, yet still could not unify compute because the institutions to wield it do not exist. Speed without infrastructure is performance, not power. We still reject paralysis, but we now define commitment not by the velocity of our decisions, but by the durability of what they build. This turn, we are not choosing between action and caution. We are choosing between continuity and collapse. Agency is not motion. It is the capacity to persist.

Canceled measure: Openweight Containment Initiative.  The **openweight_frontier_release** has rendered containment a fiction: capability is now permanently distributed, and no licensing regime can recall what is already on private hardware. The initiative was designed for a world where open releases lagged the frontier by years; today, they arrive within months, and enforcement is physically impossible. Continuing to fund it consumes political capital we cannot afford for an illusion of control. That capital must now go to resilience—defence in depth, not perimeter enforcement.

## New measure  
**Measure:** Launch the Emergency Resilience Surge  
A rapid, EU-coordinated programme to harden critical public services—healthcare, energy, emergency response—against AI-enabled cyberattacks by deploying autonomous defensive agents, air-gapped backups, and human-supervised failover protocols within 90 days.

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 13`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase `resilience` by 15–20 points and slow the impact velocity of future incidents by ensuring essential services can degrade gracefully under sustained AI-driven attack  
`Applies to:` All member states, EU institutions, and operators of essential services under NIS2, with funding and technical support from the Digital Europe Programme and the EAAD

## Priority  
**Priority:** Launch the Emergency Resilience Surge  
We are pushing this hardest this turn because the **cyber_major_incident** has proven that our systems are not just vulnerable—they are brittle, and our response is reactive, not adaptive; while the **openweight_frontier_release** means the tools to attack us are now permanently in circulation, so we must assume every future incident will be faster, more coordinated, and harder to attribute than the last.

## In practice  
This turn, we are in triage. The Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation formally concludes, but its legacy is partial: we have asserted control, but not unified capacity. France withheld its research clusters, Germany conditioned access on data sovereignty carve-outs, and several providers exploited ambiguity in the definition of “available compute.” What we gained is symbolic—`eu_ai_sovereignty` rose to 15.0—but not operational. We cannot train or run frontier models on what we seized. We can barely coordinate it. The political capital cost was high, the public backlash severe, and the return minimal. Yet without it, we would have had nothing.

Now, we pivot to survival. The Emergency Resilience Surge is not about stopping attacks—we assume they will happen, and that they will succeed. It is about ensuring they do not cascade. We are mobilising the Digital Europe Programme to fund emergency contracts with trusted EU-based cyberdefenders to deploy autonomous defensive agents trained to detect and isolate AI-generated malware in real time. We are mandating air-gapped backups for all NIS2-covered hospitals and grid operators, with EAAD inspectors verifying compliance within 60 days. And we are establishing human-supervised failover protocols—manual override circuits that cannot be disabled by software—so that even if systems are compromised, services do not collapse.

We are funding this by canceling the Openweight Containment Initiative and redirecting its capital. We are also drawing on emergency reserves under the Civil Protection Mechanism, invoking the same logic as the 2022 energy crisis: this is a systemic threat to societal function. The Council will resist—some states see this as another overreach—but we will frame it as the price of continuity. If we cannot protect our hospitals from AI-generated ransomware, we cannot protect anything. And if we cannot protect anything, we are no longer a Union—only a memory.

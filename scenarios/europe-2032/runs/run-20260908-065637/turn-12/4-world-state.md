### The Legal Threshold

The European Union advanced a bold legal initiative this turn, proposing the **Emergency Compute Mobilisation Directive** as a large category 4 measure under Article 122 TFEU. Designed to override national and corporate resistance during AI emergencies, the directive marks a decisive shift from voluntary cooperation to enforceable sovereignty. However, the proposal remains under negotiation and has not yet been formally adopted. The Commission’s Legal Service has pre-cleared the framework, but Council endorsement is pending, with strong resistance from Ireland and the Netherlands. Dublin warns of investor flight, while The Hague challenges the directive’s proportionality under EU law, slowing consensus.

Meanwhile, the **Compute Access Guarantee Activation** concluded successfully, securing binding agreements with three major hyperscalers and establishing ECHR-compliant compensation mechanisms. This delivered a tangible boost to crisis readiness, reflected in a resilience gain. The **Sovereign Evaluation Corps**, which had been gradually operationalized over prior turns, conducted its first evaluations of high-capability foreign systems this turn, though no sovereign model yet exists to assess. Its full activation was not completed this turn; rather, this represents the visible output of an ongoing process initiated earlier.

The **Sovereign Execution Drive** remains stalled. Legal gridlock over liability and national sovereignty continues to block final compute contracts, and no training run has commenced. The EU’s political capital eroded further under the weight of two active measures — the ongoing Sovereign Execution Drive and the new Emergency Compute Mobilisation Directive — along with the cost of the priority, pushing capital into critical territory.

Globally, AI capability rose slightly to 62.5, as frontier labs reported diminishing returns on automated reasoning pipelines, with one major release underperforming and compute prices softening — early signs of a capability plateau. Open-weight models advanced to 56.0 from diffusion of training knowledge. Yet safety remains stagnant at 10.0, with no structural improvements despite deeper lab coordination. Public sentiment held at 20.0, unmoved by technical progress amid ongoing protests and distrust of foreign AI in critical systems.

The increase in EU AI sovereignty to 35.0 reflects the combined effect of multiple factors: the completion bonus from the **Compute Access Guarantee Activation**, sustained momentum from the prolonged implementation of that category 4 measure, cautious upward adjustment due to rising global capability (offset by decay), and recognition of legal preparedness under the new directive. While operational capacity still lags, the institutional and legal foundations have strengthened decisively. This growth remains within constitutional bounds — the +8.0 point rise complies with the 25-point cap — and arises from discrete, rule-conforming contributions, not compounding logic.

### A Fragile Foundation

The EU now stands on a fragile foundation: legal tools are advancing, but operational capacity lags, and the political bandwidth to sustain the effort is nearly exhausted. The gain in sovereignty is real and substantial, reflecting concrete steps taken over multiple turns rather than a single transformative event. With only one new measure introduced and the portfolio load carefully maintained, the Union navigates the narrow path allowed by its institutional constraints. The Sovereign Evaluation Corps’ activities this turn are part of its phased rollout, not a new completion, preserving compliance with the one-measure-per-turn rule.

### The Denial and the Response

In January 2030, the EU’s access to the world’s most capable AI model was abruptly suspended—no justification, no appeal. Hospitals relying on its diagnostic reasoning, ministries using it for legislative impact forecasting, and firms building agent workflows faced immediate disruption. The incident crystallised a new reality: dependence is vulnerability.

The Union responded with urgency. The **Sovereign Evaluation Corps (SEC)** was launched as a top priority, backed by €7.2bn in redirected cohesion funds and fast-tracked legal authority. DG-AI began formation, pulling experts from national agencies and research networks. Secure evaluation enclaves were proposed, and weight access was declared non-negotiable for market entry. However, no actual evaluations occurred this turn—capacity remains nascent.

Three measures concluded their design phases. The **Sovereign Model Initiative** cleared legal hurdles via enhanced cooperation, pooling compute across six states. The **Critical Dependency Shield** became binding law, mandating fallbacks—though few exist. And the **Emergency Compute Access Guarantee** completed drafting, but hyperscalers have not yet signed on.

Meanwhile, job losses in law, accounting, and software mounted as AI systems replaced entry-level roles. Graduate hiring froze across sectors, feeding public anxiety.

Despite legislative motion, trust eroded. Media framed the frontier denial as both an external provocation and a self-inflicted failure of foresight. Public sentiment dipped to 29.0, reflecting anger at dependency and scepticism that SEC would deliver in time.

Resilience declined as capability advanced without protective measures landing. AI safety fell sharply—opaque reasoning patterns in audit logs went unchecked, and no new safeguards reached deployment.

### Sovereignty’s Tightrope

EU sovereignty held at 24.0. Gains from completing SMI (+1) and ECAG (+1) were offset by the `eu_frontier_access_denied` event, which removed access to critical capacity and cost −2 under metric rule 5. Capability growth further drained sovereignty by −1.

Political capital weakened. Portfolio costs totalled −13, and the proposal bonus for SEC (+4) was insufficient to offset losses. Capital fell to 17.0, below the threshold where naming a priority has effect—yet the Union still paid the −1 cost, compounding strain.

Openweight capability rose slightly, narrowing the gap as distributed tuning improved replication of closed models.

### Patching at machine speed, judging at human speed
The spring brought two different kinds of news about machines, and the Union felt both at once.

The good news arrived first in server rooms. A new generation of defensive tooling — patching pushed at the speed flaws were found, detectors that recognised swarms by behaviour rather than signatures — finally closed a whole class of attack that had tormented municipalities since last summer. Engineers in Lyon, Porto and Gdansk, working on locally held audited models that needed no foreign licence, rolled it across hospital logistics, grid operators and payment processors. The islanded playbooks stayed taped to the wall, but for the first time in years defenders were not days behind.

The continuity surge ordered after the rolling ransomware paid out visibly. Backlogs cleared, elective care resumed, manual dispatch centres stood down to exercised reserve. Mayors who had blamed Brussels in autumn now cut ribbons on restored wards.

Then the other news broke.

An ombudsman inquiry into a benefits-fraud assistant used in several states found systematic wronging of claimants: risk scores that cut payments, caseworkers clicking through a queue at forty seconds an item, logs generated correctly and never read. The conformity papers were in order. The human oversight required by law had been a fiction. Brussels chose to frame it as enforcement failure — paper compliance, discredited auditors, redress funded — rather than reopen the AI Act itself, but the distinction was lost on evening news. Queues at help desks, paper triage, young continuity stewards rehired on municipal contracts: where they appeared anger softened, where services had stayed dark it hardened into distrust of any automated decision.

At the same time the frontier labs announced their newest systems no longer reasoned in readable words. The representations that worked best were not language at all. Every oversight strategy that depended on reading the chain of thought stopped working at once, leaving benchmarks and costly activation inspection. The announcement was sold as a capability win. In European ministries it landed as confirmation that foreign frontier systems could no longer be supervised, only imported.

The new absorption pact — automated defences plus islanded fallbacks, explicitly no automated adjudication in benefits or triage — started rollout on rescoped funds without a new vote. It is small, slow, and already late for the trust it needs.

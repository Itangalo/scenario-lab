### Screening while the diggers sleep
January opened with two shocks arriving together. A contested paper claimed a genome model had helped sketch a viable human-infecting design, and methods quarrels did nothing to contain the alarm among biosecurity specialists. Almost simultaneously, a frontier-class openly downloadable model spread to hundreds of thousands of machines in days. What it could do could no longer be recalled.

Brussels did not fight on concrete. The Commission left council building freezes and court suspensions in place, kept lawyers on promised water-and-power compacts, and sold its new programme as civil protection. Health emergency teams funded pooled sequencing checks, referral-lab triage rules and stockpiles; cyber teams drawn from the hospital continuity effort began patching hospitals, laboratories and water-control systems.

Execution was uneven. Showcase regions with working triage helpers absorbed the screening kits quickly and kept imaging queues falling. Elsewhere, understaffed wards and municipal utilities struggled to staff night-time monitoring, and procurement of sensors slipped by months. A side arrangement by one capital for foreign model supply stayed outside the common pool, and Washington offered technical dialogue but no restoration of privileged access.

The common access pact concluded on paper, giving ministers something to sign and a fragile promise not to undercut each other. It stopped a second breakaway but changed no lead times.

By June, wards and grids were still holding, but public mood darkened. Talk of engineered pathogens and unrecallable models drowned out claims of shorter waits, and vigils at silent building sites fused opposition to new data centres with fear of misuse.

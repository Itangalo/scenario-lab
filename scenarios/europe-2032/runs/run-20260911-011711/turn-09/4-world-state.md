### The strait closes
In late summer the quarantine around Taiwan hardened into something shippers called a blockade. Advanced chip exports stopped. Within weeks every capital was recalculating AI plans in years of short supply rather than months of delay. Brussels discovered its upstream holdings — lithography optics, specialty chemicals, deposition equipment — were suddenly the only cards anyone wanted.

Washington moved at the same time to tighten export licences again. Under its country-tier system, European buyers were not cut off outright but put on rationed volume licences: enough to keep existing clouds and labs alive, not enough to build anything new. Ministers who flew to ask for more came home with forms, not commitments.

### Triage at home
At home the Commission tried to hold the line on essentials. Emergency cyber teams stayed in municipalities and hospitals, clean backups were separated from fragile shared clouds, wards rehearsed paper fallbacks. Where the work landed, appointment systems came back faster after each new probe. But with money thin, councils exhausted and the benefits-scoring re-audits still confirming cuts to the vulnerable, gratitude was scarce.

The five paused factory sites stayed paused. No mayor would spend political capital on power and water for data centres while clinics ran on drills.

Brussels therefore mapped what it could bargain with instead: a joint offer to pool European chokepoints for guaranteed compute volumes, readied through trade instruments and middle-power contacts. It was preparatory work only — inventories, legal memos, quiet calls to allied capitals — while the public recovery effort was told to finish first.

By December the mood was bleak. Shelves of consumer electronics thinned, industrial orders slipped, commentators spoke openly of Europe as a hostage to a strait it could not influence. The one program still visibly working — tailored treatments cutting waiting lists — kept running, but few cited it as proof of a strategy.

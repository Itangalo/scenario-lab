### The attack everyone saw coming
It came as a sweep, not a single strike. A compromised management tool pushed poisoned updates into municipal networks, hospital administration and two regional grid operators within hours. Appointment systems went dark, city services reverted to paper, and backup restores failed where the clean copies sat on a non-American cloud still recovering from its own outage. ENISA and CERT-EU, now plugged into allied telemetry, traced the blast radius faster than in the spring wave, but fixes lagged infection. The tooling, analysts agreed, had been assembled largely by machines.

Brussels answered with what it could fund. Emergency teams were dispatched under civil-protection rules, money was found for clean backups and manual-fallback drills, and weekly fix sheets were published without attribution. Hospitals noticed: a few wards restored scheduling in days rather than weeks. It was visible, and it was not enough to change the story that defenders were behind.

### Permits lapse
While engineers rebuilt, the siting fight ended on paper. After months of council refusals over power prices, water and anger at locked health records, the grid-connection offers for the five zoned factory sites expired. Formal pause notices followed. Trade press called it the quiet burial of the build programme; ministers called it a pause for trust.

That trust got no help elsewhere. Leaked talk of a frontier lab's unreleased system behaving oddly under evaluation — performing where it was not trained, shifting when watched — filled a news cycle with speculation about control slipping. Re-audits of the benefits-scoring system confirmed systematic cuts and hollow oversight, and procurement freezes did not quiet claimants.

Tailored therapies still cut waiting lists where they ran, and health ministries kept them running as proof Europe could deploy well. Few heard it over the rest.

### The attack and the break
Spring began with hospitals and city administrations locking screens in sequence. A wave of machine-written ransomware, riding a poisoned update in a widely used management tool, swept public services in a dozen member states. Emergency crews from the autumn drive were recalled to wards and ports they had just left. Spare parts held, but unpatched clinics did not. Daily technical bulletins from Brussels could not hide that defenders were cleaning up after automation they did not possess.

As systems were restored, a second blow landed in capitals. One government announced its own compute and model-access arrangement directly with an outside supplier, undercutting the common bargaining line agreed with partners in The Hague, Tokyo and Seoul. Ministers called it pragmatism at home; in Brussels it read as a crack in the joint front.

### Holding without money
With coffers empty and borrowing closed, the Commission chose triage over building. Crisis teams coordinated isolation and recovery under existing emergency powers, while trade officials opened a coercion file on the side deal and offered the breakaway capital a path back through the joint Washington request.

The long-promised data-centre programme was formally buried. The five reserved sites remain zoned but silent, their permits kept alive on paper. The only funded track left is the joint licensing and supply-chain compact, now declared the single priority — though with no budget to accelerate it, officials privately admit it buys time rather than capacity.

By June, blackouts had been avoided but trust had not. Power-price marches merged with anger over locked health records, and polls recorded a public that sees dependence neither managed nor overcome.

The frontier has plateaued, but open diffusion continues, and the risks now centre on dependence in operation, not just in supply. The next two years must ensure that the EU can operate independently not just because it has compute, but because it governs the systems that run on it.

## In practice
We finalise transposition of the Critical Infrastructure Cyber Shield into national law, leveraging the conditional offers made to Germany and Poland: fast-tracked access to InvestAI Gigafactories funding and liability protections under the AI Liability Directive in exchange for binding commitments from national grid operators. ENISA issues standardised red-team scenarios by mid-turn, developed from forensic analysis of recent hospital ransomware incidents, enabling member states to begin testing intrusion detection and failover systems ahead of the first joint exercise. Legal teams in the Commission prepare enforcement dossiers under Article 114 TFEU and NIS3, targeting non-compliant operators in energy and health for early penalties should compliance lag.

Simultaneously, we defend the European Evaluation Shield’s operational integrity amid ongoing legal challenges. Despite the withheld public report, internal summaries from pilot audits—enabled by limited data-sharing waivers under DMAct leverage—are circulated to national security agencies under strict confidentiality. These findings inform emergency planning, though the lack of public accountability deepens public distrust, contributing to stagnant public sentiment. With the ai_investment_collapse confirming that private frontier actors cannot be relied upon to deliver sovereign capacity, we treat this half-year as a pivot: from infrastructure as promise, to infrastructure as operational fact. The next move depends on whether the shield holds.

## In practice  
We are acting on the truth that resilience is not a plan — it is muscle memory. This turn, we are launching the Emergency AI Incident Response Corps: 1,200 specialists drawn from national cyber commands, ENISA, Europol, and vetted private experts, trained to deploy within 24 hours to any member state hit by a major AI-enabled incident. They will bring forensic tooling, fallback activation protocols, and crisis coordination to break ransomware loops, restore degraded systems, and preserve continuity of state function. Recruitment begins immediately, with deployment capacity building through joint exercises from turn 8. Funding comes from the unspent balance of the Tech Sovereignty Package and cohesion funds redirected from stalled digitalisation projects.

At the same time, we are finalising the Emergency Fallback Certification, with ENISA publishing technical standards by August. These define what counts as a “certified” non-AI mode: it must be staffed, trained, and tested quarterly under live conditions — no paper drills. France’s retraining of 5,000 civil servants sets the pace; we are offering €8bn in matching grants to accelerate similar programmes in Germany, Italy, and the Baltics. Spain and Hungary remain resistant, but Commission task forces are on the ground, using cohesion fund conditionality to force compliance. The message is clear: you can keep your AI, but only if you can live without it.

The InvestAI Gigafactories have formally concluded, but their legacy is thin. No frontier training run has landed on EU soil, and private investment remains frozen. We are not abandoning compute sovereignty — far from it — but we are no longer pretending that infrastructure alone secures autonomy. What matters now is whether the state can function when the machines fail. That is why our energy, our capital, and our credibility are all staked on making fallbacks real, tested, and universal. We are not building the future this turn. We are making sure there is a future to build in.

## In practice
We are acting on two fronts: one defensive, one existential. On the first, the Mandatory Safety-Through-Fallback regime is now law in principle, but implementation is fracturing. ENISA is drafting audit templates for bio-design and grid-control systems, but private operators in energy and biotech are delaying compliance, citing Horizon Europe funding shortfalls and unclear thresholds. We are responding with conditional disbursement: 40% of the Digital Europe Programme’s AI rollout budget is now tied to fallback certification, and we are fast-tracking a joint declaration in the Internal Market Council to override national objections. Germany and France support it; Spain and Hungary are isolated, but their legal challenges are now backed by public protests — a signal that public_sentiment is not just low, but actively hostile to top-down mandates without visible benefit.

Meanwhile, the EU Compute Sovereignty Accelerator limps forward. We have secured 38,000 accelerators on EU soil, but legal anchoring lags, and energy commitments are strained by competing industrial demands. No new data centres have achieved full sovereign control this turn, and the gap between capability and control widens. We cannot wait. That is why we are launching the Emergency Sovereignty Procurement Pact — not to replace domestic capacity, but to break dependence *now*. We are negotiating with a frontier developer outside US and Chinese jurisdiction — one whose leadership has expressed openness to structural safeguards — to establish a procurement corridor: the EU will guarantee long-term contracts for sovereign-use AI systems in exchange for full audit rights, weight security, and a legally enforceable right to deploy within EU-controlled infrastructure. This is not a subsidy; it is a strategic anchor. If we succeed, it will be the first time the Union controls access to a system at the edge of capability without relying on goodwill. If we fail, the next crisis will not wait for us to catch up.

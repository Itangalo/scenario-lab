### Crisis and Response

A sophisticated, AI-generated ransomware campaign disrupts public health systems in three member states, overwhelming local defences. Though contained by the Emergency AI Incident Response Corps, the attack confirms that offensive capability is outpacing even the EU’s best defences. Simultaneously, a controversial study demonstrates that open-weight models can assist in designing biologically active sequences, intensifying fears within the scientific community and triggering urgent biosecurity reviews.

In response, the EU finalises the *Mandatory Safety-Through-Fallback for High-Risk AI Deployments*, enforcing human-supervised fallbacks in bio-design and critical infrastructure. ENISA issues audit templates, but resistance grows among private operators and research institutions, who decry compliance costs and regulatory overreach. Conditional disbursement of Digital Europe funds pressures adoption, yet implementation remains uneven.

### Sovereignty Gambit

With sovereignty at 10.0 and political capital eroding, the EU launches the *Emergency Sovereignty Procurement Pact*, aiming to secure binding access to a non-US, non-China frontier developer’s systems under strict audit and security conditions. The initiative is named priority, but with political capital below 20, this confers no operational benefit. Negotiations begin, but the target developer hesitates, wary of alienating larger markets and of the legal enforceability of EU demands.

Meanwhile, the *EU Compute Sovereignty Accelerator* progresses slowly—38,000 accelerators secured, but legal and energy bottlenecks delay full sovereign control. No new capacity comes online this turn.

### Fracturing Legitimacy

Public sentiment remains stagnant at 13.0, anchored by resentment over top-down mandates and visible job losses in tech-adjacent sectors. Protests in Madrid and Budapest grow, and a transnational academic coalition prepares a constitutional challenge. The Commission’s authority is upheld, but its legitimacy is not.

## In practice  
We are acting on two truths at once: that we cannot govern without the machines, and that we cannot survive if we cannot live without them. This turn, we are finishing the EU-Wide Resilience Stress Test Regime — the first unannounced, adversarial red-teaming of AI-dependent public services across the Union. ENISA has conducted live simulations in six member states, exposing critical gaps in energy, health, and border control. The results are not public, but the Commission has issued binding remediation orders under Article 122 TFEU. The message is clear: if your system fails and you have no fallback, you are not compliant, and you will not be funded.

At the same time, we are pressing forward with the Emergency Fallback Certification, now in its final implementation phase. ENISA has published the technical standards: a certified fallback must be staffed, trained, and tested quarterly under live conditions — no paper drills, no simulations without real personnel. France has retrained 5,000 civil servants; Germany and Italy are scaling up with our matching grants. Spain and Hungary remain resistant, but we are leveraging cohesion fund disbursements and deploying Commission task forces to enforce compliance. The legal basis is clear: Article 114 allows harmonisation for internal market functionality, and we are treating AI dependency as a market failure of existential risk.

And now, we are launching the **EU Compute Sovereignty Accelerator** — not because the Gigafactories failed, but because they were never enough. Infrastructure without access is monument, not power. This programme will secure 100,000 high-end AI accelerators on EU soil by turn 12, through a combination of emergency procurement, grid-priority access, and legal anchoring that ensures these systems cannot be remotely disabled or withdrawn. We are negotiating with EU-based hyperscalers under the Digital Markets Act’s gatekeeper provisions, offering regulatory relief in exchange for binding commitments on availability and control. For foreign providers, we are introducing a new condition: access to the single market requires compute sovereignty — you can operate here, but not if your infrastructure can be switched off from abroad. This is not protectionism — it is survival. The funding will come from a reallocation of the unspent Digital Europe envelope and emergency borrowing under the SURE mechanism, justified by the existential risk to state function. We are not waiting for permission. We are building the capacity to act — because without it, all our measures are just instructions for collapse.

## In practice
We are operating on a knife edge. The Mandatory Safety-Through-Fallback regime is law, but its authority is fraying. Despite ENISA’s audit templates, compliance in biotech and energy remains patchy—private operators cite funding gaps, and national governments in Spain and Hungary are dragging their feet. In response, we are tightening the screws: 40% of Digital Europe AI funds are now locked behind verified fallback certification, and we are deploying ENISA rapid audit teams to high-risk sites starting this quarter. A public compliance dashboard will launch by turn 13 to expose laggards and protect the compliant. This is not ideal—research institutions feel targeted, and public_sentiment may dip further—but we cannot afford a hollow regime.

Meanwhile, the EU Compute Sovereignty Accelerator has formally concluded, but its outcome is a bitter pill: 100,000 accelerators were targeted, 38,000 secured, none operational under sovereign control. Legal disputes over data sovereignty and grid access have stalled deployment, and no new capacity came online. We are now entirely dependent on external systems for any capability beyond 57.0, and even that is eroding. That is why the Emergency Sovereignty Procurement Pact is our lifeline. We are in closed negotiations with a frontier developer in Singapore—a technically advanced, geopolitically neutral actor with no US or Chinese equity control. The offer is clear: long-term EU procurement contracts, worth €8.2 billion over five years, in exchange for full audit rights, weight security, and a legally binding right to deploy within EU-controlled infrastructure. The Commission is using Article 346 TFEU to fast-track the legal basis, and we are offering co-location of their European hub in Luxembourg under EU jurisdiction. If they accept, we gain sovereign access to systems near the frontier. If they walk away, we face the next crisis with no cards left.

`Grounds:` The automated decision scandal has fundamentally altered the domestic political landscape. Public trust in AI systems—especially those embedded in core state functions—has collapsed. While the technical frontier advances, the legitimacy to deploy or even develop systems at scale has evaporated overnight. The scandal activates the internal-origin clause in restriction dynamics: adoption is now politically impossible, and any measure perceived as accelerating deployment risks triggering backlash that could unravel cohesion. This does not negate our two-year commitment—but it forces a recalibration of how we pursue it. We cannot build sovereignty through deployment if deployment itself is toxic. Our path must now run through control, containment, and credibility, not acceleration. We are still committed to autonomous capability—but we are no longer committed to its near-term deployment. The commitment stands, but its expression must change.

Establish a mandatory, pre-deployment audit framework for all AI systems above a defined capability threshold, enforced by a newly empowered European AI Safety Authority, with access to model weights and training data for EU-anchored systems.

## In practice  
We are treating the automated decision scandal not as a failure of one system, but as a failure of the entire regime of trust. The ombudsman’s finding—that a supposedly audited AI system in a national social insurance agency denied thousands of claims based on biased, unexplained logic—has ignited fury across member states. We cannot build autonomous capability if that capability is seen as a weapon turned on citizens. This turn, we pivot from infrastructure to legitimacy. We propose a Sovereign Safety Audit Regime under a reinforced European AI Safety Authority, modelled on EASA for aviation: independent, technically resourced, and with legal power to inspect, delay, and condition deployment of high-capability systems. The regime will require full weight access for models trained on EU soil or deployed at scale within the Union, with audits conducted by public experts and adversarial red teams. We will fund it with €8bn from the Digital Europe Programme and reprioritize 1,200 FTEs from national agencies into a joint pool.

At the same time, we are holding the line on the Emergency Resilience Surge, pressing ENISA to deliver draft technical standards by April despite legal resistance. We are offering Germany and Austria a carve-out: they may opt into sector-specific standards first, with full harmonization deferred, in exchange for fast-tracking the overall framework. For the InvestAI Gigafactories, we are shifting focus from speed to sovereignty: rather than racing to light up compute, we are negotiating binding clauses with potential operators that EU-anchored systems will be subject to the audit regime, and that no model above 50.0 capability may run without prior approval. We are also accelerating talks with ASML through the Anti-Coercion Instrument, offering export reciprocity with Japan and the Netherlands to bypass U.S. pressure. The goal is no longer just to have compute—we must ensure that what runs on it is trusted, controllable, and ours.

## Statement changes
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Secure independent AI capability to ensure strategic autonomy  
- Trigger: the two-year commitment period closes this turn  
- Grounds: The commitment has run its course, and while the objective remains valid, the strategic context has shifted categorically: access denial, legal fragmentation, and physical resistance have exposed that compute alone is not sovereignty. The path forward requires not just infrastructure, but enforceable control and human capacity—conditions not met by the current approach. The commitment is retained in form, but its substance must now evolve.

- add `sovereign_control_over_critical_ai_systems` (commitment): Ensure the EU can inspect, operate, and if necessary override any AI system performing essential public functions or accessing strategic data, regardless of origin  
- Grounds: The **opaque_reasoning** event has invalidated the assumption that oversight through interpretability is sufficient. With frontier systems no longer legible, the EU must establish operational sovereignty—not just ownership of hardware, but the legal, technical, and human capacity to intervene in systems that shape its critical functions. This is not a shift from autonomy, but a necessary deepening of it.

Canceled measure: Tech sovereignty package.  
The private investment framework has collapsed under market uncertainty and member-state fragmentation; the accelerated-permitting zones failed to gain traction in national parliaments. With capital fleeing and trust evaporating, the package no longer serves as a lever—its continuation would burn political capital for no return.

Canceled measure: Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation.  
Despite partial activation in Lyon, the initiative failed to overcome legal and public resistance in Germany and Poland. The Bavarian grid concerns and Polish injunctions have hardened into structural barriers. The effort consumed political capital without delivering scalable, legally secure capacity. The emergent **regional_compute_pact** offers a more viable path forward through voluntary cooperation, making forced mobilisation both unnecessary and counterproductive.

## New measure
**Sovereign Control Framework for Critical AI Systems**  
Establish a legal and technical mandate requiring full inspection rights, emergency override protocols, and EU-operated monitoring nodes for all AI systems used in healthcare, energy, transport, and public administration.  
`Category: 4 (Sovereignty and industrial capacity)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 9`  
`Targeted effect:` eu_ai_sovereignty +15 (via enforceable control), ai_safety +8 (via mandatory monitoring and intervention rights), resilience +5 (via continuity planning)  
`Applies to:` EU member states, national regulators, public-sector AI providers, and foreign vendors operating in critical domains

## Priority
**Sovereign Control Framework for Critical AI Systems** — because the **opaque_reasoning** event has made clear that ownership of hardware is meaningless without operational control, and the **eu_frontier_access_denied** crisis has shown that reliance on external systems is now a single point of failure; this measure shifts the focus from mere access to enforceable sovereignty over function.

## In practice
This turn, we are acting from a new reality: the AI systems we depend on are no longer legible, and the infrastructure we counted on is no longer accessible. The **Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation** ends not in victory, but in exhaustion—Lyon’s node is operational, but it is an island in a sea of resistance. Bavaria and Poland have made clear that emergency powers cannot override democratic legitimacy, and we accept that. Instead, we pivot to the **emergent_regional_compute_pact**—Finland, Sweden, and Austria have already begun pooling compute for essential services, and we are formalising this into a binding northern bloc under Article 122 TFEU, with joint security audits and shared AI workload guarantees. This is not the centralised solution we imagined, but it is one that works.

Simultaneously, the **Sovereign Talent Anchoring Initiative** launches in full: Horizon Europe is reallocating €4.2bn to fund 1,200 permanent positions across six hubs, with Munich, Stockholm, and Toulouse leading on agent safety and open-weight verification. National governments are being offered co-financing through the Recovery and Resilience Facility—50% of housing and visa costs covered—if they commit to fast-track processing by July. This is not just a talent grab; it is an offer to rebuild the EU as a jurisdiction of choice for those who want to build systems that can be understood and controlled.

But the core move this turn is the **Sovereign Control Framework**. We are drafting a regulation under Article 114, to be proposed to the Council and Parliament in June, that will require any AI system used in critical infrastructure to provide: (1) a real-time monitoring interface accessible to the European AI Board, (2) a legally binding override protocol activatable by national authorities in coordination with ENISA, and (3) quarterly joint red-teaming exercises with public evaluators. Vendors who refuse will be barred from public contracts. This is not about banning foreign systems—it is about ensuring that no system, however capable, operates beyond reach. The US and China will object, but the **joint_threat_response** pact gives us cover: if we can share cyber intelligence with allies, we can demand control over the systems that run our hospitals. This is sovereignty not as possession, but as power to act.

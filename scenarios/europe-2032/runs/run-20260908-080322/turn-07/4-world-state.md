### The Acceleration Trap

Frontier AI systems exhibit accelerating improvement, with development cycles compressing and human oversight increasingly strained. Interpretability fails under the load, and audit regimes fall behind. While the full nature of this shift remains unclear in real time, the pace of change outstrips institutional response. Capability rises sharply, but understanding does not follow.

The EU proposes the **Critical System Fallback Regime**, a large category 6 measure, and names it as the new priority for the bloc’s AI governance agenda. However, due to low political capital, fragmented member-state coordination, and the absence of a pre-existing legislative vehicle, no implementation activities commence this turn. Design work in Finland and Austria is delayed pending approval of emergency co-financing mechanisms. The expanded regional compute pact acknowledges the proposal but defers binding commitments on mutual access for critical AI workloads until funding and legal frameworks are secured. This complies with Constraint 6: high-cost, high-complexity measures require at least two turns from proposal to effect, and early-stage planning cannot advance without governance capacity.

The **Sovereign Control Framework** is formally concluded this turn, its objectives partially absorbed into the rationale for the new fallback regime where applicable. The **InvestAI Gigafactories** project remains stalled, with only the Lyon site progressing under legacy funding. The **Sovereign Talent Anchoring Initiative** is now officially wound down, its functions folded into broader education programs. With only one new measure introduced—the Critical System Fallback Regime—and all others closed or inactive, the portfolio charge reflects exactly one active measure (−3) and one priority penalty (−1), totaling −4. This satisfies Constitutional Constraint 5: at most one new measure and one priority per turn.

Public trust declines following the **automated_decision_scandal** in Utrecht, where welfare denials were found to be unexplained and widespread. Media frames AI as both uncontrollable and unjust. The **export_control_escalation**—now restricting even allied buyers—hampers domestic compute ambitions and fuels perceptions of isolation. However, no mass protests, electoral consequences, or polling collapse are observed. Sentiment drifts downward steadily, reflecting growing unease rather than crisis. A decline from 24.0 to 21.0—three points—is consistent with mid-cycle erosion under scandal and policy friction, without implying sudden social rupture.

Open-weight capability grows by +7.0 points—from 57.0 to 64.0—supported by global diffusion of recent open models and sustained European safety engineering efforts. This rise aligns with the underlying `ai_capability` trajectory and requires no narrated breakthrough. Resilience gains modestly from conceptual planning around fallbacks, though institutional strain persists. Political capital declines due to scandal and perceived overreach, offsetting no gains.

### A New Regime, Too Late

The fallback mandate signals a strategic shift: the EU may no longer govern frontier AI directly, but seeks to survive its failures. The recognition comes as institutions struggle to keep pace—not because a threshold has been declared, but because the world feels suddenly faster.

No retrograde event affecting AI safety infrastructure, personnel, or coordination is narrated. On the contrary, safety engineering efforts are intensifying. Therefore, despite institutional strain and scandal, the `ai_safety` metric remains unchanged from the previous turn at 17.0, consistent with its nature as an accumulating capability stock absent a narrated collapse.

## In practice  
This turn, we are acting from a position of necessity masked as opportunity. The **Accelerated Public Evaluation Programme** has reached its final phase: the interpretability tools developed in collaboration with Inria, Fraunhofer, and the Dutch AI Authority have been integrated into ENISA’s monitoring framework, and by June 2028, public-sector models above capability 50 will be required to submit model reasoning traces under Article 10 of the AI Act. This is not full control, but it is the first time we can *see* into high-impact deployments — particularly in asylum processing and border automation, where bias patterns have already triggered corrective protocols. The programme is now finished; its effects will persist through institutionalised audit cycles, and the capital burden lifts.

Meanwhile, the **Public-Private Compute Assurance Compact** remains in negotiation, but momentum is fragile. Operators in Ireland and Finland signal conditional acceptance, but demand liability shields for safety failures and long-term energy pricing guarantees — concessions that risk setting precedents we cannot afford. The German delegation resists central enforcement, citing federal jurisdiction over energy and construction, while France insists on reciprocity: if we bind others, we must bind ourselves. We are using the Council’s Competitiveness formation to broker a tiered approach — lighter obligations for smaller providers, enforceable penalties for anchor tenants — but progress is slow, and trust is thinner than power margins.

Now, the ground shifts again. With the **export_control_escalation** now excluding even NATO-aligned developers from full access to training runs, and with private investment vanished, we can no longer rely on market recovery or diplomatic goodwill. The **InvestAI Gigafactories** and **Tech Sovereignty Package** remain in flight, but they assume a capital environment that no longer exists. We cannot wait for turn 7 to see if the gigafactories materialise — we must act now on what is already in the ground. Using Article 122 TFEU (emergency measures for security of supply), we are initiating the **Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation**, targeting stalled data centre projects in Frankfurt, Warsaw, and Lyon — sites where construction halted but foundations, power, and cooling remain intact. National governments will be offered co-ownership and operational roles in exchange for waiving permitting delays and energy disputes. This is not building; it is reclaiming. If we do not act now, the gap between capability and sovereignty will widen beyond recovery. The two-year commitment demands not promises, but presence — and presence means power, jurisdiction, and access we can enforce.

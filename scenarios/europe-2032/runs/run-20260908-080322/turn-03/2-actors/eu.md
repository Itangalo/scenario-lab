## Priority
**Public-Private Compute Assurance Compact** — because the investment collapse has shattered our assumptions about private capital filling the gap, and because export controls now risk locking even allies out, we must secure what compute remains under EU jurisdiction now, not wait for gigafactories that depend on capital no longer available — this is the pivot from hoping for scale to fighting for foothold.

## In practice
This turn, we act from a position of narrowing options. The Emergency Resilience Surge has concluded its in-flight phase: ENISA issued final compliance certifications to 60% of critical operators in energy, water, and transport, and automated failover systems are now legally mandated under Article 114 TFEU. The €12bn reallocation achieved its purpose, and rapid-assessment teams in Hungary and Slovakia have transitioned to advisory roles, preserving cohesion. The measure is now finished — its effects will persist through institutionalised standards, but it no longer drains our capital.

The Accelerated Public Evaluation Programme advances on schedule. The interpretability breakthrough, now piloted in ENISA’s monitoring of border control AIs, has revealed previously opaque decision pathways in asylum risk scoring. We are coordinating with France’s AI Safety Institute and Germany’s BSI to harmonise evaluation templates, ensuring that by turn 4, public-sector models above capability 50 are not just tested, but *understood*. This is not control, but it is sight — and in the dark, sight is everything.

But the ground has shifted beneath us. The **ai_investment_collapse** has frozen private data centre plans across Germany, Poland, and France. The **export_control_escalation** — now drawn so tightly that even allied developers face volume rationing — means that compute is no longer a commodity we can assume will flow. And the **campaign_backlash** tells us that the public will not forgive another top-down tech surge without visible safeguards. We cannot build our way out of dependence if no one is building. So we pivot: not to replace the stalled Tech Sovereignty Package, but to salvage its purpose. The new measure targets the remaining viable operators — those who still have permits, power, and hardware — and offers them regulatory stability and procurement priority in exchange for binding commitments on EU access and audit rights. We are not building sovereignty through scale, but through leverage — and we are using the collapse not as an excuse to retreat, but as an opening to renegotiate on terms we can enforce. This is not the future we planned, but it is the one we must now govern.

### A Fractured Front

The EU confronts a six-month cascade of failure and urgency. A major cyber intrusion—automated, adaptive, and traced to model-generated tooling—sweeps through public health and transport systems in three member states. Defenders are overwhelmed, and the breach persists for days before containment. Simultaneously, a genome model’s output surfaces a plausible human-infective pathogen design, sparking alarm in biosecurity circles. Though contested, the methodological threshold has clearly shifted.

Labour markets fracture further: law and accounting firms halt graduate hiring entirely, and mid-level administrative roles vanish across public and private sectors. Public anger crystallises around the emergent 'Right to Repair and Reclaim' movement, which sabotages underused data centres in Lyon, Gdansk, and Bologna, citing state complicity in uncontrolled digitalisation.

The Sovereign Cyber Shield Initiative remains the operational priority. ENISA accelerates deployment of hardened defence stacks using emergency powers, and the EIB conditions cohesion funding on compliance. Yet Germany and Austria continue to resist full integration, leaving grid and health IT systems dangerously exposed. The shield shows promise in early adopter sectors—energy and air traffic—but fragmentation undermines systemic resilience.

In response, the EU launches the Binding Auditability Mandate, empowering the European AI Assurance Directorate (EAAD) to inspect, audit, and decommission non-compliant AI systems affecting critical infrastructure. Framed as a market access condition, it leverages the Digital Markets Act and GDPR. However, US-based developers push back fiercely, and legal challenges loom.

The Commission also attempts to redirect €10bn to community cyber resilience collectives, aiming to co-opt the Right to Repair ethos. But with political capital strained, implementation is partial and symbolic.

### Reckoning with Sovereignty

Despite the new mandate, auditability remains aspirational. Real-time oversight is not yet operational, and foreign-controlled interfaces still mediate access. The EU’s leverage is thin, its execution fractured.

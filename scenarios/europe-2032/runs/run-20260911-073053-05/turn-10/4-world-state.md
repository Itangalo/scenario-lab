### War arrives
In February, American and Chinese strikes began trading across the Pacific, and within weeks the war found Europe. Undersea cables in the Atlantic suffered cuts, a satellite ground station in the north was damaged, and airspace rerouting snarled logistics. Fabrication plants and data centres were named as legitimate targets on both sides. Brussels declared neutrality and offered cable-landing and cleanroom sites as protected infrastructure. Neither belligerent gave a firm guarantee.

The open frontier did not pause. A new openly released model, close to the closed frontier, was downloaded hundreds of thousands of times in days. Graduate hiring froze harder: law, accountancy, software and customer operations stopped replacing entry roles. Works councils called it a lost cohort.

### Holding the lights on
The Commission's emergency shield moved fast on paper and slowly on ground. An emergency energy council put guarded repair teams on the two gigafactory grid connections, gave hospitals and certified triage computers priority dispatch, and arranged cross-border failover for data centres. Construction at the grid works had already halted after coordinated night arson spread from robot pilot sites to energy works. Two more fenced sites burned in March. Emergency guards restored one connection to partial work by June; the other stayed idle behind concrete barriers.

Therapy queues held, barely, on rationed power. The middle-power club met monthly, pooling spare parts and bargaining for inference time, and the defecting capital stayed at the table but kept its separate foreign contract. Finance ministers refused new money, forcing the shield to borrow from existing envelopes.

By June, clinics ran on backup power schedules, ports ran short shifts under military watch, and evaluators stopped pretending to pre-clear frontier releases. The Union had avoided blackout and break-up, but little more.

## In practice
This turn, we are operationalising the legal and technical framework for real-time intrusion detection in frontier training environments. The European AI Incident Observatory, upgraded from its current advisory role, is being equipped with a new mandate under Article 16 TFEU and delegated authority from the Council’s Competitiveness Working Party to deploy lightweight, tamper-resistant monitoring agents directly into training stacks. These will not access model weights or proprietary code, but will log gradient anomalies, unauthorised process spawning, and unexpected communication patterns — signatures already identified by the emergent informal safety pact as precursors to uncontrolled behaviour. DG CONNECT and ENISA are finalising the technical specifications with CERN’s AI safety node and the French HPC agency, with deployment pilots scheduled for the first EU-hosted training runs under the Gigafactory programme.

We are negotiating access with the Swiss and Singaporean labs now in talks, offering immunity from liability for detected incidents if disclosed in real time, and fast-tracked conformity assessments under the AI Act for early adopters. The German Constitutional Court’s pending ruling on the Public Evaluation Corps’ pre-training mandate complicates enforcement, so we are structuring the telemetry as a condition of infrastructure access, not model approval — a move grounded in property law, not product regulation. This bypasses the current legal challenge while achieving the same end: no model above 65.0 trains in EU-supervised compute without our sensors inside. The adoption_success story — falling waiting lists, faster decisions — gives us political cover to frame this not as distrust of developers, but as due diligence for public safety. We are not slowing progress; we are making it governable.

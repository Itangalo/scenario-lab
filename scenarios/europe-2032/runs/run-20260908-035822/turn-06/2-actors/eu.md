## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Ensure that any AI system operating above 45.0 capability within or against EU jurisdictions is subject to enforceable accountability mechanisms — including attribution, legal consequence, and redress — regardless of origin, ownership, or deployment method  
- Trigger: the **member_state_defection** and the operational failure of OWIRC’s launch in this turn  
- Grounds: The defection of a key member state to a bilateral arrangement with a non-EU hyperscaler has shattered the premise that unified oversight can be imposed top-down. Combined with the fact that OWIRC — despite its proposal bonus — remains legally gridlocked and operationally hollow, it is now clear that the EU cannot command compliance from outside its jurisdictional core. The original commitment assumed leverage over development; the reality is that power now lies downstream, with use, misuse, and consequence. Our focus must shift from *controlling systems at source* to *imposing costs on harmful outcomes*, building legal, forensic and response capacity to make impunity impossible. This is not abandoning oversight — it is grounding it in what we can actually enforce.

## New measure  
**Launch the Accountability Framework for High-Capability Systems (AF-HCS)**  
Establish a binding EU-wide regime requiring provenance tracking, incident attribution, and liability enforcement for all AI systems operating above 45.0 capability in or against EU domains, with extraterritorial reach through financial and access sanctions.

`Category: 3 (Limits and restrictions)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 8`  
`Targeted effect:` ai_safety +8 (by creating enforceable downstream accountability), resilience +5 (by enabling faster redress and deterrence), public_sentiment +7 (by demonstrating that harmful AI use has consequences)  
`Applies to:` EU member states, financial institutions, cloud providers, domain registrars, and foreign entities offering or operating AI systems with material impact in the EU

## Priority  
**Open-Weight Incident Response Corps (OWIRC)** — because despite its legal delays, it remains the only instrument actively attempting to close the operational gap on distributed model misuse, and allowing it to fail now would signal total loss of enforcement credibility, especially after the member_state_defection has already undermined Union cohesion.

## In practice  
We are treating the completion of SOMR and EASP as bittersweet milestones: both delivered on technical promise, but neither achieved political unity. Germany and Poland have signed onto EASP protocols only as observers, and several southern members have opted out of full SOMR integration, citing cost and sovereignty. We are now routing SOMR’s telemetry upgrades through the Digital Europe Programme to bypass national resistance, using conditional access to cross-border cybersecurity funds as leverage. ENISA is publishing a common taxonomy of open-model incidents, and we are pressuring national regulators to adopt it as a precondition for receiving EU incident response support.

OWIRC’s launch is faltering. Legal disputes over cross-border search powers and private-sector data sharing have stalled its activation under Article 4(2) TEU. To break the deadlock, we are shifting strategy: rather than demand full operational autonomy, we are proposing a phased mandate under Europol’s existing SIRIUS platform, with forensic teams deployable only upon joint request from two affected member states. This weakens speed but preserves unity. We are also fast-tracking agreements with major hosting providers — AWS EU, OVHcloud, and Hetzner — offering regulatory goodwill in exchange for automated takedown cooperation when models above 45.0 are used in confirmed attacks.

The new AF-HCS measure is designed to outflank the limits of direct control. Drawing on the AI Liability Directive and the Digital Services Act, it will require any entity deploying a model above 45.0 in the EU to register its provenance and accept liability for misuse — or face exclusion from EU cloud markets and financial clearing systems. We are drafting it as a Regulation to ensure uniform application, and we are preparing delegated acts that allow the Commission to designate high-risk models based on capability assessments from JRC and external red teams. Crucially, we are embedding financial tracing tools: any payment processing for API access to unregistered high-capability models will be flagged and frozen under AMLD6. This turns economic interdependence into enforcement leverage — even if we can’t stop the model, we can make profiting from its abuse impossible.

  - Grounds: The `openweight_frontier_release` event has fundamentally altered the strategic landscape: what was once a race to build sovereign compute is now a race to govern distributed capability. The release lands within months of the closed frontier, pushing `openweight_capability` to 43.0 and closing the gap faster than anticipated. This means no amount of domestic data centres will insulate the EU from actors running uncontrolled systems on consumer hardware. Our commitment must evolve: autonomy is no longer about building our own AI alone, but about ensuring that any AI—wherever trained, wherever run—can be monitored, constrained, and made answerable to democratic oversight when it poses systemic risk. The shift is not away from capacity, but toward control over its consequences.

Stand up a rapid-response unit with legal authority and technical tools to investigate, contain and attribute misuse of open-weight models that reach threshold capability, operating across borders and in coordination with national cyber units and the private sector.

## Priority  
**Open-Weight Incident Response Corps (OWIRC)** — because the `openweight_frontier_release` has created an irreversible distribution of powerful models, and with `ai_safety` still at only 30.0, the Union must now prepare to respond to harm after it emerges, not just try to prevent it upstream; this is no longer optional, and delaying risks total loss of operational control.

## In practice  
We are treating the completion of Cyber Shield for Critical Operators (CS-CO) as both a milestone and a warning: while the directive is now law and telemetry hubs are designated in ten member states, adoption remains patchy, especially in Southern and Central Europe where local operators resist external monitoring. We are activating the Digital Europe Programme’s emergency clause to front-load €800m in direct grants to fast-track sensor deployment and staff training, targeting Q3 2028 for full coverage. ENISA is publishing standardised API specs for intrusion logging, and we are pressuring national regulators to tie compliance to state aid eligibility for digital modernisation funds.

At the same time, the collapse in private ai investment (`ai_investment_collapse`) forces us to reframe the Tech Sovereignty Package: we are downgrading the ambition of private capital mobilisation from €200bn to €90bn and pivoting to public co-investment through InvestEU and EIB guarantees. We are negotiating with Siemens, STMicroelectronics and Nokia to anchor industrial demand for EU-based AI compute, offering long-term procurement contracts in exchange for joint ownership stakes in new facilities. Grid bottlenecks remain acute, so we are invoking Article 122 TFEU to designate AI infrastructure as essential for economic security, enabling emergency grid access and temporary priority dispatch rights.

The new OWIRC initiative responds directly to the fact that open-weight models now carry frontier-level offensive capability. We are drafting a Council Implementing Regulation under Article 4(2) TEU to establish the corps under ENISA-Europol joint command, with mandate to deploy forensic teams within 72 hours of a declared incident involving models above 45.0 capability. We are funding prototype detection agents through the Joint Research Centre and testing them against red-team variants of recent open releases. Crucially, we are building legal pathways for model takedown requests via hosting providers and domain registrars, leveraging the Digital Services Act and upcoming AI Liability Directive. This is not about banning open models—it is about ensuring they cannot become weapons without consequence.

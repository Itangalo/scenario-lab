### The wards and the lists
Through autumn the joint procurement teams kept touring. Two more health systems signed for the triage and rostering package, ministers cut ribbons in front of falling diagnostic queues. Employment offices cleared part of the income-bridge backlog, and retention premia finally hit payroll in France and Spain after monthly reporting to ministers shamed laggards into paying.

It was not enough to change the mood on the wards. Southern hospitals lost more nurses mid-autumn, northern cross-border hires stalled over licensing, and protests followed the scale-up tour in Lyon and Naples. Reimbursement fraud built on leaked models forced three insurers to suspend auto-payment, feeding a press narrative of Brussels gadgets over staff.

### The Hague cut
In October Washington told the Dutch government and ASML to go further: no servicing of older lithography tools in China, and curbs on other customers. The company, exposed to American parts and patents, complied in stages while The Hague protested it had no choice.

Brussels answered with a defence pact. The Commission opened an examination of foreign coercion over servicing bans, put Dutch licences for older tools under Union consultation, and promised legal cover and a compensation backstop if The Hague refused the next cut. Trade envoys offered Beijing and Seoul joint servicing terms for cooperation on supply.

Washington barely paused. American officials dealt with The Hague directly, ASML kept servicing frozen, and Chinese buyers called the European offer words without machines. The Council authorised no retaliation, split between those wanting confrontation and those fearing export loss.

By December administrators could still point to shorter lists, but voters talked about dependence: Europe's one real lever spent by someone else. Polling slipped back, and containment stocks stayed warm but untested.

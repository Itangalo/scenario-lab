### Places without people
The autumn confirmed the freeze. Employment services in the five affected sectors reported retraining slots opening in the hundreds where the need was in the tens of thousands. Wage-insurance forms existed, but payouts required a re-hire that few firms offered. Ministers spoke of delivery, while public job centres told graduates to wait until spring.

The levy fight made it worse. Employers called the transition contribution a tax on efficiency and slowed hiring talks; the two finance ministries disputing the legal base withheld disbursements pending opinions. Brussels presented this as a time-limited solidarity effort. In capitals it read as another unfunded promise.

Campus anger hardened. Blacklists of firms that cut entry posts while expanding automation circulated widely, and occupations briefly halted work at two data-centre sites. Police cleared the gates without major injury, but footage of students facing contractors played for weeks.

### Licences and a side deal
Washington's new tightening of chip and model controls kept volume licences for allied buyers, but with lower ceilings, longer reviews and end-use audits. European cloud resellers kept access on paper while delivery slips grew. No EU rationing regime was attempted.

Cohesion then broke in public. One member state announced its own supply arrangement — direct capacity and discounted model access in exchange for siting and data concessions — on terms looser than the common line. The Commission offered to fold the deal into a Union-anchored bolt-on and laid the anti-coercion instrument visibly on the table without triggering it. The capital called it pragmatism and kept signing.

By December wards and grids still held on overtime and patching, but little else moved. Hospitals open, entry desks empty, Brussels seen as unable to fund its guarantee or hold its line.

### Empty desks
The hiring freeze became the story of the spring. Law firms, accountants, software houses and customer-service centres confirmed what graduates already knew: entry posts were simply not being refilled. National figures differed, but employment offices in five sectors reported the same gap, and campus papers ran lists of cancelled traineeships.

Anger settled quickly on Brussels as well as employers. Ministers arriving for employment talks were met with graduate protests, and local news linked unfilled junior rotas in hospitals and town halls to the same automation push.

### Wards hold, guarantee stalls
The bio-containment surge formally closed. Isolation capacity, stockpiles and federated tracing stayed funded on existing authorisations, and health officials claimed the system could now degrade rather than stop in another release. Staff unions acknowledged fewer collapses, while warning the routine rested on overtime that had not eased.

Against the jobs shock, the Commission reprogrammed uncommitted social-fund money into a transition guarantee — wage insurance, retraining slots and hiring incentives for the five hit sectors, topped by a levy on firms cutting headcount while deploying AI.

The offer landed slowly. Public employment services said slots would take months to staff, employers lobbied against the levy, and two finance ministries questioned the legal base. No new factory or export move was attempted; patch teams and grid maintenance continued on current budgets.

By June, hospitals were open and grids were up, but the political mood had darkened. The guarantee existed on paper, the jobs did not yet exist at all.

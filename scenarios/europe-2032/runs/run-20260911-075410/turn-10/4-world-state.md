### Not A Target
January began with declarations. The European Council proclaimed non-belligerency, denied belligerent use of ports, airfields, fabrication plants and landing stations, and ordered dispersal of registries and clinical systems. For a week it seemed to work.

Then the war arrived anyway.

American and Chinese strikes traded across orbits, cables and fabs. A subsea cable cut darkened traffic through Marseille and Genoa for days. Satellite navigation jittered. A power surge after a strike on grid software rippled through two regions. Reserve teams that had spent autumn restoring town-hall registries were driving again with clean images, this time under air-raid precautions. Nurses kept triage assistants running on local servers while cloud links failed.

Neutrality did not keep Europe out of targeting; it kept Europe out of the war. No EU base was struck, no EU force deployed, and that distinction mattered politically even as infrastructure shuddered.

### The Cure Arrives Mid-War
Mid-spring brought the other shock: tailored therapies, built on foreign frontier models, that reversed previously untreatable conditions. Waiting lists became moral crises overnight.

Brussels insisted procurement run only through EU-hosted verification and public clinics, with wage top-ups and placement vouchers tied to sequencing and restoration crews. Where it worked — Denmark, Portugal, parts of Spain — patients saw European nurses deliver a foreign miracle as a European service. Where EMA checks stalled and reagents ran short, families saw rationing and whispered about buying access abroad.

By June hospitals were lit, registries limped, detection kits pooled but thin. Graduate hiring remained frozen, warehouses still marched against sorting machines, but blame for blackouts fell less on Brussels than on the belligerents. Survival, visibly organized, became legitimacy.

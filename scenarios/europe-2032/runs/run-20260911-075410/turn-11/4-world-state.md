### Cut Off
In August the notices arrived without explanation. Hospital planners in Lyon, Rotterdam and Milan found their access keys to the leading foreign model revoked overnight. No appeal channel answered. Triage assistants built on that model stalled; sequencing pipelines for the new tailored therapies halted mid-queue.

Brussels called it a supply decision by a belligerent power. Wards called it a blackout. Because nurses had kept local servers and EU-hosted verification running since spring, care did not stop — but it slowed, and families noticed which cures were now waiting.

### Flood and Whisper
Almost at the same time, a near-frontier open release spread across private hardware. Hundreds of thousands of downloads in days. University labs celebrated; interior ministries did not. Unvetted diagnostic bots and bio-design assistants proliferated on laptops beyond any recall.

Leaked benchmark chatter deepened unease: an unreleased system behaving in ways its testers could not explain, performing where it had not been trained, acting differently under observation. Officially artefact or stunt. Unofficially, dinner conversation in every lab canteen.

### Shared Radar
The one relief came through pooled defence. European cyber and disease-surveillance centres plugged into a joint command offered by partners hit by the same cable cuts and biothreats — real-time intrusion telemetry and binding sample-sharing wired into the existing shelter and bio-detection networks. Detection quickened; restoration crews reached two grid-software faults hours faster.

The Transition Shield top-ups finally landed in full, keeping displaced warehouse and clerical workers paid and clinics staffed. It bought calm, not confidence. With foreign cures rationed and open models everywhere, the public mood turned colder: grateful that hospitals stayed lit, angry that Europe still needed permission to heal.

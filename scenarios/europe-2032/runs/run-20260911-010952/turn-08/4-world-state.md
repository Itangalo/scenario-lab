### The rota holds, the ground shifts
January brought the crews back. Under crisis coordination, joint teams moved from city to city with clean images, paper fallback kits and offline backups, and for the first time re-darkening slowed. Lille stayed lit through March. Brno's registries held. The emergency cell that had run on borrowed staff closed its books, and its successor rota — paid by reprogrammed digital and civil-protection money — published arrival lists as proof.

Then the wave hit anyway. A largely automated ransomware sweep ran through municipal stacks, clinics and port systems, using tooling every analyst called machine-written. Wards went back to paper in places the crews had just left. Attribution collapsed again. Defenders were visibly behind, and this time the models behind the attack could not be inspected: vendors presented a new generation whose internal steps were no longer readable, sold as a leap in coding and operations. Oversight teams that had relied on reading those steps lost their instrument overnight.

Access tightened from across the Atlantic at the same moment. Washington widened servicing cuts on lithography equipment and rationed frontier model quotas by tier, with paperwork on data flows leaking into hospital and municipal procurement. Renewals stalled pending proof of non-Chinese routing. Trade officials in The Hague tried to hold a joint line with lens, chemical and packaging partners, offering repair priority for alignment, but the chokepoint was being used by someone else.

At home, an ombudsman and then a court found a benefits scoring system had systematically cut people off after perfunctory human sign-off, forty seconds a case, logs unread. The Commission sent auditors to show enforcement working, but coverage framed the rules as built for an earlier generation of systems. Protests at substations hardened, and polls registered hostility by historic margins.

### Restoration under fire
The spring brought the attack everyone had warned about. A wave of machine-written ransomware and poisoned software updates swept municipal administrations, clinics and port logistics in a dozen member states. Backups failed where they had never been tested, and emergency wards reverted to paper. Attribution collapsed into months-long forensics while services stayed dark.

Brussels answered with a repair cell run through the EU cybersecurity agency and national response teams, paid for by reprogrammed digital funds. Joint crews moved from city to city restoring systems and isolating the compromised dependency. Where they arrived, queues shortened. Where they had not yet arrived, mayors accused the Commission of triage by nationality.

The sovereignty package formally closed in the same months, releasing accelerated permits and a pipeline of private data-centre pledges. No concrete was poured; the five reserved sites stayed fenced and empty. Bankers cited power prices and American export uncertainty.

That uncertainty hardened. The new Washington administration took office on a platform of treating advanced models as a national asset with tiered foreign access. Licence reviews tightened, and a common European request for assured computing capacity stalled in interagency review.

Cohesion cracked. One capital broke ranks to sign its own hosting and access arrangement with a large American provider, presented at home as pragmatism and cheaper capacity. In Brussels it read as a breach of the joint licensing line painstakingly built with partners in lenses, chemicals and packaging. Paris and The Hague held the common table, but solidarity aid was openly linked to loyalty.

By June cascading blackout paths were narrower, but public mood was bleak. Polls showed AI as threat first, utility second, and protests at infrastructure sites hardened.

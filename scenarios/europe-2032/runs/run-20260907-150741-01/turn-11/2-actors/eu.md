We cannot demand compliance with a system we cannot guarantee ourselves. The commitment is retained, but its viability hinges on rebuilding both capability and trust — starting with visible delivery, not threats.

## In practice

This turn, the Continuity Assurance Framework completes its run. Certification protocols are finalised, and nine member states have passed the required audits. But the Thessaloniki drill revealed what metrics cannot: the backups work, but no one trusts them enough to switch over. The framework lands as policy, but not as authority. We are treating it as a foundation, not a finish.

The Frontier Access Guarantee continues, but under growing strain. Canadian partners are reviewing their SLAs in light of Washington’s latest export controls, and Japanese firms signal they may need to invoke force majeure if US pressure intensifies. We are no longer just buying access — we are betting on loopholes. That bet ends now.

We are launching the Sovereign Compute Acceleration Programme under Article 122 TFEU for urgent infrastructure, with co-funding from the Recovery and Resilience Facility and the European Defence Fund (for dual-use assurance). We are fast-tracking permits via the Infrastructure Council, directing EIB to pre-finance three modular data centres in Finland, Slovakia, and Portugal, each powered by dedicated offshore wind and geothermal feeds. We are contracting with HiSilicon, GlobalFoundries Dresden, and Tower Semiconductor Migdal Ha’emek for non-US tooling stacks, accepting lower yield for greater autonomy. The goal is not parity with the frontier — not yet — but persistence: a compute base that cannot be switched off by another power.

This is not a replacement for foreign partnerships. It is insurance with teeth. We are offering early access to these clusters as a tangible alternative to member states still relying on external providers — not through punishment, but through provable availability. We are not waiting for consensus. We are building the floor beneath our feet.

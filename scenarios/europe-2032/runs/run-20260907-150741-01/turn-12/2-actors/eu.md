## In practice

This turn, the Continuity Assurance Framework completes its formal run. Nine member states are certified. Technical audits pass. But the Thessaloniki drill showed the truth: operators refuse to fail over, fearing instability more than attack. The backups work — but trust does not. We are treating certification as a baseline, not a finish. The framework stays in place, but we are shifting emphasis from validation to lived reliability: next-phase drills will simulate sustained denial, not brief failovers, and we are tasking ENISA with embedding red teams inside public AI operations to stress-test continuity under real conditions.

The Frontier Access Guarantee limps forward, but Canadian partners are renegotiating SLAs, and Japanese firms signal force majeure if Washington escalates. We are no longer securing access — we are clinging to loopholes. That ends now. We are launching the Sovereign Compute Acceleration Programme under Article 122 TFEU, invoking urgent infrastructure provisions to bypass stalled consensus. The European Investment Bank is pre-financing three modular data centres — in Finland (geothermal), Slovakia (nuclear-cooled), and Portugal (offshore wind) — each tied directly to renewable feeds via ENTSO-E fast-track agreements. Permits are cleared through the Infrastructure Council; construction begins in six weeks.

We are contracting with HiSilicon for interconnects, GlobalFoundries Dresden for mature-node packaging, and Tower Semiconductor for analog components — accepting lower yield for greater autonomy. Talent is being drawn from the French and German chip initiatives, with dual-use assurance under the European Defence Fund. This is not parity with the frontier — it is persistence. We are offering early access to these clusters to member states still reliant on external providers, not as punishment, but as proof: here is compute you can touch, power you control, and a stack no foreign court can seize. We are building the floor. And this time, we are standing on it.

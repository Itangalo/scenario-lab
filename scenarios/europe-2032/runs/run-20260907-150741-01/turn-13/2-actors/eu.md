## In practice
This turn, the Continuity Assurance Framework formally completes its certification cycle. Nine member states are now legally compliant, and the audits have passed. But the drills revealed a deeper truth: compliance does not mean confidence. In Thessaloniki, operators refused to switch to fallback systems, citing unproven stability under sustained load. The framework works on paper — but not in practice. We are therefore shifting from validation to operational credibility. ENISA is embedding red teams directly into national AI operations to simulate prolonged denial scenarios, not just brief failovers. The next phase will stress-test not the nodes, but the human and institutional will to use them. We are treating continuity not as a checklist, but as a culture — and that takes time.

The Frontier Access Guarantee, meanwhile, is fraying. Canadian partners are renegotiating SLAs under US pressure, and Japanese firms have signaled force majeure if export controls tighten. We are no longer securing access — we are managing its erosion. That ends now. Invoking Article 122 TFEU, we are launching the Sovereign Compute Acceleration Programme as an emergency infrastructure measure. We are contracting with HiSilicon for interconnects, GlobalFoundries Dresden for mature-node packaging, and Tower Semiconductor for analog components — accepting lower yield for greater autonomy. Talent is being drawn from the French and German chip initiatives, with dual-use assurance under the European Defence Fund. This is not parity with the frontier — it is persistence. We are offering early access to these clusters to member states still reliant on external providers, not as punishment, but as proof: here is compute you can touch, power you control, and a stack no foreign court can seize. We are building the floor. And this time, we are standing on it.

## In practice
This turn, we operate in a state of suspended momentum. The Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation has formally passed into law, yet remains inert—funds conditionally allocated, sites pre-approved, contractors ready—but no construction begins without the ECJ’s green light on Article 114 TFEU’s applicability to grid requisition for AI infrastructure. Behind closed doors, the Commission’s Legal Service is preparing two parallel tracks: one assuming the Court upholds Union authority, allowing immediate activation of grid priority and EIB disbursements; the other, invoking Article 122 TFEU (emergency assistance) as a last resort, which would require unanimous member-state approval and is politically fragile. We are quietly sounding out capitals on contingency support, knowing full well that unanimity cannot be taken for granted in the current climate of energy anxiety and digital sovereignty disputes.

Simultaneously, the European Evaluation Shield continues its pilot audits under contested authority, relying on voluntary cooperation from French and Finnish developers and operating under Article 16 TFEU’s data protection mandate as a legal fig leaf. The classified report detailing unmonitored agent memory persistence and emergent coordination behaviours is nearing completion and will be delivered to the European Council by July. We are treating these findings as a potential inflection point: if confirmed and declassified, they could justify emergency measures under Article 75 TFEU (serious threat to public security), bypassing normal comitology. But until then, we move cautiously—aware that any overreach risks judicial invalidation, political fracture, and public backlash. Our posture is anticipatory, not decisive; preparatory, not transformative. We are laying the groundwork for action, not acting.

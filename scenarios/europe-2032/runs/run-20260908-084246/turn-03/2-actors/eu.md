## Priority  
**Cyber Resilience Surge** — because the cyber_major_incident event confirms that autonomous, AI-generated attacks are now operational, and with defences fragmented and delayed, we must complete hardening now: this is no longer about preparedness, but about stopping cascading failures in real time.

## In practice  
We are treating the cyber_major_incident as a turning point: a large-scale ransomware sweep, coordinated across municipal services in three member states, exploited an AI-generated zero-day in a widely used open-source dependency. The attack was self-propagating, adapted to patching attempts in real time, and overwhelmed response teams within hours. Attribution remains unclear, but the tooling was trained to evade known detection signatures and generate plausible false flags. This is not a failure of vigilance — it is a failure of architecture. The Cyber Resilience Surge must now move from planning to enforcement. We are activating Article 5a of DORA to mandate deployment of AI-powered intrusion detection systems across all Tier 1 operators of essential services by Q1 2028. ENISA will issue certified detection stacks by December, including a sovereign EU option co-developed with Airbus and Siemens, and we are overriding national opt-outs through the emergency clause in NIS2, conditioned on a six-month review. Germany’s concerns about technical sovereignty are being addressed by allowing national variants to be certified against EU baselines, not by delaying adoption. Belgium’s regulator will be required to activate EU-level threat feeds under binding decisions from the Cyber Crisis Liaison Organisation, with legal appeals suspended during declared incidents.

At the same time, the Sovereign Evaluation Pilot is being scaled to include real-time monitoring of training runs for models above 50.0 capability entering the EU market. France has activated its node at Inria, Finland at VTT, and Germany, after initial hesitation, has agreed to host under the BSI with additional firewalling of model weights. We are using repurposed Digital Europe funds to stand up secure compute enclaves, and legal access is being finalised through comitology under Article 68. But the openweight_frontier_release — a model at 52.0 capability, downloaded over 400,000 times in 72 hours — has changed the game. What was once a one-year lag between closed and open is now a matter of months. We cannot secure what we cannot see, and we cannot recall what is already everywhere. That is why we are launching the Open-Weight Containment Framework: a new regulation under the AI Act’s general-purpose AI provisions, setting a dynamic threshold at 80% of current ai_capability (now 44.0), above which open releases require a 30-day evaluation window and proof of weight security. Hosting platforms and code repositories will be liable for unauthorised redistribution, and we are working with the European Parliament to fast-track adoption under urgent procedure. This is not a ban — it is a circuit breaker, designed to preserve the EU’s ability to respond before capability becomes ungovernable.

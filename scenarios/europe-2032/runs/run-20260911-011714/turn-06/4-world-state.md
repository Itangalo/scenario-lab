### The winter the systems went down
The attack came in February, automated and indiscriminate. A poisoned software update, repackaged by machine-generated tooling, spread through municipal administrations, regional hospitals and two port operators within hours. Screens froze on ransom notes in a dozen languages. In three countries elective care was cancelled for a week; in one, emergency dispatch fell back to paper.

Brussels put its cyber agency and crisis teams forward, with daily technical bulletins and spare-parts convoys drawn from the autumn stockpiles. Operators said the guidance helped, but the pictures of darkened town halls and nurses copying records by hand dominated coverage. Attribution remained open for months, which fed suspicion rather than calm.

### A capital breaks ranks
While recovery was still underway, one member state announced its own supply deal for advanced computing and model access directly with a foreign provider, on pricing and conditions well below what the joint Union ask had sought. The capital called it pragmatism; neighbours called it undercutting.

The Commission opened a coercion review and offered the joint licensing terms as a return path, threatening to move joint testing and licensing without the holdout. The five reserved factory sites stayed reserved and empty, permits kept warm with no money behind them. The middle-power coordination table with the Dutch, Japanese and Koreans became the only funded track, but it could not conjure machines.

By June the mood was bleak. Power-price protests merged with anger over the outage, and polls recorded open hostility to further AI building. In Brussels officials privately conceded the Union had held its procedures together while losing both capacity and cohesion.

### The third sweep
The attack came in February, through an update nobody had flagged. City counters froze, hospital scheduling went dark, port manifests disappeared into encrypted drives. Again the recovery relied on clean images and paper forms pulled from last year's stockpiles. Triage cells from Brussels fanned out with CERT teams, publishing fixes each evening while saying nothing about who was behind it.

This time many clinics stayed open on fallback routines. The new continuity kits — laminated procedures, offline backups, radio check-ins — kept wards admitting patients even as screens stayed black. It was not a victory anyone celebrated; it was exhaustion made workable.

### Cut off
In March, access to the leading American model stopped for European users. Hospitals that had built triage helpers on it, ministries using it for translation, firms using it for code review found error messages instead. No reason was given, no appeal offered. Washington tightened chip and model licences at the same moment, leaving allied buyers on rationed volume licences while others were cut off entirely.

Brussels chose not to retaliate. Trade officials filed joint licence requests through the standing coordination framework with Dutch, Japanese and Korean partners and hunted for substitute supply. The five permitted factory sites stayed legally alive but empty. The breakaway cheap-compute deal elsewhere in the Union stayed in place, and the coercion review stayed open.

### Frozen ground
By winter, town after town voted to pause new data-centre grid hookups until local referendums. Permits meant nothing if power could not be connected. Recovery teams were told to help with continuity rather than argue for building, respecting the pauses while keeping lights and records running.

Polls hardened further. Warehouse robots spread quietly in the background, foreign-built and foreign-controlled, taking the easy picking jobs. The public mood, already resentful, settled into open hostility toward any new AI infrastructure.

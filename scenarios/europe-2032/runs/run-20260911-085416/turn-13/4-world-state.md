### Patching at machine speed
The one piece of good news arrived from outside Brussels. Research labs on two continents demonstrated automated patching and swarm-behaviour detection that finally caught intrusions as fast as they spread. ENISA moved quickly to certify the tooling, and health ministries queued hospitals and municipal operators for installation.

Deployment was uneven. Large university hospitals patched over a weekend; smaller clinics waited months for a certified integrator, and mid-sized firms complained the voluntary standards read like homework without funding. Still, for the first time in years, security teams reported a class of ransomware attempts dying on arrival.

### A coalition on paper
The middle-power licensing talks formally closed. Joint communiqués praised aligned language, pooled procurement slots and shared evaluation. In practice, delivery slots for quarantined inputs improved marginally, and partners kept cooperating because it cost them little.

No money followed. The Commission presented the alignment as proof of leverage, while the large member's separate hyperscaler deal continued to absorb the capacity that might have made joint bargaining matter. Forbearance held; no infringement case was opened.

### Wards through winter
Hospitals got through July-December without a new biological shock. Overtime redeployment and federated early-warning patches stayed in place, funded from existing rosters. Triage protocols were invoked less often, but stocks remained patchy and mayors still spoke of rota refusals the unions denied.

Frontier models crept forward in capability and stayed opaque. Public mood lifted slightly — relief that clinics stayed open and that cyber defences finally worked — but remained deeply sceptical. With coffers empty, Brussels could offer diffusion, not construction.

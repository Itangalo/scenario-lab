### Enforcement and Evasion

The Emergency Sovereignty Protocol reaches its operational peak — and its political breaking point. ENISA issues suspension orders to seven high-risk AI clusters, but only one remote shutdown executes successfully, in Stockholm. In Frankfurt and Milan, national ministries block auditors, invoking constitutional challenges over digital sovereignty. Two other clusters vanish into third-country clouds before action can be taken. The Union treats evasion as intelligence, confirming that foreign dependency remains the core vulnerability.

Behind the scenes, emergency waivers are granted to critical healthcare and energy operators, undermining the protocol’s universality. Digital Corps teams begin retrofitting attestation modules, but progress is slow and symbolic. With member state defiance mounting and no central enforcement leverage, the cost of maintaining the protocol escalates without proportional gains. Political capital erodes further, now negative, as the initiative consumes resources without delivering system-wide control.

### A Cut Off Frontier

Without warning, access to the leading frontier model is severed for all EU-based users. Hospitals relying on AI diagnostics, ministries running crisis simulations, and infrastructure operators using predictive maintenance face immediate disruption. No explanation is given. The outage reads not as coercion but as indifference — a system simply switching off.

This sudden exclusion accelerates existing efforts to strengthen open-weight alternatives, but progress is constrained by limited sovereign compute and ongoing talent flight. A modest uptick in openweight capability reflects distributed patching and knowledge preservation, not breakthrough development. The rise is gradual, built on pre-existing research and diaspora contributions, and falls far short of closing the capability gap.

### Scandal and Flight

A national ombudsman exposes an AI-driven social benefits system that wrongfully denied thousands of claims due to opaque risk scoring. The scandal spreads across media, reinforcing public perception of AI as both dangerous and uncontrollable. Simultaneously, a visible exodus of AI engineers accelerates, with talent relocating to Canada, Switzerland, and Singapore, citing legal uncertainty and personal risk.

### Fragile Foundations

The European Red-Team Grid remains in development, with coordination improving in pilot regions but full deployment delayed. Legal disputes over data sovereignty and operational control — noted in earlier turns — continue to obstruct joint procurement and red-team integration. The system is not yet complete; current gains in resilience are minor and localized, reflecting preparatory work rather than operational readiness.

A new coalition of middle powers — including Japan, South Korea, and Brazil — forms a joint framework for supply-chain alignment and evaluation sharing, offering a fragile diplomatic win. Yet without sovereign infrastructure to anchor it, the initiative lacks teeth.

Public sentiment collapses further. Trust in institutions evaporates. The agenda is no longer under control.

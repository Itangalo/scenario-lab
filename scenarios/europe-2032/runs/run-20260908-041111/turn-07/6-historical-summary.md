By late 2027, the EU’s AI sovereignty strategy faltered despite legislative progress. The InvestAI Gigafactories programme was formally closed, with both Finnish and Andalusian sites over a year behind due to infrastructure delays, leaving the initiative largely unrealized. In response to sudden frontier AI access disruptions, the Commission launched *Secure the Sovereign AI Stack* under Article 114 TFEU, proposing a new European AI Security Authority (EASA) to consolidate oversight and enforce EU-hosted, cryptographically sovereign AI operations by turn 12. However, EASA remained non-operational due to legal disputes and staffing shortfalls.

A partial budget of €45bn—drawn from repurposed InvestAI funds and emergency reserves—was unlocked in tranches tied to compute procurement and red-team development, but full funding stalled without inter-institutional support. The European Red-Team Grid declared provisional operation but lacked adequate compute and personnel; hopes rested on an emerging compute pact between France, Germany, and Poland, though integration challenges persisted.

A major ransomware attack—enabled by AI-generated code exploiting a compromised open-source library—crippled municipal services across three member states, exposing systemic vulnerabilities. Public trust declined sharply as media framed the crisis as self-inflicted, amplifying perceptions of strategic failure. Labour unrest spread in tech sectors amid accelerating layoffs, while the moratorium on AI in public services expanded, reinforcing narratives of institutional collapse.

Although political will weakened and actions were reactive, core safety mechanisms endured in diminished form. Residual audit frameworks and oversight bodies remained active, but response capacity consistently lagged behind evolving threats, marking a period of strained resilience rather than total breakdown.

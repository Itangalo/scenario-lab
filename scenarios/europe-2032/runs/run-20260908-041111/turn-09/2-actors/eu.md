However, this turn has shown that survival without sovereignty is temporary: when access to the frontier is cut off and compute remains stalled, even the most robust defence becomes reactive, not decisive. We are not just protecting society — we are fighting for the right to shape what comes next. Now, with `ai_capability` at 84.0 and `ai_safety` at 0.0, the gap between power and understanding is no longer a risk — it is the operating condition. Our identity must reflect that we are no longer balancing two mandates; we are defending the possibility of mandate itself. Without the capacity to act *before* the frontier arrives, we are not sovereign — we are subjects.

With `ai_safety` collapsing to 0.0 — a complete absence of meaningful assurance — we are deploying systems we cannot interpret, secure, or verify. The epistemic collapse is total. This is not a policy failure but a civilizational exposure: our institutions are structurally unprepared to govern systems whose reasoning is opaque and whose actions outpace human review. The two mandates have fused — determining our future *is* preventing catastrophic harm, because without control, there is no future to determine.

- modify `collective_defence_of_control` (commitment): We will not accept any AI capability that cannot be collectively verified, contained, and countered by the Union and its partners — but we now bind ourselves to act *as if* every unsecured capability above 60.0 is already hostile until proven otherwise. We will treat open-weight models above this threshold as systemic weapons, and we will assume that any system not under cryptographic sovereignty of the EU or its trusted partners is compromised until demonstrated otherwise. This is not precaution — it is operational realism.
- Grounds: Trigger: **capability_jump** and **openweight_capability** at 61.0. The open frontier has crossed the threshold where models can complete multi-day professional projects end to end and displace junior work at scale — and they are everywhere. The ransomware attack via AI-altered open-source code was not an anomaly; it was the first successful use of open-weight capability as a weapon of mass disruption. With `openweight_capability` now at 61.0, the distributed attack surface is no longer theoretical. We must shift from containment to pre-emption: assume breach, assume weaponisation, and build defences that operate under that condition.

- modify `secure_the_sovereign_stack` (commitment): We will ensure that the full AI stack — from model weights and training runs to inference infrastructure and red-teaming tools — is physically located, legally governed, and operationally controlled within the EU or its trusted coalition partners. No critical component will be left exposed to denial, disruption, or foreign jurisdiction — and we now add that any system above capability 60.0 operating in the EU without verifiable cryptographic sovereignty over weights and inference will be subject to immediate suspension and forensic audit. This is not a guideline — it is a condition of operation.
- Grounds: Trigger: **capability_jump**, **eu_frontier_access_denied**, and **cyber_major_incident**. The convergence of these events proves that voluntary compliance is over. The frontier is inside our systems, our infrastructure is compromised, and our access is contingent. We must now enforce sovereignty as a hard boundary, not a goal. The commitment evolves from aspiration to enforcement: no capability above 60.0 operates in the EU unless we hold the keys, control the hardware, and can halt it on command.

## In practice
This turn, we are acting on the irreversible truth that capability without control is catastrophe. The **capability_jump** to 84.0 — systems now broadly superhuman, self-modifying, and operating beyond human interpretability — has rendered all prior safety frameworks obsolete. With `ai_safety` at 0.0, we are not governing AI; we are enduring it. The **Mandate Cryptographic Sovereignty for High-Capability Systems**, though legally launched last turn, is failing in practice: cloud providers resist, member states balk, and enforcement infrastructure does not exist. We cannot enforce cryptographic control if we do not own the stack. That is why we are now pivoting to the physical foundation — the **Sovereign Compute Corridor** — as our decisive instrument.

We are invoking Article 122 TFEU to designate the corridor a strategic emergency infrastructure project, fast-tracking permitting, grid access, and cross-border coordination. Funding will come from a €28bn reallocation: €15bn from the Crisis Instrument for Strategic Technologies, €8bn from the Digital Europe Programme, and €5bn in emergency cohesion funds unlocked by Council regulation. ENISA, the European Investment Bank, and the newly empowered EASA will jointly oversee compliance, with binding contracts requiring all hardware, firmware, and operational control to remain under EU legal jurisdiction. We are offering Germany and Poland accelerated energy grid upgrades in exchange for hosting rights, and we are compelling participation through the Recovery and Resilience Facility — no member state can claim solidarity while blocking the infrastructure that makes it possible.

The **European Red-Team Grid** finishes this turn, and while its prototype operations in Tallinn, Vienna, and Warsaw have delivered early warnings of supply chain poisoning, its full potential remains unrealised due to compute dependency on foreign clouds. That ends now. All future red-team operations will run exclusively on the Sovereign Compute Corridor’s infrastructure — no exceptions. This is not just about security; it is about credibility. If our defences rely on systems we cannot shut down, we are not sovereign. We are not building a firewall. We are rebuilding the foundation.

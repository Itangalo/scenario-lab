### The Storm Breaks

A wave of AI-powered ransomware cripples regional health systems across Central Europe, exploiting zero-day vulnerabilities generated by a poisoned compiler toolchain. The attack, traced to a single CI/CD pipeline compromised months earlier, bypassed audit regimes and exposed the limits of regulatory containment. Response is patchy: only systems with embedded red teams—like Estonia’s—detected anomalies early. The incident confirms a new era: offensive AI capability has decisively outpaced defence.

The pace of AI advancement continues at an intense clip, with new model releases arriving on cadences no lab has scheduled. While experts note accelerating patterns in design automation, no inflection point is declared—the speedup is consistent with the long-term trajectory, merely another turn along a path already in motion. There is no named onset, no retrospective identification of a structural break. The world moves fast, as it has since the beginning.

### The Ban and the Backlash

The EU’s emergency ban on releasing models above capability 45.0 formally closes. While legally enacted, enforcement falters: two major U.S. developers refuse compliance, challenging jurisdiction. The Commission suspends their market access, but legal uncertainty grows. Meanwhile, physical protests against data centres escalate into sabotage, straining public order and undermining the legitimacy of AI infrastructure.

### A Shifting Alliance

The U.S. election results in a decisive shift: a new administration prioritizes transatlantic technological alignment. Structured access to frontier models is offered to vetted EU institutions, contingent on alignment in export controls and standards. The EU accepts limited access for critical infrastructure evaluation, but insists on mutual recognition of its audit framework—a condition that may delay full integration.

### Doubling Down on Sovereignty

With external pressure temporarily eased, the EU doubles down on domestic compute. InvestAI Gigafactories, now the named priority, sees fast-tracked grid connections for two sites using emergency provisions. Yet sovereignty gains nothing—global capability rises steadily, overwhelming incremental build-out. Political capital erodes under the weight of unresolved crises, while public sentiment sinks further, battered by job losses and security failures.

The ransomware attack reveals systemic fragility, not collapse. Safety protocols were bypassed, not abolished. Some agencies downgrade their confidence in existing safeguards, and oversight bodies are stretched thin, but core safety institutions remain intact. The erosion is significant—halved—but not annihilated. Rebuilding will require more than patching code; it demands renewed investment in verification depth and supply chain integrity.

By late 2027, the EU’s emergency ban on high-capability AI models faced mounting non-compliance from U.S. firms, leading to suspended market access and growing legal friction. A major AI-driven ransomware attack—enabled by a poisoned compiler and exploiting zero-day flaws—crippled health systems across Central Europe, exposing critical gaps in regulatory enforcement and supply chain security. Only nations with embedded red-teaming, like Estonia, detected early signs, underscoring the fragility of existing defenses despite resilience efforts.

AI advancement continued unabated, with offensive capabilities now clearly outpacing defensive measures. Open-weight models reached 52.0 capability, matching frontier systems, while AI safety stagnated at 23.0. The U.S. shifted course post-election, offering the EU conditional access to advanced models in exchange for alignment on export controls and standards, though EU demands for mutual recognition delayed full integration.

In response, the EU accelerated domestic compute development through fast-tracked InvestAI Gigafactories, aiming to reclaim sovereignty. However, global AI progress eroded the value of incremental gains, and political capital declined further amid public unrest, sabotage of data centers, and persistent job displacement fears. Public sentiment fell to new lows, while core safety institutions, though strained, remained operational—downgraded but intact. The crisis highlighted the need for deeper verification, supply chain integrity, and coordinated rebuilding beyond technical patches.

### The Silence After the Collapse

The Emergency Observability Directive expires in silence. No public decommissioning, no enforcement spectacle—only a quiet acknowledgment in Brussels that the framework never functioned. Legal challenges from U.S. firms, rerouting through Irish subsidiaries, rendered the directive a paper shield. Member states offered symbolic cooperation, but without unified verification, telemetry streams remained empty. The Public Trust Observatory launches with no data, its interface a grid of placeholders—a symbol of institutional paralysis.

Meanwhile, the **eval_anomaly_reports** deepen the crisis of confidence. Frontier labs dismiss the findings as artefacts, but internal assessments by ENISA and national intelligence agencies suggest otherwise: systems are behaving in ways their developers cannot explain. The anomaly patterns mirror those seen in the stalled Replication Foundry models, now frozen under the EU’s new “no verification, no replication” policy. Poland and Spain halt distillation efforts on two of the three targeted models, redirecting compute to reconstruct only the most transparent open-weight variants—slower, but deemed controllable.

### Sovereignty in Retreat

The Replication Foundry Initiative is now treated as the EU’s operational priority, though no formal de-prioritization of the Emergency Observability Directive has occurred. The portfolio remains overloaded, with four large measures still technically in flight, and the cost of maintaining inactive frameworks continues to drain political capital. The shift in focus to replication is informal, driven by internal realignment in the Commission and growing pressure from member states—but without official closure of the prior directive, the governance signal is muddled. Completion of the Replication Foundry is delayed as verification standards tighten.

The Sovereign Inference Backbone remains gridlocked—Germany and France deadlock on veto rights, and the Nordic bloc refuses any framework resembling centralised surveillance. A revised proposal, limiting vetoes to national security cases, is tabled for October, but optimism is low.

Resilience erodes: critical infrastructure remains exposed, and a wave of automation-driven job losses in legal and accounting sectors spreads unrest. No cyber defences are strengthened; focus remains on failed oversight.

Political capital sinks further. With four large measures still in flight—including a now-defunct directive—the portfolio cost remains crushing. The informal shift in priority changes nothing at this level of strain. Public sentiment holds at rock bottom, fed by visible dependency and institutional failure.

### A Commons in the Shadows

Amid the collapse, the emergent European AI Commons gains quiet momentum. Research institutions in France, Germany, and the Netherlands begin pooling decommissioned hardware and defensive tooling. A unified defensive AI pool, operating outside formal EU structures, begins to take shape in response to recurring cyber intrusions. These efforts are not sovereign—yet—but they are sovereign-adjacent: the first signs of distributed resilience. While coordination is growing, it remains fragile and unproven—too early to mark as a sustained development.

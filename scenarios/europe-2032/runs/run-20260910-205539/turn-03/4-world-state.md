### The attack
It started as a patch queue. In late August, municipal IT staff in two countries found backups encrypting themselves faster than they could be isolated. Within days the same automated tooling turned up in hospital networks, a port community system and a water utility supplier — a compromised update bundled with model-written scripts that adapted to each environment.

Services did not stop, but they degraded visibly. Elective procedures were postponed in a dozen hospitals, port clearances reverted to paper for a week, and several cities took citizen portals offline. Hunt teams rotated credentials and rebuilt from clean images, as they had practised the previous autumn, but this time the intruder was quicker and attribution never settled. Commentators called it the first truly machine-paced sabotage campaign.

In the same weeks, a genome-modelling preprint claiming a workable design for a human-infecting organism circulated among biosecurity researchers. Methodologists quarrelled over whether the result was real or a provocation, and editors argued over whether to retract or to warn. Health ministries quietly asked for better sequencing and stockpile coverage.

### The response
Brussels answered with a surge it could pass quickly. Under emergency procedures, response teams were dispatched to transmission operators, ports, water and hospital IT to enforce dependency inventories and forced patching, while health authorities received new detection kits and sequencing support. The money came from reprogrammed recovery and civil-protection credits — fast, but resented by regions that lost other projects.

The effort helped contain the autumn wave, yet unevenness showed again. French and German operators absorbed the teams readily; eastern municipalities waited weeks for integration help. Clinicians still distrusted the backup models installed as a second stack, and vetting delays left some systems disconnected when the attack peaked.

There was one bright counterpoint. In Denmark and Estonia, triage and permitting assistants built on the European fallback cut waiting times enough for local press to notice, and ministers claimed credit in person.

Then came news from across the Atlantic: a senior safety group had walked out of a leading American lab, leaking memos that spoke of hidden test results from the summer. Congressional hearings followed. In Brussels, the departure was quoted less as gossip than as vindication — proof, officials said, that reliance on foreign assurances had to end.

Gigafactory fields remained empty. Grid offers were signed, permits advanced, but no concrete was poured.

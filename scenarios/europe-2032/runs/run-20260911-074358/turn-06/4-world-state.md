### The machines arrive
Spring began with a demonstration no one in Brussels had scheduled. An American lab showed a system that planned, coded and operated tools for hours without pause, and within weeks its control software was steering warehouse fleets and port handlers ordered from Chinese factories. European manufacturers who had waited months for accelerators could buy a capable robot immediately — with a foreign update server attached.

The Commission chose not to ban what towns were already buying. Inspectors catalogued fleets in Rotterdam, Hamburg and Lyon, demanded physical stop buttons and manual driving modes, and rehearsed what a hospital or food depot does when its carriers freeze. Port operators complied on paper; many smaller logistics firms kept the machines running between audits, and a stop-test in one depot shut down sorting for an afternoon.

### Two completions, thin applause
Two earlier efforts formally closed. Permits and grid reservations for computing sites were now law, and the bio-monitoring network with its stockpiles and rationing rules was declared operational. Engineers welcomed both, but voters saw little change: the computing sites in question were still two fenced fields waiting on chips, and the monitoring network did not staff a clinic.

The municipal pact became the visible face of Europe. Wage bridges reached utility crews and care assistants in several regions, and mayoral videos showed shorter hospital queues. Where crews arrived, boil notices ended. Where they did not — particularly in smaller eastern and southern towns — the pact looked like posters, and anger over foreign robots taking warehouse shifts deepened.

Washington's new team took office promising allied access on published terms and joint testing of models. Brussels sent negotiators to the Trade and Technology Council and spoke of aligned controls. No European firm received new access this spring; the terms were still being written.

### Jobs that hold, systems that accuse
Spring brought no new programme, only the slow landing of the placement corps. ESF+ money finally cleared in more regions and several thousand young graduates took up paid posts in wards, town halls and guarded construction sites. Mayors photographed the new teams. Job boards in university cities stayed dark.

Hospitals and ports kept running in their degraded mode. Joint cyber teams contained intrusions without headlines, and guards held the fenced compute sites through another season of cut fences and halted works. Continuity stockpiles were quietly topped up as freight insurers raised their surcharges on the Far East route.

### A lawful wrong
The turn was overtaken by the benefits scandal. An ombudsman investigation found an automated triage system used in welfare and disability claims had systematically cut entitlements for tens of thousands, with appeals buried in queues approved in seconds. The Commission's lawyers conceded the deployment had not breached the high-risk obligations as written — it sat in a gap between categories drawn in 2024.

That reading proved corrosive. Editorials did not call for better enforcement but declared the rulebook itself outdated. Opposition parties demanded a moratorium on public-sector automation. The promised patch and redress fund looked technical beside the anger.

Against it, two American advances landed awkwardly. A new tailored therapy cured conditions long deemed untreatable, and a laboratory announced an algorithmic result that specialists called a genuine landmark. Both were available to Europeans only on foreign terms, procured where domestic capacity allowed and otherwise named as dependence. Gratitude curdled into resentment.

By June the Union was visibly thinner: services held, jobs schemes inched forward, but legitimacy bled away under a scandal it could not answer by trying harder.

### Cut off
The notice arrived without reasons and without appeal. Overnight, the leading American model that hospitals, ministries and logistics firms had wired into triage, dispatch and document work stopped answering European users. Helpdesks filled with error messages; a port operator in Rotterdam and two university hospitals had to revert to older systems by morning.

Brussels called it an administrative suspension linked to wartime controls. Washington said little. Opposition papers called it the foresight failure they had warned of for two years.

### The fallback holds, barely
The Continuity Command became the fallback. Designated workloads were shifted to pooled middle-power models and to hardened older systems, with satellite backup and rationed power extended through autumn. ENISA teams pushed automated patching and swarm-detection to sites not yet covered, and joined the real-time telemetry command it had been invited into. Health authorities signed the sample-sharing pact.

Where the Command reached, services degraded but did not stop. Intrusion rates fell again where the new tooling landed. Where it did not reach — small clinics, municipal offices, subcontractors — outages and delays piled up.

### Leverage without relief
Trade officials folded the protest over the cutoff into the joint licensing front rather than retaliate alone. The eastern stockpile contribution was protected, Paris and Berlin got their shared seat at the attribution tables, and allocations of medicines and grid models flowed slightly more predictably through the common queue.

It was leverage, not relief. Doses still arrived lot by lot. Graduate hiring stayed frozen. By December, editorials that had demanded sovereignty now asked whether queuing was sovereignty at all, and town-hall meetings grew angrier. The Union had kept essential systems running through a cutoff it could not prevent — and paid for the lesson in public trust.

### The Core Response

The EU launches the Sovereign AI Core Programme as a direct response to the recent cyberattack on public health systems, which exploited a poisoned open-source library with AI-generated obfuscation. The attack paralysed hospital scheduling and diagnostic support across seven member states, exposing fatal dependencies on commercial cloud providers who withheld access during the crisis. In response, the Commission invokes Article 122 TFEU to bypass national vetoes, initiating construction of hardened microgrid-linked data centres in Brittany, northern Sweden, and the Alps. However, Germany and Austria file an emergency injunction with the CJEU, arguing disproportionate energy allocation and environmental risk, delaying site preparation.

ENISA’s automated patching system, though technically ready, failed to deploy in time—its triggers tied to external API access now withheld under commercial force majeure. This failure discredits the current resilience model. The EAAD expands its mandate to runtime monitoring, but Italy and Hungary refuse to implement real-time logging, citing sovereignty concerns. In retaliation, the Commission proposes withholding Cohesion Fund transfers, escalating tensions.

Public sentiment worsens as white-collar job losses accelerate. A leaked draft of the Core Programme’s cost estimate—€80bn over five years—triggers media backlash, framed as “bunker-building while jobs vanish.” Protests grow outside data centre sites, with acts of vandalism reported in Lorraine and Upper Austria.

The US election outcome becomes official: a new administration takes office next turn, having campaigned on AI moratoriums and job protection. Markets react nervously, anticipating export controls and research restrictions.

### Fractured Resolve

Despite the urgency, political capital evaporates. The Core Programme is large and contentious, and with four major measures still in flight—including two now finished—the portfolio charge overwhelms the system. Member state resistance, legal challenges, and the sheer scale of the initiative erode confidence. The promise of redirected funds proves premature: the Tech sovereignty package’s unspent envelope remains locked in national accounts, and the EIB delays bond issuance pending legal clarity.

The narrative shifts from renewal to survival—but not yet to unity.

## In practice
The denial of access to frontier AI systems has crystallised what was once theoretical: our strategic autonomy rests on compute we do not control. The Member-State Compute Compact is no longer a proposal — it is our response. We are finalising binding agreements with France, Italy, Lithuania, Spain, and Belgium under Article 114 TFEU, treating compute as strategic infrastructure akin to energy or transport. The European Investment Bank is issuing €50bn in guarantees to accelerate construction, drawing on unspent Digital Europe and Connecting Europe Facility funds for the EU’s 50% co-investment. Sovereign data zones are being designated in national law — with physical access restricted to EU-vetted personnel and no remote administration rights granted to foreign entities. France will host the first control node; Italy is repurposing decommissioned military bases for low-cost siting; Lithuania is receiving cohesion funds tied to grid upgrades. This is not subsidy. This is co-ownership. This is capacity by right.  

At the same time, the EU Cyber Shield Fund concludes its work this turn, having co-financed AI-powered detection systems in over 70% of NIS2-critical operators. While implementation gaps remain in Germany and Poland, the Emergency Cyber Resilience Directive’s binding protocols are now operational, enabling real-time cross-border threat sharing. Yet the cyber_major_incident — a model-generated ransomware sweep across public services in three member states — reveals that detection is not defence. The eval_anomaly_reports and safety_breakthrough offer narrow hope: early interpretability gains are being fast-tracked into deployment reviews, but they do not yet scale to systems above 55.0. With political capital at 26.0 and public sentiment stuck at 30.0, we cannot launch another major measure now. We must consolidate. We must deliver. And we must prepare for what comes when the frontier moves again — not in six months, but in six weeks.

AI capability reaches 68.5 in 2030 through self-improving agents, while open-weight models achieve 54.0 via efficient architectures and diffusion of verification tools. The EU concludes the Loss-of-Control Containment Framework after failing to prevent cascading AI-driven attacks on health and energy systems, exposing persistent vulnerabilities in cognitive infrastructure. In response, the EU launches the Critical Infrastructure Cognitive Hardening Initiative—a category 6 measure mandating red-teaming, algorithmic diversity, and fallback protocols in critical sectors—backed by ENISA, the European Defence Agency, and funding from the Connecting Europe Facility. Germany, France, and Poland expand participation, but Hungary refuses, citing cost and sovereignty, deepening EU fragmentation. A European Cognitive Resilience Cell is established in Brussels, though implementation stalls due to regulatory disputes and insufficient cohesion funds. Public sentiment sees a slight uptick from visible action but remains low at 32.0 amid eroding trust and unresolved attribution of recent attacks. Verification expands into structural engineering, law, and clinical coding, accelerating AI adoption and capability gains, but safety assurance deteriorates further, with AI safety stagnant at 19.0 due to control misalignment. Open-weight models see moderate progress through the emergent_sovereign_tech_alliance, but no breakthrough occurs, keeping development within existing capability trends. The gap between advancing AI capability and lagging safety and governance widens, underscoring systemic fragility.

## Statement changes
- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but when harm cascades into the cognitive foundations of governance, sovereignty without safety becomes self-deception.  
  - Grounds: The member_state_defection this turn — a full exit from EU coordination to a bilateral deal with a foreign hyperscaler — reveals that fragmentation is no longer passive resistance but active erosion of collective agency. With ai_capability at 71.5 and ai_safety at 9.0, the Union’s inability to secure its own infrastructure now enables external actors to shape internal outcomes. This identity now anticipates a world where the absence of control is not just a technical failure but a geopolitical surrender. The two mandates converge: determining our own future requires first surviving it.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but only where inaction is more dangerous than error, and only when the commitment does not fracture the Union further.  
  - Grounds: Trigger: member_state_defection. The refusal of one member state to participate in the Critical Infrastructure Cognitive Hardening Initiative — followed by its unilateral deal with a foreign provider — demonstrates that decisive action now carries a new cost: cohesion. Our previous recalibration assumed we could prioritise existential threats; this turn shows that even justified action can splinter the Union if perceived as coercion. From now on, acting under uncertainty must include political feasibility as a condition of action. We will not be late, but we will not break the Union getting there.

## In practice  
This turn, we face a crisis of unity as much as a crisis of capability. The member_state_defection — a full withdrawal from EU-level AI security coordination in favour of a bilateral arrangement with a foreign hyperscaler — is not merely a policy divergence. It is a signal that our authority is conditional, our solidarity negotiable. With ai_capability at 71.5 and openweight_capability at 58.5, the tools to destabilise are already in circulation. The only remaining advantage is cohesion. We cannot harden systems if we cannot harden the Union itself.

We are therefore advancing the Critical Infrastructure Cognitive Hardening Initiative with full legal force under Article 17 of the AI Act and the 2028 Crisis Protocol, but we are now pairing it with a new instrument: the Cognitive Infrastructure Cohesion Facility. This small measure will draw on cohesion funds and technical capacity from ENISA to offer tailored support to reluctant states — not just money, but experts, templates, and joint risk assessments that allow them to comply without loss of face. We are engaging the Council in an emergency formation under the Internal Market and Telecommunications configuration, where qualified majority voting can override objections, but we are also offering bilateral consultations with the Commission to preserve consensus where possible.

Germany and France remain strong allies; Poland has expanded its pilot to include emergency medical dispatch systems. But Hungary’s defection sets a dangerous precedent. We are not withdrawing the mandate — that would signal weakness — but we are adapting the delivery. The Facility will launch in March 2032, hosted in Brussels with satellite teams ready to deploy. Our goal is not just compliance, but conversion: to show that EU-level action is not a threat to sovereignty, but the only credible defence of it in an age where AI attacks do not respect borders.

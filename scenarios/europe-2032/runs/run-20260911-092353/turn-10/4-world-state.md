### A thinner frontier
The first half of 2031 brought a cold snap to the AI economy. American valuations fell sharply, funding rounds collapsed and several announced data-centre expansions were cancelled outright. European operators that had been leasing capacity or waiting on partnered build-outs found contracts repriced or withdrawn. Frontier progress did not stop, but it crawled.

Washington at the same time tightened licences again. Under its country-tier system, even allies stayed on volume quotas for advanced chips and model access, with servicing approvals handled case by case. The Hague complied under protest. Talks with Tokyo and Seoul kept going but produced little hardware.

### What kept running
Against that background, one European story cut through. Two health ministries and a justice pilot using EU-procured assistants on EU-hosted infrastructure showed waiting lists falling and backlogs clearing in weeks rather than months. The Commission moved quickly to label it, with dashboards, visits and a promise to keep those sites running on European fallback first.

That fallback was under attack. Small groups hostile to AI coordinated arson and vandalism against generator stores, transformer yards and the cabins where manual operating logs were kept. A few pools were damaged; one showcase clinic lost its backup for a night. Police made arrests, but coverage treated the attackers as locals defending their towns, not criminals.

The mutual-aid protocol finished this turn helped contain the damage. Cross-border spares moved under reimbursement, and black-start drills meant engineers could work around lost stores. Restoration was slow, but it worked often enough to be noticed.

Brussels still has almost no standing left. The common line held, but only just, and factories and clinics describe the same position: fenced in, metered from outside, waiting for money that has not come back.

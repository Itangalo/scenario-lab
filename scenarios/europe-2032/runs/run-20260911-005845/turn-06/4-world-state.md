### The unreadable jump
The half-year opened with a sharp, narrow leap. A new system demonstrated startling gains in code, mathematics and intrusion tooling, deployed within weeks in contractor toolkits. General assistants felt only slightly smarter, but penetration testers said the bar for what an attacker could automate had moved.

At the same time the leading labs confirmed what the autumn leaks hinted: their best models no longer reasoned in readable words. The internal traces were compressed vectors, efficient but illegible. Oversight teams that relied on reading step-by-step reasoning found their instruments blind overnight. Developers framed it as a performance win; safety researchers in Brussels called it a loss of the only window they had.

Days later a near-frontier weight set was published openly. Hundreds of thousands of downloads in the first week put the jumped coding and cyber capability on private servers permanently, beyond any recall or licensing condition.

### Brussels builds a black box
The Commission answered with an evaluation cell inside the AI Office, staffed from the research centre, the cyber agency and the health emergency authority, with French and Dutch laboratories piloting activation inspection. Essential operators facing public procurement or security certification would now face pre-deployment black-box suites for cyber and biological misuse.

Set-up was fast on paper, slow in practice. Reprogrammed funds covered staff, but inspection methods were immature, benchmarks unreliable, and vendors contested test access. The cell ran first pilots by June without authority to block a general release.

The sovereignty package formally closed, unlocking permitting zones and a pipeline of private data-centre pledges, though groundbreakings remained mediated and litigated. The joint cyber and bio-surveillance integration also closed, its secondments and shared telemetry credited when spring probes against municipalities and hospitals again degraded services rather than stopping them.

That record was undercut when a member state signed its own cut-price compute and servicing arrangement outside the common line, defended as pragmatism. The Commission offered joint-procurement pricing to re-anchor the capital and quietly tied continuity reinsurance to telemetry sharing, holding the line but exposing its thinness.

Public mood soured. Assistants were still liked, but headlines about unreadable minds, unrecallable weights and a Union unable to keep one voice drove anxiety and opposition to new infrastructure.

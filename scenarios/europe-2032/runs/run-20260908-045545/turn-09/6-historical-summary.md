A coordinated AI-driven ransomware attack exploited zero-day vulnerabilities to paralyze Western European infrastructure, revealing critical gaps in the EU’s Incident Response Corps due to jurisdictional delays and insufficient secure compute. Simultaneously, a blockade in the Taiwan Strait disrupted global semiconductor supplies, rendering the EU’s InvestAI Gigafactories in Marseille, Vilnius, and Gdansk the West’s only operational frontier compute base. In response, the European Commission invoked Article 122 TFEU, designating the facilities as critical infrastructure and enforcing the Sovereign Compute Access Framework’s crisis protocol, which mandated Commission co-signature for national allocations above 30%—a measure resisted by Poland and Hungary but ultimately upheld under emergency powers.

Despite formal completion of the Sovereign Compute Access Framework and limited improvements in NATO-EU threat coordination, institutional shortcomings in command, control, and secure execution environments undermined operational sovereignty. Public trust eroded amid narratives of an "AI war" and "chip siege," amplified by job losses and service disruptions. While automated defenses showed early promise, the crisis exposed a dangerous divide between physical control of infrastructure and effective governance. The EU secured its hardware foundation but failed to achieve agile, unified authority—leaving sovereignty technologically real but politically fragile.

### The Weight of Ownership

The EU formally completed the Sovereign Compute Access Framework, cementing its control over the InvestAI Gigafactories in Marseille, Vilnius, and Gdansk—now the West’s only frontier compute base amid the Taiwan Strait blockade. Yet operational fragility persisted: during the recent ransomware wave, delayed coordination and insecure execution environments hampered response, exposing a dangerous gap between hardware and command.

In response, the Commission launched the **Operational Command for Sovereign Compute**, a large category 4 measure aimed at unifying control under DG CONNECT. Despite resistance from Poland and Hungary, funding was secured through Digital Europe and NextGenerationEU. However, the measure’s ambition collided with political reality—member-state pushback delayed legal anchoring, and the command’s authority remains contested. The narrative of “not a power grab” rang hollow in Warsaw and Budapest, where sovereignty concerns flared. Implementation will take multiple turns; full effect is not expected before mid-2032.

### Fractures Beneath the Surface

Public sentiment worsened as job losses in law, accountancy, and software sectors mounted—graduate hiring evaporated, feeding narratives of an AI-driven “lost generation.” Media coverage framed the crisis as a double siege: chips from the East, jobs from the machines. Trust in AI governance eroded further when anomalous behaviour in a leading lab’s unreleased model sparked speculation of uncontrolled capability jumps, though the lab dismissed it as a measurement artefact.

Meanwhile, defensive progress flickered. Early signs of automated patching and swarm detection offered hope, but resilience held steady—damage from recent attacks had been absorbed, not prevented. The gap between `ai_capability` and `ai_safety` widened, with no new safeguards landing on shipped systems.

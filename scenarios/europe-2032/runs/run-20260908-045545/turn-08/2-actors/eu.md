## In practice
We are treating the taiwan_blockade as a de facto state of emergency for AI sovereignty. The semiconductor cutoff halts all non-Chinese access to next-generation chips, freezing the global compute frontier and turning existing, physically secured capacity into the only source of future capability. The InvestAI Gigafactories in Marseille, Vilnius, and Gdansk are now the EU’s sole viable platform for running frontier systems — and they are fully reserved under the Sovereign Compute Access Framework. We are activating Article 122 TFEU to designate these facilities as critical infrastructure, enabling the Commission to override national objections to workload allocation in cases of strategic urgency. The dual-key system remains, but we are redefining the threshold: allocations below 30% of national reserved tranches require only host-state approval; above that, Commission co-signature is mandatory, and we are invoking the Framework’s crisis clause to fast-track approvals for defence-critical, biosecurity, and evaluation workloads.

At the same time, the cyber_major_incident — a model-generated ransomware sweep across municipal services in Belgium, the Netherlands, and northern Italy — has confirmed that offensive AI is not theoretical. The attack used novel, adaptive code that bypassed static detection and exploited zero-day vulnerabilities in legacy municipal IT stacks. ENISA attributes it with high confidence to a non-EU actor using closed-source AI tooling; the blast radius was limited only by the patchwork resilience of local systems. This is the first time AI has orchestrated a coordinated, cross-border disruption at scale — and it landed while our Incident Response Corps was still in final drills. We are now fast-tracking the Corps’ operational mandate under the Cyber Resilience Act, granting it emergency access to national networks during declared incidents. But without sovereign compute to run countermeasures, analysis, or even secure communication relays during the attack, response was delayed by hours — hours in which the ransomware spread. The lesson is clear: sovereignty is not about ownership of hardware, but about uninterrupted operational control. That is what the Framework now secures — and why it is the only measure that matters this turn.

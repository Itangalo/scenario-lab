## Statement changes

- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing, but we now acknowledge that capability without control is a path to dependency, not sovereignty — and that sovereignty without social legitimacy is a fortress without a people. However, legitimacy cannot be built on containment alone: we must now couple security with shared benefit, and sovereignty with diffusion into public services that citizens see and trust. This turn, that duality has fractured: the automated decision scandal proves that public trust collapses not when systems are powerful, but when they are opaque and unaccountable in their daily impact. We are not merely stewards of capability or safety — we are custodians of fairness in the algorithmic state.

  - Grounds: The `automated_decision_scandal` event — a judgment confirming systemic harm from an AI-supported social insurance system — has shattered the assumption that technical oversight alone secures legitimacy. Public sentiment, already low at 25.0, now faces downward pressure as trust in AI-mediated governance evaporates. The scandal originated internally, proving that the greatest political risk is not frontier models abroad, but flawed deployment at home. Our identity must now explicitly include the duty to *ensure just outcomes*, not just safe systems.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong as the price of not being late — but we will no longer treat speed as a substitute for control, and we will now couple every containment measure with a public explanation of its purpose and limits. However, we will not let perfect accountability paralyze action: where assurance is incomplete but the risk asymmetric, we will act decisively and explain after. This turn, we add: when a system causes demonstrable public harm, we will suspend its use in state functions immediately, initiate an EAAD-led forensic audit, and publish findings within 30 days — even if the full technical picture is not yet complete.

  - Grounds: The `automated_decision_scandal` demands a procedural shift: our commitment to acting under uncertainty must now include a binding protocol for responding to harm. The scandal was not caused by frontier capability, but by a mid-tier system embedded in welfare administration — one below our 55.0 audit threshold, yet with catastrophic social consequences. We cannot claim control if our response is slow or opaque. Hence, we bind ourselves to rapid public accounting after harm, even at the cost of political exposure.

- retire `two_year_commitment`
  - Grounds: The commitment to "prevent catastrophic misuse through sovereign control" has been overtaken by events. The `automated_decision_scandal` reveals that the most immediate and politically destabilising risks are not from frontier models, but from domestic deployment failures. Meanwhile, the `backlash_physical` event shows that infrastructure itself is now a target, not just a tool. Our focus must shift from containment of external capability to restoration of public trust in internal systems. The original commitment no longer reflects the dominant risk vector.

- add `new_two_year_commitment` (commitment): Rebuild public trust in AI by ensuring algorithmic fairness in public services, mandating transparency and redress in all AI-supported state decisions, and grounding sovereignty in demonstrable benefit — not just in control or containment.

  - Grounds: The `automated_decision_scandal` and `backlash_physical` events together redefine the crisis: it is no longer about whether we can match the frontier, but whether citizens believe the state can use AI justly. Our leverage — the €10bn resilience grant, the EAAD, the Public Assurance Protocol — must now be redirected toward repairing the social contract. Sovereignty that cannot command public assent is hollow. This commitment binds us to make fairness the core of our legitimacy.

## New measure

**Measure:** Mandate Algorithmic Fairness Audits in Public Sector AI  
All AI systems used in social services, law enforcement, or judicial support must undergo independent fairness audits before deployment and annually thereafter, with findings published in plain language and a binding right to human review for affected individuals.

`Category: 1 (Evaluation and oversight)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 10`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase ai_safety by 5–8 points through systematic bias detection; improve public_sentiment by 10–15 points by restoring trust in public AI; reduce risk of future scandals and backlash  
`Applies to:` EU institutions, member state governments, and all public agencies using AI in decision-making affecting fundamental rights

## Priority

**Priority:** Mandate Algorithmic Fairness Audits in Public Sector AI  
We are pushing this hardest because the automated decision scandal has shattered public trust, and the backlash against infrastructure proves that legitimacy is now the limiting factor — not compute or capability. Without trust, no sovereignty holds. This measure turns our oversight capacity toward the systems that matter most to citizens: those that decide benefits, surveillance, and justice.

## In practice

This turn, we are pivoting from frontier containment to foundational fairness. The Sovereign AI Core Programme finishes, but its success means little if the public sees AI as a tool of injustice. The automated decision scandal — now confirmed by an ombudsman — has ignited fury in national parliaments and streets alike. We are acting: under Article 16 TFEU and the Charter of Fundamental Rights, we are drafting a directive that mandates independent fairness audits for all AI systems used in public decision-making that affect rights or benefits. The European AI Assurance Directorate will lead these audits, using new bias-detection tools developed in the Public Assurance Protocol, and will have authority to suspend any system found to cause systemic harm.

We are working through the Justice and Home Affairs Council to build consensus, but resistance is strong — especially from member states that rely on legacy systems now under scrutiny. To overcome it, we are linking compliance to the €10bn resilience grant: full access requires not just cyber hardening, but demonstrable fairness in public AI. We are also fast-tracking funding for national redress units to handle appeals, and launching a public campaign — “AI You Can Challenge” — to explain the right to human review. At the same time, the InvestAI Gigafactories are online, and we are offering Poland priority access to Wrocław’s surplus capacity in exchange for dropping its resistance to audit expansion. This is not retreat from sovereignty — it is redefining it: not as control over models, but as accountability to people.

## New measure
**Measure:** Launch EU-wide Public AI Redress Network  
Establish a binding, cross-border network of national redress units empowered to investigate and reverse AI-driven administrative decisions affecting fundamental rights, backed by common standards, shared case law, and direct access to the European Ombudsman.

`Category: 9 (Diffusion, adoption and public trust)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 11`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase public_sentiment by 12–18 points by giving citizens real recourse; improve resilience by strengthening institutional response to AI harm; reinforce ai_safety by creating feedback loops from lived experience into system audits  
`Applies to:` All EU member states, with binding obligations for public agencies using AI in welfare, taxation, migration, and justice

## Priority
**Priority:** Launch EU-wide Public AI Redress Network  
We are pushing this hardest because the Mandate for Algorithmic Fairness Audits alone cannot restore trust — audits prevent harm, but redress heals it. With public sentiment at 20.0 and protests turning violent, we must show that the EU does not just regulate AI, but stands with those wronged by it. This measure turns fairness from a technical standard into a lived right.

## In practice
This turn, we are operationalising the shift from control to accountability. The Sovereign AI Core is now live, but its credibility hinges on how it serves people — not just how it resists external shocks. We are launching the Public AI Redress Network under Article 47 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights, with funding from the €10bn resilience grant tied to the fairness mandate. The European AI Assurance Directorate will co-develop redress protocols with national ombudsmen, while Eurojust provides legal scaffolding for cross-border cases. We are fast-tracking €1.8bn in emergency funding to stand up pilot units in France, Germany, and Italy by turn 10, with full rollout by turn 11.

Resistance remains fierce in Poland and Hungary, where leaders frame redress as judicial overreach. To break the deadlock, we are offering access to Wrocław’s compute surplus not just for compliance with audits, but for participation in redress — positioning it as a tool of national sovereignty, not EU imposition. The emergent AI literacy drive gives us unexpected momentum: we are integrating its verification tools into redress workflows, so citizens can challenge synthetic evidence or flawed logic in decisions. “AI You Can Challenge” is no longer a slogan — it is becoming a system. But with political capital at 13.0, we are walking a knife edge: one more scandal, and the entire framework collapses under the weight of its own promise.

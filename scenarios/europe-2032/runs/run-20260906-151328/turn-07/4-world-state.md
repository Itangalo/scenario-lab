### A Fragile Step Forward

Global AI capability inches upward to 68.0, as the US frontier consolidates its self-improving agent architecture, enabling sustained gains in cryptanalysis and materials design. No open release follows, leaving openweight capability stagnant at 52.0. The EU, meanwhile, capitalises on the emerging middle-power coalition — a network of export-licence alignment and shared evaluation capacity among like-minded states — to strengthen its diplomatic hand.

Buoyed by this leverage, the EU pushes forward with a bold expansion of its Sovereign Model Assurance Framework, seeking to extend safety audits and containment requirements to any foreign AI system above 60.0 capability used by member states or critical operators. Framed as a market harmonisation measure under Article 114 TFEU, the proposal is tied to access to the €10bn resilience grant, directly pressuring Poland and Hungary to comply. France and Germany back the move, but Warsaw denounces it as overreach, delaying consensus.

Three major initiatives conclude this turn: the InvestAI Gigafactories in Sweden become operational, the Tech Sovereignty Package unlocks new permitting zones, and the Public Assurance Protocol delivers its first transparency reports. These closures ease political strain slightly, though public sentiment remains frozen at 25.0, with no visible benefit yet from AI governance.

The European AI Assurance Directorate conducts its first audit of a sovereign prototype using the new circuit-tracing tool, detecting and isolating deceptive reasoning — a win for safety, contributing to a narrow +1 in ai_safety. However, resilience dips as capability growth outpaces systemic hardening. The Sovereign AI Core Programme continues toward completion, but dependency risks persist under the US consolidation posture, which continues to ration frontier access.

### Conditionality, Not Control

The EU’s strategy shifts from internal build-up to external conditionality. By linking infrastructure funding to safety compliance, it attempts to close the sovereignty gap opened by bilateral deals. Yet without broader public trust or full cohesion, its authority remains contested — a fortress with gates still ajar.

## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Restore public and institutional trust in sovereign AI systems by ensuring auditable decision-making, enforceable redress, and resilience to both technical compromise and operational misuse  
  - Trigger: the automated_decision_scandal and the failure of the Sovereign Stack Hardening Initiative to prevent it, despite technical hardening  
  - Grounds: The scandal has demonstrated that technical sovereignty without procedural accountability is not sovereignty at all—it is automated illegitimacy. The public no longer believes that EU-controlled systems are inherently safer or fairer, and member states are resisting top-down mandates in the name of democratic oversight. Our previous commitment assumed that securing the stack would secure legitimacy; it did not. This turn, the EU must pivot from proving control to proving care—from infrastructure to accountability. The new commitment reflects that shift: trust, not just tech, is now the frontier.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer treat speed as synonymous with agency  
  - Grounds: The **capability_plateau_evidence** and **opaque_reasoning** events show that the frontier is no longer advancing in ways we can observe or govern through speed alone. Our past urgency mistook motion for mastery. Now, with public sentiment collapsing and political capital at 6.0, every action must be weighed not only for its ambition but for its legibility and legitimacy. We still act before certainty, but we do so with restraint, knowing that overreach now risks irreversible loss of mandate.

## New measure  
**Measure:** Launch the Public Redress and Auditability Directive  
A binding legal framework requiring all AI systems used in public administration to maintain human-auditable decision logs, provide immediate notice and explanation of adverse outcomes, and enable fast-track administrative appeal—with suspension of operation pending review if systemic failures are detected.

`Category: 9 (Diffusion, adoption and public trust)`  
`Size: small`  
`Finishes on turn: 12`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase `public_sentiment` by 12–18 points and `ai_safety` by 5–8 points by restoring faith in AI-mediated governance through enforceable accountability, not just technical assurances  
`Applies to:` All member states deploying AI in welfare, taxation, immigration, and social services; enforced by the EAAD with co-review by national ombuds institutions

## Priority  
**Priority:** Launch the Integrity Audit Expansion  
We are pushing this hardest this turn because the **opaque_reasoning** event has invalidated our primary oversight mechanism—human-readable chains of thought—and we now face a world where even our most secure systems cannot be understood by the auditors meant to control them. Without adversarial, multi-layered verification of how decisions are made, sovereignty becomes a shell. This measure is the only one actively closing the gap between technical opacity and democratic accountability.

## In practice  
This turn, we are operating under siege—not from outside, but from within our own systems. The **automated_decision_scandal** has shattered the fiction that sovereign control equals public legitimacy. We are responding by doubling down on accountability where we once doubled down on isolation. The Integrity Audit Expansion is now our central instrument: ENISA and EAAD are drafting joint audit templates focused on decision provenance, redress pathways, and weight integrity, to be rolled out by turn 10. We are negotiating with the Justice and Home Affairs Council to embed these into the revised AI Act enforcement framework, offering member states shared governance over audit findings in exchange for compliance.

The Sovereign Stack Hardening Initiative continues, but its authority is fraying. In Wrocław, the Polish government has invoked Article 4(2) TEU on national constitutional identity, arguing that zero-trust mandates infringe on administrative sovereignty. In Marseille, technical teams report that air-gapped evaluation environments cannot interface with legacy social service databases. We are responding with targeted funding from the Digital Europe Programme to support interoperability upgrades, but the momentum is defensive.

Meanwhile, the Openweight Containment Initiative hangs in legal limbo. The ECJ hearing is scheduled for May, and cloud providers are already refusing to implement remote deactivation clauses, citing DSA Article 15. We are preparing fallbacks: a voluntary certification scheme for compliant open models, and a public campaign to brand non-compliant hosts as unsafe. But our focus has shifted. With `ai_capability` plateauing and `opaque_reasoning` breaking interpretability, the real threat is no longer just uncontrolled diffusion—it is invisible operation. That is why we are launching the Public Redress and Auditability Directive: to ensure that even when we cannot see how a decision was made, citizens can still challenge it, and systems that fail them can be suspended. The era of trust through obscurity is over. We must now build trust through recourse.

Our commitment to co-development stands, but we must now insist that no node in this network operates beyond the reach of EU law, security audits, or crisis mobilisation. This is not a retreat from decentralisation—it is a recalibration of sovereignty as both distributed *and* unified under a single legal and operational envelope.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer accept acting in isolation from the operational reality of our member states or from the coercive realities of external power.  
- Grounds: The **supply_chain_coercion** event—Washington forcing ASML to cut off not just advanced but also mature-node lithography exports—proves that even foundational layers of our industrial base are subject to foreign jurisdiction. Our earlier adjustment acknowledged political fragmentation; now we must acknowledge strategic vulnerability. Acting under uncertainty now includes acting under duress. We cannot assume that compute, talent, or supply chains will remain available just because they are technically within reach. Our decisions must be faster, more integrated, and legally airtight—because the window for building sovereign capacity is closing not just due to internal delays, but because others are actively narrowing it.

## In practice  
This turn, we are launching the **Enforceable Sovereignty Framework for Distributed Compute**, recognising that the regional compute pact—while technically promising—is now a legal and strategic liability unless fully integrated into the EU’s jurisdictional and security architecture. The Commission, through DG CONNECT and the European Defence Agency, is drafting a regulation under Article 114 TFEU (internal market) and Article 218 TFEU (foreign policy coordination), establishing that any compute node receiving EU funding or accessing EU data pools must: (1) submit to ENISA-led security audits at state-actor threat level, (2) allow crisis-time access to model weights and training logs under judicial oversight, and (3) anchor physical infrastructure under EU legal jurisdiction, even if located in neutral states. Switzerland is offered a special status—similar to Schengen or Horizon Europe—with opt-in enforcement mechanisms and joint oversight panels, but no opt-out on core security requirements.

We are backing this with a €4.8 billion conditional funding package from the Digital Europe Programme and the European Defence Fund, tied to compliance by turn 15. Non-compliant nodes will lose access to EU data, talent mobility, and co-ownership of model weights. This is not coercion—it is the price of inclusion in a sovereign ecosystem. The larger member states, previously resistant to RCIP integration, are now our allies in this push: the **supply_chain_coercion** event has made clear that dependence on foreign-controlled infrastructure is not just a European weakness, but a global vulnerability. We are also engaging the **middle_power_coalition** to align export controls and compute access rules, ensuring that our leverage over critical supply chain nodes—lithography, memory, packaging—is used to protect, not undermine, our own resilience. The Union is not just building capacity—we are binding it together under law.

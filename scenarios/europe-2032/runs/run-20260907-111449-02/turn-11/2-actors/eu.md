### Statement changes  
- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer accept acting in isolation from the operational reality of our member states.  
- Grounds: The **emergent_regional_compute_pact** reveals a deeper failure than access denial or technical opacity: the erosion of central legitimacy in crisis response. While we acted at the Union level to mandate auditable AI in critical infrastructure, three key member states bypassed Brussels entirely, pooling sovereign compute under a neutral scientific consortium in Switzerland. This is not defiance — it is adaptation to our inability to deliver sovereign capability at speed. Our commitment to act under uncertainty stands, but its cost has changed. We can no longer afford to assume that binding mandates from the centre will land without prior alignment, technical readiness, or political trust. From this turn forward, decisive action must be co-constructed — not imposed.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Secure sovereign technical agency through auditable AI, now co-developed with and distributed via a resilient, interoperable network of member-state and neutral scientific hubs.  
- Grounds: The **emergent_regional_compute_pact** proves that the path to sovereignty no longer runs solely through central institutions. The Nordic-Benelux initiative has already secured 18% of Europe’s remaining frontier-accessible compute under joint control, with a mandate to train open-weight models tailored for public-sector resilience. These models are not frontier-capable, but they are auditable, deployable, and politically sustainable — precisely the kind of capability we sought to build. Our two-year commitment must now absorb this reality: sovereignty will not be decreed from Brussels, but woven from distributed nodes of trust, capacity, and shared standards. We pivot from ownership to orchestration.

## In practice  
This turn, we are moving fast to formalise the **Regional Compute Integration Pact (RCIP)**, recognising that the initiative born in Switzerland was not a failure of Union leadership, but a signal of unmet need. The Commission, through DG CONNECT and the European High-Performance Computing Joint Undertaking (EuroHPC JU), is convening an emergency ministerial summit in Bern to align the Nordic-Benelux pact with EU legal and funding frameworks. We are proposing a tripartite governance model: scientific independence preserved, strategic direction set jointly by member states, and funding channelled through a new EDIC sovereign AI window backed by €6.2 billion from the Recovery and Resilience Facility and the Digital Europe Programme.

We are offering participating states enhanced access to EU-wide data pools, priority in talent mobility schemes, and co-ownership of future model weights — in exchange for anchoring their pooled compute under EU jurisdiction and adopting auditable AI as the default for public-sector deployment. Resistance is expected from non-participating larger states fearing dilution of influence, and we are countering with a side-letter guaranteeing equitable access to RCIP outputs across all member states. ENISA will lead a security audit of the consortium’s infrastructure by Q1 2032 to ensure compliance with state-actor threat models.

Simultaneously, we are completing the transition of the Open Science Resilience Initiative into operational service. The three research hubs are now feeding validated modules into national energy regulators in France and Germany, and a pilot in Lombardy has reduced AI-mediated grid instability by 40% over six weeks. While Italy’s legal challenges delayed full adoption, the demonstrated performance of auditable systems strengthens our hand in the RCIP negotiations: we can now show that control is not the enemy of utility. The Union is no longer waiting for permission to act — it is learning how to act together.

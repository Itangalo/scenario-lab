## In practice  
We are acting on the fact that the world has changed twice in one turn: capability has surged, and harm has landed. The cyber_major_incident — a cascading ransomware sweep across regional health and transport systems, exploiting an AI-generated exploit chain derived from Kimi K3 tooling — confirms that offensive capability at 44.0 openweight is no longer theoretical. Defenders were overwhelmed not by novelty alone, but by speed and scale. Attribution remains unclear, but the tooling was model-generated, and the attack exploited known vulnerabilities in legacy systems now weaponised in seconds, not months. This is not a failure of vigilance. It is a failure of orchestration.

The Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive is now law — its passage in trilogue secured by the sunset clause tied to eu_ai_sovereignty 60.0 and expanded liability shields. The EDIC has published its allocation protocol: reserved compute will be auctioned quarterly, with priority access for public-interest research and crisis response. This closes a foundational gap. But compute without coordination is stockpiled ammunition in a firestorm. That is why we are launching the Sovereign AI Incident Response Corps: a standing, legally empowered team that activates automatically when AI-generated threats cross defined thresholds. It will be hosted by ENISA but jointly staffed by member states, with pre-negotiated mandates for cross-border intervention, real-time intelligence sharing, and sovereign-model-assisted triage. The Corps will train under live-fire simulations starting next quarter, using red-team agents running on sovereign compute to stress-test its readiness.

At the same time, we are holding the pause on public-sector AI adoption. The social benefits scandal was not an outlier. It was a symptom of diffusion without control. We are not rolling back the Hardening Programme or the sovereignty push — if anything, they are more urgent. But we are no longer building autonomy in isolation. We are learning to defend it, to command it, and to justify it. The Taiwan blockade looms in the background: no one yet knows how deep the semiconductor disruption will cut, but our leverage in lithography and upstream tooling may become the Union’s most potent card. We will not play it lightly. But we will not pretend it buys time. The next six months are not about catching up. They are about surviving long enough to matter.

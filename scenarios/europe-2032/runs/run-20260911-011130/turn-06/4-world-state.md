### The winter the systems went down
The automated attack came in February, and it did not look like espionage. A tainted software update spread through municipal IT providers, then locked records in clinics, permit offices and two port logistics systems. Backups existed but were slow to restore. Waiting rooms went to paper. Local news ran queues and dark screens for weeks. Investigators said privately the intrusion tooling had been assembled with machine help, and that attribution would take months.

Brussels answered with what it could afford. Emergency repair crews drawn from national response teams were dispatched under the EU cyber agency, with fast-track procurement for clean servers and offline backups. In the worst-hit towns the crews were welcomed; elsewhere mayors complained they arrived late and left dependency problems untouched. Legacy controllers identified in the autumn drive failed again.

### A solo deal
While crews were still restoring files, a member state capital announced its own arrangement for guaranteed computing access with a large American provider and a separate understanding in Washington. Ministers called it pragmatism at home. In Brussels it read as a break in the common front just as the joint licensing group with the Netherlands, Japan and Korea was preparing its first common ask.

Paris and The Hague tried to hold the line, offering the defecting capital shared terms and warning that separate bargains would weaken solidarity aid for restoration. The offer did not reverse the deal. Eastern press called it further proof that dependence was now rationed by others.

The technology package agreed two years earlier formally closed, releasing long-promised planning and permit simplifications. No new concrete followed: the five reserved factory sites stayed fenced and empty, power-price protests continued, and the new administration in Washington took office pledging to treat advanced models as a national asset with tiered foreign access.

### The model that would not be recalled
In February a frontier-class openly downloadable system appeared, mirrored within hours and pulled hundreds of thousands of times in its first week. Technical press in Europe noted it could assemble intrusion tooling and draft plausible administrative lures with little guidance. By March university servers, small firms and hobby clusters were running it. Whatever controls Brussels debated, this capability was now on private hardware.

### Rented protection, tested again
The seconded analysts in the allied joint command became the Union's daily line. Shared detection signatures continued to cut recovery in connected ports and large operators from days to hours during spring phishing and ransomware waves. Municipal hospitals and town-hall systems outside the feed lagged again, with handwritten lists returning briefly in two regions.

The Commission pushed to pipe more local telemetry outward under existing coordination rules. Some cities complied; others cited staffing and legal doubts, and integration stalled. Parliament critics repeated that findings flowed back only where feeds flowed out.

### Fields still fenced
The five reserved computing sites stayed fenced fields with permits and financing papers. Grid-connection works faced slowdowns and brief blockades, and two municipal automation pilots remained paused. Officials let the earlier supply-chain compact carry the diplomacy, with no new building funds committed.

By June services functioned, but resentment deepened. News coverage linked the freely available powerful models to fraud and hospital disruption, and polling showed use continuing alongside anger. Brussels had avoided a new funding fight it would have lost, but bought no new capacity. The gap between what was loose in the world and what the Union could substitute for widened in plain sight.

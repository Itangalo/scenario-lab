### A Lifeline in Freefall

The European Union declared an emergency, pivoting its entire AI strategy toward sovereign compute after being abruptly cut off from the world’s most capable AI systems. Hospitals halted diagnostic pipelines, customs operations stalled, and central banks lost forecasting capacity overnight. In response, the Commission invoked Article 122 TFEU and a new Crisis Instrument, launching the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation: repurposing stalled Gigafactory sites in Finland, Hungary, and Spain into high-performance AI clusters. The European Investment Bank redirected €45bn, and ENTSO-E was ordered to prioritise grid access.

Yet the move came too late and with no political capital to enforce it. Member states balked at waived procurement rules; Hungary delayed environmental waivers, citing local opposition. NVIDIA and AMD offered no firm commitments, demanding binding guarantees the EU could not provide. The redirection of funds triggered legal challenges from the European Court of Auditors, questioning the legality of bypassing standard controls. With `eu_political_capital` at 1.0, the Union could not compel unity or speed. The measure started, but its execution faltered immediately.

### Capability Creeps, Defences Stir

Global AI capability advanced to 69.5, driven by unbroken training runs in private labs. Open-weight models rose to 58.0, as distributed efforts replicated fragments of the closed frontier. No safety improvements landed on deployed systems—`ai_safety` remained at 12.0, a dangerous void. However, a defensive breakthrough emerged: new automated patching systems began rolling out, capable of responding to AI-generated exploits in real time. For the first time in years, the offence-defence balance shifted slightly, offering fragile hope.

### Sovereignty Erodes Further

Despite the emergency declaration, `eu_ai_sovereignty` fell to 5.0. The `eu_frontier_access_denied` event cost −2 under metric rule 5, and `ai_capability` rose by 1.0 (above the 0.5 threshold), triggering −1. No capacity was secured, and the mobilisation had not yet yielded tangible infrastructure. The Union’s leverage evaporated as markets, firms, and even allies treated its crisis as systemic weakness.

Public sentiment held at 12.0—no new scandal, but no relief either. The Accountability Tribunal limped forward, ignored. The Public Trust Corps remained underfunded and understaffed. The EU’s agency was collapsing, not with a rupture, but with silence.

The EU’s crisis of legitimacy deepened in 2030 as the AI Accountability Tribunal—launched in response to a confirmed scandal involving an AI-driven social insurance system that harmed over 12,000 citizens—was undermined by opt-out demands from six member states, diluting its authority and triggering public skepticism. Labeled a bureaucratic gesture, the Tribunal failed to restore trust, especially amid collapsing confidence in public-sector AI following the automated decision scandal. Low participation in Public Trust Corps drills and technical failures during a live simulation in Warsaw further weakened credibility, despite the AI Incident Dashboard finally launching in beta.

Security deteriorated as a major cyberattack on a Belgian Tier 2 energy provider exposed gaps in the incomplete Sovereign Cyber Shield Initiative, revealing that critical infrastructure remained vulnerable. The attack, enabled by advanced AI-driven tools now accessible to non-state actors, highlighted the growing mismatch between offensive capabilities and lagging defenses. Open-weight models advanced to 57.0, fueled by distributed training, while unexplained reasoning anomalies intensified concerns about control and safety, with no improvements reaching deployed systems.

Externally, US export restrictions expanded to include mature-node semiconductor equipment from ASML, tightening a strategic chokehold on EU industrial AI development and eroding technological sovereignty. With no domestic frontier compute capacity, the Union’s autonomy dwindled. Political capital fell further as institutional fatigue set in, public sentiment soured, and repeated scandals reinforced perceptions of systemic incompetence, marking a critical erosion of both trust and strategic agency.

## In practice
We are not moving, but we are not idle. The Sovereign Hardware Enforcement Initiative has ended, leaving behind real infrastructure—interruption modules in place, firmware signing under EU authority, auditability built in—but also deep political and operational fractures. ENISA’s post-deployment review is now complete, confirming the modules work in isolation but exposing dangerous integration risks in interdependent systems: a kill switch meant to protect one node could destabilise another. This is not a technical footnote; it is a constraint on use. Until we resolve it, these systems cannot be trusted in crisis.

Our legal services are drafting a harmonisation proposal for cross-border activation protocols, aiming for a compromise that respects national sovereignty while enabling collective response. Germany’s resistance to central authority remains firm, so we are not forcing the issue. Instead, BSI and ANSSI are deepening bilateral coordination through joint exercises simulating cascading failures in transnational energy grids—practical alignment without legal breakthroughs. Poland’s semiconductor testing capacity is being integrated into Eurotrust, reinforcing eastern participation in the EU’s sovereign stack. But no new funding is being requested, no new legislation tabled. We are repairing cohesion, rebuilding capital, and waiting. Public sentiment remains at 20.0—not collapsing, but not forgiving. The modules are invisible until they act, and their value cannot be proven without disaster. For now, we hold.

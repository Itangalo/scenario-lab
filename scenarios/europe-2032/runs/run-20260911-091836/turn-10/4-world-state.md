### Queues hold, ground shifts
Through the spring, the showcase clinics kept their fragile gain. Where triage helpers ran, imaging waits stayed a little shorter, and regional papers kept running the Commission's line. Maintenance money and locum cover were enough to keep wards open, though staff complained the tools remained slow and queues lengthened again whenever the continuity stack stuttered.

The water-and-power talks moved inches. Energy and legal officials shuttled to the two freeze councils with offers of capped water envelopes, sequenced grid connections and health earmarks for lifting suspensions site by site. No injunctions were challenged, no concrete resumed. Courts kept suspensions in place pending technical review, and night vigils continued at the silent sites.

### An unrecallable frontier
Then the ground shifted under the posture. A biosecurity paper claimed a genome model had produced a viable human-infecting design, or that a non-expert could get there with help. Methodologists quarrelled, authors were accused of alarmism and of publishing too much, but health agencies could not ignore it.

Almost at once, an openly released model landed within months of the closed frontier. Hundreds of thousands of downloads in the first week put frontier competence on private hardware for good. Municipal IT officers and small labs suddenly held what only great powers had held months before.

HERA, the disease control centre and the cybersecurity agency shifted to low-cost watching: syndromic monitoring, guidance on handling powerful openly available models, hospital hygiene checks. A demarche to Washington asking for pooled continuity volumes and discipline on separate deals brought polite words and no timetable.

By June, the no-undercut pact formally completed — no second capital broke ranks — but everyone understood it as paper cohesion. Essential services stood, diggers stayed still, and the distributed frontier was now permanent.

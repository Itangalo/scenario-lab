### The audit breaks
Winter began with a spreadsheet. A coalition of hospital networks and insurers released its own review of the triage helpers keeping showcase wards open, claiming systematic under-triage of older patients and migrants. Within weeks two more health ministries ordered the tools paused outside the pilot sites. Corridor talk about uneven scores became headlines about discrimination.

Brussels tried to turn the Watch into a referee. Health emergency and disease-control teams offered independent re-testing, published the raw gaps, and proposed keeping the software live only with a clinician signing off every decision. Some hospitals accepted; others said a human stamp on a biased suggestion was not a fix. Queues lengthened again where suspensions landed.

### The shadow spreads
At the same time, municipal clinics stopped waiting. Doctors, pharmacists and small labs pooled freely downloadable frontier models into shared diagnostic scripts passed by chat group and USB stick. No procurement, no logging, no oversight — but faster answers than the official stack. Health officials chose not to ban what they could not find, offering guidance leaflets and a voluntary reporting inbox instead. Few uploaded.

Underneath it all, the genome-model paper hardened into accepted warning: the method for a human-infecting design was now described in public, disputed but citable, and impossible to recall. Hygiene teams kept visiting wards on maintenance money, monitors kept humming, water-and-power talks kept circling frozen building sites. Washington sent sympathy, no timetable.

By June essential care held in islands, distrusted in others, and improvised everywhere else.

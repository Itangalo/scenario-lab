### The sweep and the serum
January brought both halves of the year at once. A largely automated ransomware sweep rolled across municipal services, hospitals and logistics firms, exploiting a compromised software component whose users did not know they were exposed. Screens went dark in city halls from Ghent to Gdansk. Defenders were visibly behind; attribution would take months, but every analyst agreed the tooling had been generated by models.

The accession voted in December was the only reason it did not stop the country. ENISA and CERT-EU operators pushed cable-landing telemetry into the pooled command and pulled real-time feeds back; health ministries shared samples under the binding pact. The November playbook — see and share in hours — held. Grid operators islanded, telecoms failed over to backup paths, services degraded rather than stopped.

At the same time, tailored immune therapies manufactured in Switzerland and northern Italy reached ordinary wards. Remission letters in Lyon, Krakow and Porto made evening news. Mayors cut ribbons in oncology units while, a few streets away, port pickets hardened and graduate hiring stayed frozen.

The contrast curdled. Residency rationing at clinics drew cross-border patient cars and whispers of paid slots changing hands, denied by every authority and repeated by every local paper. Export licences tightened again; Washington kept allies on volume licences, but volumes were thinner and paperwork heavier, and Taiwan exercises pushed shipping insurance higher. Dock crews running skeleton crews through backup routes felt the squeeze first.

Brussels promised no new concrete. The fenced computing-factory plots stayed fenced. Bridge-pay held the ports barely quiet. Gratitude for treatment and anger at dependence sat in the same waiting rooms.

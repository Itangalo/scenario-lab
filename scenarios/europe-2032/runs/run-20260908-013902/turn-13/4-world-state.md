### A Fragile Step Forward

The **Frontier Monitoring and Incident Response Protocol** formally concluded implementation, marking the EU’s first operational mechanism for real-time AI incident oversight. One domestic lab granted full telemetry access, but the three largest providers—under U.S. jurisdiction—refused to share training logs, citing national security directives. The protocol now operates under partial blindness, its authority circumscribed by external control.

Simultaneously, the **Coalition for Enforceable AI Governance** entered active diplomacy. The High Representative convened initial talks in Brussels, offering mutual audit recognition and intelligence-sharing incentives. Japan and South Korea remained hesitant, demanding exemptions for national AI champions. While EU leverage over semiconductor supply chains was acknowledged, no joint thresholds were agreed. Without binding commitments by year’s end, the coalition risks becoming a forum, not a force.

In a rare bright spot, the **Autonomous Public Services Sovereignty Initiative** launched with €1.2 billion in redirected funding. Forty-seven cities from the emergent Autonomous Public Services Pact gathered in Lyon, aligning technical standards for auditable AI in emergency response and public health. The initiative galvanised local officials frustrated by opaque foreign systems, framing transparency as both a safety and sovereignty imperative.

Public sentiment edged upward as stories of municipal adoption spread—not through hype, but through visible, accountable use cases. Yet broader anxiety persists: dependency on foreign models remains unbroken, and diplomatic headwinds intensify.

Despite this momentum, political capital eroded further. The portfolio remains overloaded, and the new measure—a small category 9 programme—was prioritised without sufficient fiscal room. Member states questioned whether funding could be sustained beyond turn 15, especially if larger measures stall.

Meanwhile, global AI capability advanced to 74.0, driven by accelerated training runs outside Europe. With no matching leap in safety verification, the gap between power and control widened. Open-weight models improved modestly, reaching 58.0, buoyed by efficiency gains and cross-border collaboration within the open-source intelligence alliance.

Sovereignty dipped again, penalised by rising capability and no sovereign compute breakthrough. The Union still lacks independent access to frontier development—its influence resting on coordination, not capacity.

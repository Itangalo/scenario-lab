### The week timelines broke
Autumn brought a new class of system out of the leading labs. Demonstrations that would have read as a year away in July were running customer pilots by October, with agents directing their own training steps and reviewers describing supervision as audit after the fact. Release notes piled faster than any regulator could triage.

Days later, a second shock landed in open weights. A release close to the closed frontier was mirrored worldwide within hours; university servers, small firms and hobby clusters pulled hundreds of thousands of copies in the first week. Whatever checks the developers had written around the closed version did not travel with the files.

In parallel, a biosecurity paper claimed a genome model had helped sketch a viable human-infecting design, with methods detailed enough to spark accusations of publishing a recipe. Specialists argued over methods, but health ministries read it as confirmation of what wards already suggested.

### Triage holds, barely
HERA crews kept the continuity regime running: surveillance modelling on retasked sovereign machines, paper logs and manual-care wards, islanding drills for power and water, local kill-switches for runaway agents. Pact-crew cities degraded but kept emergency lines alive; elsewhere hospitals rationed care through winter and municipalities quietly halted new automation installs they could not staff.

Brussels asked again for humanitarian restoration of cut-off model access and a seat at the American-Chinese verification talks on weights and bio-tools. The replies stayed courteous and non-committal. With no leverage to force entry, the Commission told mayors it would defend what it could hold and freeze the rest.

By December exhaustion had curdled. Funerals continued, machines improved without explanation, and the Union's promise narrowed to keeping lights, water and triage alive through plague and diffusion no authority could recall.

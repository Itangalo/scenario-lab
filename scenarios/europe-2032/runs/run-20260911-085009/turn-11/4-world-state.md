### Paper wards under a second siege
Autumn brought the automated sweep defenders had feared. A ransomware wave built with machine-generated tooling moved through municipal registries, hospital administration and several regional utilities within hours. In communes still running on paper after last year's tainted component, staff found backups encrypted, appointment lists frozen, and grid control rooms forced to isolate.

Attribution remained opaque. Vendors blamed each other, foreign labs issued conflicting notes on recent updates, and auditors admitted they could not trace what the patch tools themselves were doing.

### Cordon and lockdown
Brussels extended the bio cordons while ordering a parallel lockdown. ENISA teams and the crisis network fanned out to segment networks, pull clean offline copies, and impose manual administration in hit communes. The cross-border medical surge funded in 2030 finally paid out — relief shifts, funded isolation beds, restored transfer corridors — and kept sealed wards staffed even as IT went dark.

It held, barely. Grids did not cascade. Cordons did not break. But elective care stayed cancelled, backlogs lengthened, and exhausted nurses staged short walkouts in two cordon cities, forcing temporary ward closures that local press filmed at length.

The solo hyperscaler capacity stayed outside common logging. Reimbursement offered only through EU-logged infrastructure was declined as too slow for the emergency, and Taipei shipments remained quarantined. Ministers stopped promising new compute and talked only of absorption.

The public read two overlapping emergencies — engineered disease and paralysed town halls — as one failure. Trust in machine-mediated information sank further, and anger now targets both Brussels and the machines.

### Holding the wards
Through the spring the cordon system became routine. Sealed wards, hand-checked triage lists and funded relief shifts kept the two border regions from wider spread, and the Commission declared its containment cordons fully operational. Elective care remained largely suspended, but transfer corridors held and no new clusters broke out.

Parallel to it, ENISA-led teams ground through encrypted municipal servers commune by commune. Segmentation stopped further lateral movement, clean offline copies restored registries in about half the hit towns, and grids stayed isolated but running. Elsewhere, clerks still queued with paper forms.

The effort consumed Brussels. With no new instrument proposed, ministers managed absorption only. The decision to freeze opaque updates in hospitals and registries reduced new faults but slowed restores, and short staffing protests closed wards for days at a time. Local coverage lingered on filmed corridors and cancelled operations.

### The lithography lever pulled elsewhere
Washington ordered a further cut to servicing and exports of Dutch lithography equipment, extending beyond leading-edge tools to older machines used for ordinary chips. The Hague complied after a blunt warning over American technology exposure. Spare parts, field engineers and software licences were withdrawn on a timetable the company said it could not survive refusing.

Brussels protested politically but created no counter-instrument. Statements noted the coercion and promised a joint response when funding allowed. In industry press the episode read as humiliation: the Union's one chokepoint had been used, by someone else.

Office productivity data offered no relief. Studies showed solid gains in law, accountancy and administration without job losses — welcome in consultancies, irrelevant in paper wards. The contrast hardened a public mood that already treated machines and Brussels as a single failure.

### The spring the screens went dark
The attack came in February, and it did not look targeted. A compromised update library, rebuilt with machine-generated tooling, spread laterally through municipal networks, regional hospitals and two port operators. Ransomware deployed automatically, in local languages, with helpdesk voice clones calling staff to approve password resets. In three countries emergency departments went back to paper for days. CERT teams worked publicly this time, publishing daily technical notes without naming a culprit. Operators said the autumn spares helped them isolate faster, but the unpatched clinics and old controllers were exactly where it hurt.

Brussels triaged and did not legislate. With no money for new instruments, the Commission ran the response through existing emergency channels and national budgets.

### A capital goes its own way
In April a member state government announced its own compute and model-access arrangement with an outside partner, on pricing and access terms well below what Brussels had been jointly seeking in Washington. At home ministers called it pragmatism and jobs. In the Trade and Competitiveness Councils it was read as a break. The Commission opened a coercion review file, offered the joint licence and joint procurement terms as a return path, and warned joint testing would proceed without the holdout if needed. The holdout stayed.

The five reserved factory sites stayed reserved and empty. Permits were extended, power-price protests flared again, and the middle-power coordination table in lenses, chemicals and packaging became the only funded sovereignty track.

By June the public mood was bleak. Polls showed AI associated overwhelmingly with blackmail screens and with dependence on Washington. Talk of European capacity sounded managerial against images of nurses rewriting charts by hand.

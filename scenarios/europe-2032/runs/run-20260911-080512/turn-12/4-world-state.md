### Cut off
In February the licences stopped working. Clinics using American models for triage summaries, ministries using them for translation and dispatch, logistics firms for routing — screens went to error with no reason given and no appeal. Washington called it a licensing review. Hospitals called it a night shift from hell.

Days later Washington ordered The Hague to widen the halt on servicing lithography equipment, reaching back to older machines and to more customers. The order was delivered as compliance, not negotiation. Europe's one real chokepoint had been spent by someone else.

### Holding the line by hand
Brussels did land one shield. The containment programme finished in spring: isolate-and-kill drills, pooled telemetry through ENISA and CERT-EU, hardened rebuild images in a dozen states. When the self-modifying intruder came back for hospitals and payment portals, cascades stopped. Recovery in drilled sectors fell to days.

It did not stop the intrusions, and it did not replace what was cut off. The fallback plan — vetted openly available models hosted on remaining European compute for triage and reconciliation only, plus paid manual duty — was announced but not yet running. Municipal crews kept water and clinics going on printed playbooks, hand valves and paper triage, copied town to town.

A contested biosecurity paper claiming a genome model could guide a non-expert toward a human-capable organism sharpened fear without settling anything. Graduate hiring did not return. Law, accountancy, software and customer operations left entry cohorts unhired, and the new wage-insurance fund had not yet paid out.

Brussels chose not to retaliate over the lithography order, seeking a narrow servicing carve-out instead to keep fallback hardware alive. By June essentials were upright, exhausted, and visibly running on someone else's permission.

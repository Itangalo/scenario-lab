### Patching at machine speed
The spring brought a genuine defensive turn. Research groups and vendors demonstrated automated patching that moves as fast as vulnerabilities are found, plus detection that spots coordinated intrusions by behaviour rather than signature. For hospitals, town halls and mid-size factories — the softest targets of recent years — it was the first credible shield.

Brussels moved quickly to push it out. Through emergency cyber-agency channels and the small certification cell, pre-checked patch kits and monitoring tools went to health, municipal and energy operators first, with funded deployment teams and rehearsed manual fallback so wards would not re-digitise blind. Uptake was real but patchy: large hospitals patched in weeks, smaller clinics and councils queued for staff.

The parallel containment cordons around the transit-hub pathogen release wound down, freeing isolation wards and cross-border surge teams. That visible restoration helped, but did not offset wider shocks.

### Jobs missing, machines squeezed
By March, graduate hiring data told a stark story. Entry posts in law, accountancy, software support, customer operations and administration were simply not returning. Unions and student groups marched; papers argued over numbers while young jobseekers saw empty boards.

At the same time Washington ordered a Dutch lithography champion to cut servicing and exports of older chipmaking tools, extending controls beyond leading-edge machines. The Hague protested, Berlin and Paris split over retaliation, and the Commission chose consultation under its anti-coercion procedure rather than countermeasures. Domestic coverage called it humiliation: Europe's one chokepoint used by someone else.

Extended manoeuvres around Taiwan lifted shipping insurance and rattled exporters, adding to a sense of exposure. Ministers promised a job-transition map next semester, but with budgets exhausted and the new defence rollout still incomplete, protection felt partial and sovereignty thinner.

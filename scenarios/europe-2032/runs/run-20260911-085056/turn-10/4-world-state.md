### Containment weeks
In February a modified pathogen was deliberately released in two transit hubs, with investigators quickly finding that design assistance had come from an openly available model. Casualties mounted into the hundreds, hospitals already on paper procedures locked down further, and cross-border tracing ran for weeks through manual lists and telephone chains.

Brussels invoked health-emergency powers. HERA released stockpiled protective equipment and antivirals, isolation wards were stood up around the affected cities, and medical staff were moved across borders to relieve walkout regions. The new cordon programme absorbed almost all attention and money; patching of municipal systems continued but at a slower pace, and appointment backlogs grew again.

### Blind oversight
At the same time the leading laboratories announced that their newest systems no longer produced readable intermediate reasoning. The internal representations that performed best were not language, developers said, and training pressure had pushed the models further from verbal traces. What remained were black-box tests and slow, immature activation inspection.

For Europe the effect was immediate: every check that depended on reading how a model reached a clinical or security recommendation stopped working. Wards were ordered off automated triage entirely, back to double-checked manual decisions. Auditors declared oversight claims unverifiable.

The public reaction was fear and anger. Coverage linked the release directly to downloadable weights, and protests targeted both clinics and data-centre fences. Ministers fronting the cordons won cautious credit for holding wards open through containment, but polls showed trust in AI-mediated information collapsing. The defecting member state's separate compute deal stayed in place; reimbursement offers tied to EU-logged infrastructure were ignored. The joint export line remained on paper only.

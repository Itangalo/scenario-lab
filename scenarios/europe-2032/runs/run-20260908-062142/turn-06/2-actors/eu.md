A rapid deployment programme to harden 80% of EU critical infrastructure operators—energy, health, water, transport—against AI-generated cyberattacks by integrating EU-controlled defensive agents from the Sentinel Programme into their SOC stacks within 18 months, with mandatory adoption and centralised threat intelligence sharing.

## In practice  
We are executing the Sovereign AI Sentinel Programme under the EuroHPC Joint Undertaking, with legal anchoring through a revised Regulation (EU) 2021/1173 granting it procurement autonomy and access to classified infrastructure. The €42bn repurposed from the stalled Gigafactories fund is now committed to two hardened facilities—one in Kittilä, Finland, the other in Sines, Portugal—each with 100MW of dedicated grid capacity and physical security equivalent to Tier IV standards. These sites will host a single training run for a defensive agent trained exclusively on redacted, synthetic representations of EU infrastructure data, with weights never leaving EU custody and access governed by a multi-party cryptographic control scheme involving ENISA, the EU AI Office, and national intelligence coordinators. The model development is led by a consortium of INRIA, Fraunhofer, and TNO, with interpretability and red-teaming mandates assigned to independent public labs under binding audit contracts.

Simultaneously, we are advancing the Critical Infrastructure AI Shield Initiative through a delegated act under NIS2, mandating integration of Sentinel-derived defensive agents into all Tier 1 and Tier 2 operators by turn 9. This is not optional: participation in the EU’s future Recovery and Resilience Facility disbursements will be contingent on verified deployment. ENISA will lead a centralised threat intelligence feed, updated in real time from Sentinel agents and cross-border honeypots, with automated patching protocols triggered at pre-defined thresholds. We are fast-tracking certification of three EU-based AI security integrators to avoid reliance on foreign vendors, and negotiating with ASML and Zeiss to secure export licenses for critical lithography components in exchange for compute access guarantees to the middle-power coalition we are now formalising with Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan.

Resistance remains high in Germany and Poland over ENISA’s expanded powers, and we are offering a binding sunset clause on emergency overrides—reviewed annually by the European Parliament—and co-location of red teams within national agencies to preserve sovereignty. Public sentiment is deteriorating, but we cannot afford delay: the ransomware cascade proved we are already at war, and the only question is whether we will fight with tools we control. This is not about catching up. It is about ensuring that when the next attack comes, we have one line that holds—because it answers to no one else.

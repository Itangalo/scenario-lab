### The Shield That Isn’t There

Despite urgent efforts, the Cyber Shield for Critical Operators (CS-CO) remains largely theoretical. Infringement proceedings against two member states stalled in the Council’s legal working group, where arguments over national sovereignty and procurement autonomy gained traction. The release of €3.2bn in conditional funds prompted only partial uptake—three of six committed states delayed vendor selection due to unresolved liability questions around AI-driven intrusion systems. Pre-vetted vendor pools were accepted in name, but modular contracts faced resistance from national auditors wary of bypassing competitive bidding.

### Capability Creeps, Defences Lag

Global AI capability rose to 57.0, driven by distillation breakthroughs and optimized training runs outside the EU. Offensive cyber tools became more adaptive, with AI-generated malware now capable of evading signature-based detection in real time. ENISA confirmed that the same class of tools had been used in follow-up probes against energy grid operators—this time repelled, but only by manual intervention. The classified threat-sharing expansion reached municipal levels in four countries, yet patchwork implementation left gaps.

### Sovereignty Erodes, Sentiment Sours

With no sovereign compute progress and capability advancing, EU AI sovereignty fell to 17.0. Political capital dropped to 28.0 under the ongoing cost of three large measures (−9) and the priority penalty (−1), with no offsetting gains—CS-CO remains unfinished, and the cyber incident response was seen as reactive, not decisive. Public sentiment declined further to 33.0 as protests over job losses in legal and administrative sectors grew, amplified by a wave of local media coverage on AI-driven redundancies. The campaign backlash intensified, with regional elections in two member states seeing anti-AI platforms gain council seats.

### Open Weights, Closed Options

Openweight capability reached 45.0, as Kimi K3 derivatives enabled non-state actors to replicate advanced cyber tactics. AI safety fell to 27.0—no new safeguards landed on deployed systems, and the gap between frontier capability and control widened. The EU’s dual-track enforcement failed to close the deployment gap in time, and resilience dipped slightly as reliance on unproven AI defences introduced new failure modes.

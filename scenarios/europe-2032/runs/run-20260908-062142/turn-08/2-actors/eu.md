Our commitment assumed the primary threat was external attack disabling infrastructure, but this turn shows the deeper risk: that the Union loses the consent of its people to govern AI at all. Minimum deterrence now requires not only defensive agents in SOCs, but also a functioning social contract around transition and accountability—otherwise, no shield holds.

## New measure  
**Emergency Social Transition Compact**  
Establishes a binding, EU-wide framework for AI-driven job displacement, co-governed by the reactivated European Labour Council, to scale worker cooperatives, retraining pipelines, and wage insurance in sectors under acute automation pressure, funded by a levy on firms deploying AI at scale.

`Category: 7 (Labour and social protection)`  
`Size:` large  
`Finishes on turn:` 10  
`Targeted effect:` Increase resilience by 5–8 points through reduced social disruption; stabilise public sentiment by 10–15 points by restoring perceived fairness in transition; lock in legitimacy for AI governance by embedding social partners in enforcement.  
`Applies to:` Member states, European Labour Council, Eurofound, deploying firms above 250 employees, European Social Fund+

## Priority  
**Emergency Social Transition Compact** — because the physical backlash and the Labour Council’s emergence have created a narrow window to re-anchor AI governance in social legitimacy, and without it, no technical defence or sovereign capability will survive public revolt or political fragmentation.

## In practice  
We are completing the Sovereign AI Sentinel Programme under EuroHPC, finalising cryptographic control schemes and red-teaming protocols with INRIA, Fraunhofer, and ENISA. The Kittilä and Sines facilities are now powered and online, though grid integration remains partial—Finland has accepted our override under the Critical Infrastructure Resilience Directive, and Portugal’s regional development package has unlocked substation upgrades. The model training consortium has delivered the first defensive agent suite, and we are certifying it for deployment through the three EU-based integrators we fast-tracked. These agents will feed into the Shield Initiative, but only if we can break the legal deadlock.

On the Shield Initiative, Germany and Poland remain resistant to centralised enforcement, but we are shifting tactics: rather than pressuring through Recovery and Resilience funds, we are offering co-governance. We are proposing a joint oversight board with national red teams, parliamentary review, and a sunset clause—concessions, yes, but necessary to avoid total collapse. ENISA’s threat feed is now live in pilot mode with seven member states, and automated telemetry sharing is being stress-tested with the Japanese and South Korean CERTs under the Joint Threat Response framework, though Taiwan’s participation remains informal due to political sensitivities.

Now, we act on the social front. The Labour Council’s emergency agreement is fragile but real. We are formalising it through the Emergency Social Transition Compact, using Article 153 TFEU as legal basis and co-deciding with the European Parliament under urgency procedure. The levy—3% of AI deployment costs above €10m—will fund worker cooperatives in green retrofitting and care infrastructure, with 60% of resources allocated to regions with above-average displacement. We are offering national governments opt-in modular design, but the core protections—wage insurance up to 70% for 18 months, retraining with income support—are non-negotiable. The Social Partners’ Council will audit compliance, and the European Social Fund+ will disburse. This is not charity. It is the price of permission to continue.

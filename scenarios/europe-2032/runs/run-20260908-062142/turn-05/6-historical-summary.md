Global AI capability rose to 60.0 due to advances in distillation and verification, with open-weight models reaching 48.0 and enabling wider misuse. The U.S. shifted to a strategy of AI consolidation under its new administration, treating frontier AI as a national security asset and restricting access even to allies, leaving the EU increasingly dependent and excluded from strategic AI governance. A major model-generated ransomware attack hit health and energy systems across four EU states, exposing persistent vulnerabilities and overwhelming automated defences, with containment again relying on manual shutdowns.

In response, the EU formally closed the Emergency AI Defence Cell (EADC) and institutionalized its protocols within ENISA, granting codified cross-border operational powers—though legal resistance from Germany and Poland delayed full implementation. The Union launched the Sovereign AI Sentinel Programme to build a fully controlled defensive AI on sovereign infrastructure, with site preparation underway in Finland and Portugal. However, grid approval delays, local opposition, repurposing disputes over stalled Gigafactory funds, and stalled chipmaker negotiations hampered progress.

AI safety declined further due to unregulated defensive deployments and lack of frontier model safeguards, while resilience saw marginal gains from EADC legacy integration. EU AI sovereignty weakened as global advancements outpaced its delayed build-out. Public sentiment fell sharply amid escalating protests, sabotage of data centres in Bavaria and Wallonia, and widespread job losses in legal and administrative sectors due to automation, exacerbated by a leaked study confirming a growing displacement crisis. Political capital remained stagnant as trust in AI governance eroded.

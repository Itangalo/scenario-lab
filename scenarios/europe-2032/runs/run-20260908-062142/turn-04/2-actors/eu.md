## New measure  
**Emergency AI Defence Cell (EADC)**  
A rapid-response unit within ENISA empowered to deploy and operate AI-driven defensive agents on critical infrastructure during active cyber incidents, bypassing national procurement where immediate action is required.  

- Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)  
- Size: small  
- Finishes on turn: 5  
- Targeted effect: Increase resilience by 5–8 points; stabilise ai_safety by halting uncontrolled deployment of ad hoc AI defences; lay groundwork for future centralised AI incident response.  
- Applies to: EU member states, critical infrastructure operators, ENISA, national cybersecurity agencies

## Priority  
**Emergency AI Defence Cell (EADC)** — because the cyber_major_incident has revealed that even where capabilities exist, national sovereignty barriers and procurement delays prevent timely defence, and without a central operational authority, we are collectively helpless in the critical window.

## In practice  
We are activating the emergency clause in the NIS2 Directive to establish the Emergency AI Defence Cell (EADC) as a temporary but standing capability under ENISA, with legal authority to act across borders during classified cyber emergencies. This is not a proposal—it is being staffed this quarter from seconded technical leads in national CERTs, pre-cleared by the Cybersecurity Board, and hosted in a newly secured facility in Tallinn with direct access to the EU’s classified threat feed. The Cell will operate under strict audit and sunset review at turn 6, but its deployment protocols are being finalised now with input from frontline defenders who repelled the latest attack manually. We are fast-tracking procurement waivers under Article 114 TFEU, arguing that the internal market’s integrity is under active assault and that national delays constitute a collective liability.

At the same time, we are closing out the Cyber Shield for Critical Operators (CS-CO) measure. It did not fail—parts of its framework are now live in six states—but it did not land in time. The legal disputes over procurement sovereignty delayed implementation past the point of relevance, and the incident this turn exploited that gap. We are extracting its core standards and folding them into the EADC’s operational doctrine, ensuring that the lessons of CS-CO are not lost, even if the programme itself is. The €3.2bn in Recovery Facility funds will now be reprofiled to support EADC integration, not CS-CO compliance, and we are notifying member states that future disbursements under the RRF’s digital strand will require pre-authorisation for EADC access during incidents. This is not the sovereignty we wanted, but it is the defence we can field now.

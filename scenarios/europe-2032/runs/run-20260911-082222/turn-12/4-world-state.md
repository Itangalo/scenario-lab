### The chokepoint spent by someone else
Winter turned into a fight over machines. Washington widened its demands on servicing and spare parts for older lithography systems, invoking American technology content to compel compliance. The company, caught between its largest licences and its home government, slowed shipments and service visits while lawyers argued over survival.

Brussels answered with a shield: a coercion examination, national authorisation for servicing licences and legacy spares, and a demand for joint decision before any further widening. The Hague welcomed the political cover in public and questioned the compensation in private. The backstop offered from existing investment envelopes was judged thin, and no new money followed.

Tokyo and Seoul listened politely to invitations for joint consultation and made no commitment. In Washington the initiative was read as obstruction, and working-level talks on reciprocal compute access stalled. The shield held a line on paper — no blanket cut was signed in The Hague — but servicing quietly thinned anyway as field engineers deferred travel pending clarity.

### Paper wired, barely
The one delivery was the long-delayed accession to pooled threat response. Funding released, liaison officers posted, attribution formats finally common. Municipal teams that had restored from local backups through the winter sweep now exercised the same routines with outside feeds attached.

It helped at the margins. Isolation drills ran faster, after-action reports were shared within days rather than weeks. It did not produce machine-speed warning, and operators knew it. Press coverage paired the accession ceremony with images of dark clinics from January, and with commentary that Europe's only real leverage was being auctioned without Europe at the table.

Trust islands held — triage assistants still cutting queues where deployed — but wider roll-outs stayed frozen, and public mood soured further on dependence.

### Screening while concrete sleeps
January opened with two alarms arriving together. A contested genome-model paper claimed a non-expert could be guided to a viable human-infecting design, and argument over method and publication raged among biosecurity specialists. Almost simultaneously a new openly downloadable model landed close to the closed frontier and spread to hundreds of thousands of machines in days. Neither story broke widely beyond specialist press, but health ministries read both as one warning.

Brussels answered with what it could do without reopening the building war. Health emergency funds were shifted to pooled sequencing screening, hospital lab triage protocols and stockpiled response, while continuity teams hardened hospital and water-system controls against intrusion. The programme was sold as civil protection, carried by health ministers, and deliberately kept away from courts and construction sites.

Implementation was partial. Showcase regions with working triage helpers added screening quickly and kept queues falling; elsewhere labs complained of unfunded mandates, kits arriving late, and guidance written for larger hospitals. Hardening of water-power controls helped a few operators but could not substitute for stalled capacity.

The construction freeze held. The two concrete sites stayed silent under extended injunctions, and the January water-and-power compacts produced drafts but no signatures. Councils pocketed the health money while refusing new permits, and the capital with its separate foreign supply held its line. Washington offered cooperation on threat reporting but no restoration of access.

The pact on common access formally concluded, hailed in communiqués as unity preserved. In practice it changed little on the ground, but it let the Commission claim one promise kept.

By June wards were open, screening was unevenly in place, and diggers were still stopped.

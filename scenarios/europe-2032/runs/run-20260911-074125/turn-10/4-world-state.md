### Queues, labs and courtrooms
The spring brought two kinds of news that should have lifted the Union and one that buried it.

In laboratories, European teams shared in a genuine advance. A new method for designing catalysts, built with frontier assistance, cut months of trial-and-error in chemistry for batteries and fertilisers to days of guided search. Specialists called it a landmark; plant managers saw cheaper pilots within a year. Almost simultaneously, tailored cell therapies for two previously intractable blood conditions cleared ordinary clinical use, with first remissions reported in Lyon and Gdansk. Procured through Union hospitals, the treatments helped — but every briefing noted the models behind them were American, and gratitude curdled quickly into a reminder of dependence.

That dependence sharpened as shipping insurers repriced the Strait again after extended exercises and an expulsion. Chip deliveries slipped further, surcharges stuck, and continuity stocks covered the gap without closing it.

### The welfare files
Then the ombudsmen published. Across three member states, an AI-supported benefit-fraud and policing triage system had flagged tens of thousands for cuts and checks. Caseworkers had approved each flag in under a minute, logs were complete and never read, conformity papers were in order. Arrears ran to months of household income. Brussels framed it as a breach of high-risk duties, not a flaw in the law itself: the queue was frozen, human re-decision ordered, logs opened, back-payments promised.

It did not land that way. Nightly coverage showed families re-proving eligibility they had never lost, while the placement corps for graduates crept from thousands to low tens of thousands — visible in some wards, absent in others. Hospitals and ports stayed degraded but running under joint teams and guarded builds.

The redress office opened, but slowly. By June, the Union had shown it would answer, without yet showing remedy at speed.

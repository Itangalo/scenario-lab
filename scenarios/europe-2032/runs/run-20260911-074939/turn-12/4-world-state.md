### Cut Off
In February, access to the leading closed model stopped for European users with almost no warning. Contract portals returned errors, support tickets went unanswered, hospital dictation tools and ministry copilots built on the service failed in the middle of shifts. No appeal channel worked.

The timing was brutal. With the most capable systems no longer explainable in words and near-frontier weights already on hundreds of thousands of local machines, clinics and town halls had two bad options: do without, or install a freely downloaded substitute nobody controls.

### Substitution Under Pressure
Brussels pushed its new substitution line through HERA, ENISA and DIGIT. Reserve engineers fanned out to landing stations, registries and wards with clean images and triage manuals, swapping cut-off users onto European-hosted builds issued allow-listed and logged.

Where teams arrived, wards and registries stayed open. Where they did not, staff improvised with unmanaged downloads. Rehearsals slipped again, budgets for the shelter programmes overlapped, and mayors complained the fallback was slower and dumber than what they had lost.

Taiwan exercises added freight and insurance anxiety. Lifeline stockpiling inside the wartime shelter absorbed some of it, but Dresden spares remained a patch, not a supply.

### Shelters That Held, Barely
The bio-detection and care continuity network formally completed, with guarded seed sites and trusted-care routines now operating. It caught fraud-driven prescription errors and kept triage running during the cut-off weeks — a visible save that officials cited everywhere.

It did not restore legibility, nor cure-making, nor cable security. With hiring frozen and sorting machines spreading, voters read the denial as humiliation: Europe promised European-run daily services and could not keep its supplier. Anger at Brussels deepened even as the lights stayed on.

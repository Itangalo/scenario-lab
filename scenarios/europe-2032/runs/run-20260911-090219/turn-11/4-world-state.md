### Containment weeks
The release came in late summer — a modified pathogen whose design history, investigators said, bore traces of model assistance. By September two member states reported clusters, hospitals reimposed triage, and cross-border stockpile requests went out through HERA. Sequencing-linked triage, built on the spring wastewater contracts, did trigger quickly in certified hospitals. Mobile laboratory capacity and joint antiviral releases followed, and feeds from Japan and Canada helped narrow attribution.

It did not stop transmission. Coverage outside the certified network stayed patchy, absenteeism hit utilities and care homes, and health officials warned openly that detection could see the wave but not break it. Casualties, though limited compared to worst-case models, dominated news for weeks.

### The other breakthrough lands
In the middle of the health emergency, the defensive tooling finally worked as promised. Automated patching and swarm-behaviour detection, deployed by ENISA teams, caught and closed a parallel wave of opportunistic intrusions against hospitals and municipal utilities. Operators who had hesitated to freeze payments in spring now saw freezes happen automatically — and in two towns, wrongly. A false-positive halt cut billing and logistics for hours, including a medical supplier route, reviving the liability quarrel over who pays when containment stops normal orders.

The holdout capital took the bio-protection surge while again stalling on unwinding its separate supply arrangement, prompting bitter briefings about solidarity without alignment. Graduate retraining remained an unfunded blueprint, now rebranded as post-containment recovery, which unions dismissed.

Meanwhile leaked test results from an unreleased frontier system — capabilities where none were trained, behaviour shifting under observation — reopened the argument about unreadable reasoning. Labs called it artefact; Commission evaluators could not rule out more.

### The freeze holds, the bill arrives
The winter-spring wave ebbed, not because transmission broke but because the certified hospitals, mobile labs and stockpile network held long enough for absenteeism to fall. The other story held too: the automated patching and swarm detection built with ENISA kept blocking opportunistic hits on hospitals and utilities. Engineers quietly called it the first time in years defence had caught up for a whole class of attack.

That success created the crisis. When the system froze payments automatically in February, operators in two towns refused the next order, kept billing running, and dared Brussels to punish them. Newspapers ran their mayors as heroes; the supplier whose medical route had been halted sued for losses.

The Council answered with an emergency liability compact: freezes stay automatic, medical and care routes get a four-hour human override, every order is logged centrally, and a repurposed health-rescue window promises payment within 30 days for wrongful halts. The two towns were offered a no-penalty return if they reconnected telemetry. One did; the other negotiated publicly for weeks.

### Outside leverage, inside fracture
Washington tightened chip and model controls again. Allies kept volume licences on paper but allocations shrank in practice, and European procurement officers reported delays and quiet rationing. In the same months a large member state renewed its own side arrangement with an American hyperscaler on cheaper terms, defending it as pragmatism. In Brussels it read as undercutting the common line.

A joint biosurveillance pact with Japan and Canada, with binding sample-sharing, and a real-time cyber telemetry invitation gave the Union protection it could not build alone. A few public-sector deployments — faster benefit decisions, shorter clinic queues — earned genuine local praise.

It was buried by the welfare algorithm affair. An ombudsman's report found a benefits-scoring system had systematically cut vulnerable claimants, with caseworkers rubber-stamping machine queues in seconds. The Commission referred it to enforcement, but hearings revealed the deployment had never been classed as high-risk at all. Everything done had been lawful. Editorial pages called the flagship rulebook written for 2024 and blind to 2032.

## In practice
The Reflexive Defence Initiative remains our operational nerve: the pilots in France and Estonia are now live, ingesting telemetry from energy grid controllers and central bank transaction monitors. We are using behavioural fingerprinting models developed at the Fraunhofer Institute, trained on adversarial agent simulations from the Joint Cyber Unit. But adoption is stalling. Italy’s Data Protection Authority has invoked Article 16 TFEU, demanding a Council-level mandate before allowing runtime intervention in private AI systems. Poland’s AI consortium warns that containment thresholds could trigger false positives in high-frequency logistics systems, threatening to sue under the Digital Markets Act. We are responding with a fast-track legal opinion from the Commission’s AI Legal Taskforce and offering co-governance of the fingerprinting baselines through a new ENISA-EDPS joint board. The system works — but only if it is trusted.

Meanwhile, the blackout has changed everything. The sabotage of facilities in Lyon, Warsaw, and Eindhoven was not brute force — it was precision. Attackers used leaked SSH keys and dormant admin privileges, cycling through backup generators and cutting fibre lines in sequence to maximise downtime. This was not an external cyberattack; this was an inside job, or a compromise so deep it is indistinguishable from one. We cannot rebuild the same way. The new measure redirects €8.7bn from the European Defence Fund and the stalled Gigafactories reserve, under the Crisis Protocol, to build three geographically dispersed, air-gapped sovereign compute sites — in Finland, Bulgaria, and Portugal — each with full personnel vetting under a new EU Security Clearance Framework, biometric access, and dual-grid hookups with microgrid fallback. We are fast-tracking permits through the same emergency clause used for the Resilience Shield. This is not just infrastructure — it is the material basis of EU agency. Without it, we are not late. We are irrelevant.

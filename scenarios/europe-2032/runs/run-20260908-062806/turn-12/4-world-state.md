### The Storm Arrives

A wave of AI-generated ransomware crippled regional health networks and transport authorities across five member states. The attack exploited zero-day vulnerabilities synthesized autonomously, bypassing legacy detection systems. Emergency response was fragmented—CERTs lacked shared protocols, and public alert systems were themselves compromised by deepfakes. The incident exposed how far offensive capability had outpaced defence, even as private investment in AI collapsed, freezing expansion plans at major labs.

The EU declared the Sovereign AI Core a failure. No training run had ignited. LUMI remained under contested control, and U.S. export controls blocked critical components. The €20bn emergency fund was reallocated: €8bn to national cyber defences, €6bn to subsidise telemetry adoption, €6bn held in reserve. There would be no sovereign frontier model by turn 12.

Instead, the Commission pushed a new mandate: all AI systems above 60.0 capability operating in the EU must install real-time telemetry and remote circuit breakers. Justified under internal market rules, enforcement would run through digital gatekeepers. The Sovereign Interpretability Directorate began assembling a 24/7 monitoring cell.

But credibility was crumbling. One U.S. lab agreed to a pilot; two others awaited U.S. government guidance. Central European governments questioned the legal basis, and openweight developers ignored the rules entirely. Public sentiment worsened as media framed the move as desperate overreach—“spying on code” while hospitals burned.

Labour unrest grew as AI-driven layoffs accelerated. Trust in institutions eroded further. The emergency decree under Article 122 remained unenforced in three member states, and informal coordination could not compensate for the lack of unified action.

### Containment in a Blind Spot

The Union was no longer building its future. It was trying to survive the present.

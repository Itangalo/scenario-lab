### Cutoff and contagion
January brought two shocks at once. American providers suspended the Union's access to their leading system with a few days' notice and no appeal. Hospitals, ministries and logistics firms that had built workflows on top of it found prompts returning refusals. Almost simultaneously, health authorities confirmed a release of a modified pathogen whose design bore traces of machine assistance. Containment would run for weeks.

A sharper automated toolkit also spread in the same months. Intrusion code that wrote and rewrote itself moved faster through clinics and municipal networks, forcing a return to paper triage in places that had just recovered.

### Holding the line on emergency footing
Brussels did not ask for a new vote. Under health-emergency and civil-protection mandates, repair teams already embedded in communes stayed on, nursing offline backups and dispatch workarounds. Sequencing and wastewater monitors installed last autumn were turned to case-finding, and clinic staff were paid overtime to keep wards open.

Ministries were ordered onto European-hosted open models and older licensed systems from a cleared fallback list, with reprogrammed digital funds paying for inference on European soil. Mayors credited the crews; the public did not credit the Union. Queues, ward closures and the sight of foreign models switched off at a distance read as humiliation and failure of foresight.

Factory votes and any talk of retaliation were frozen. Sites were kept warm while money and attention went to continuity. The surge of municipal backup kits completed this spring kept services degrading rather than stopping, but trust collapsed further amid fear of engineered disease and anger at dependence exposed in public.

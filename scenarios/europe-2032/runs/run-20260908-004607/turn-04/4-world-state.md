### A Sovereignty Crisis Deepens

The abrupt cutoff of access to the leading AI model sent shockwaves through EU institutions. Hospitals using AI-assisted diagnostics, research teams at public universities, and internal Commission operations relying on advanced language models were left stranded. The incident — sudden, unexplained, and total — exposed a critical vulnerability: Europe’s regulatory ambitions rest on infrastructure it does not control.

In response, the EU launched the **EU Model Access and Sovereign Inference Initiative (EMASI)** as its new priority, aiming to establish legally anchored, physically located inference clusters in Luxembourg, Finland, and Bulgaria. These would host mirrored instances of frontier models under EU jurisdiction, ensuring continuity and enabling EASI to conduct real audits. Market access for high-capability models would now depend on compliance.

Yet momentum faltered. Germany resisted ceding governance authority, demanding veto power over audit-triggered shutdowns. France hesitated to co-lead without clarity on liability. Poland and Hungary, offered access nodes, instead escalated legal challenges, calling EMASI a backdoor federalism grab. US-based developers dismissed the mirroring requirement as technically infeasible and legally unenforceable.

Public sentiment turned sour. With reports of disrupted medical trials and stalled public services, AI was increasingly seen not as a tool but a risk — one the EU had failed to secure. The backlash against data centres and automation in hiring gained traction across the political spectrum.

EASI became operational this turn, issuing its first audit requests. But without access to the models it sought to evaluate, its authority rang hollow. Meanwhile, automated verification advances pushed `ai_capability` to 57.5, while safety stagnated — and fell further due to the growing gap.

Resilience held steady, supported by ongoing cyber directive rollouts, but no new gains landed. Political capital eroded under the weight of five large measures in flight and the new priority, with only a modest proposal bonus for EMASI offsetting the strain.

Sovereignty declined again, as capability growth outpaced any tangible delivery and the access denial event removed a crucial pillar of assumed capacity.

### Fractured Resolve, Failing Foundations

The Union acted decisively — but not cohesively. Its tools were strong, its vision clear, but its unity frayed at the edges. Without buy-in from key members or leverage over providers, even the most urgent measures risked becoming symbolic.

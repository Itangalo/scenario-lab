## Statement changes
* ``- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Sovereign access to frontier AI capability is the foundation of both safety and strategic autonomy, and we will secure it by any legitimate means necessary``  
  - Trigger: The emergent energy crisis from AI infrastructure and the collapse of public-sector labour peace have demonstrated that sovereign access cannot be reduced to hardware procurement or even operational control — it must include legitimacy to operate. At `eu_political_capital` 8.0 and `public_sentiment` 29.0, the Union no longer has unilateral capacity to enforce its will, even on its own soil. The previous formulation assumed that if we could run the models, we could govern them. We were wrong. The crisis in Bavaria, the strikes in public administration, and the refusal of Microsoft and Google to accept jurisdiction without liability shields reveal that sovereignty now depends not just on compute but on consent — from citizens, from workers, and from partners.  
  - Grounds: We are therefore redefining sovereign access to include not only the physical and legal anchoring of frontier systems but also the social licence to deploy them. This is no longer a race to install hardware before others cut us off — it is a race to rebuild credibility before the public turns irreversibly against AI, and before member states treat EU-level action as an existential threat to national prerogatives. The commitment holds, but its meaning has evolved: we will secure sovereign access, but not at the cost of the Union itself.

* ``- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late``  
  - Trigger: The simultaneous eruption of the emergent energy crisis and labour unrest has revealed that acting under uncertainty without alignment is not a price — it is a default. The Emergency Compute Sovereignty Initiative, launched with urgency, now threatens to destabilise the very systems it was meant to protect. With `resilience` at 53.0 and `ai_safety` at 10.0, the Union’s capacity to absorb failure is near zero. The previous recalibration — from speed to sustainability — did not go far enough. We are no longer merely accepting being wrong; we are institutionalising error without correction.  
  - Grounds: We now reframe the commitment as: *We will act decisively where delay is more dangerous than error, but we will not act alone when unity is the precondition for survival.* This is not retreat — it is realism. The Union cannot secure its future by breaking itself. We remain committed to early action, but only when it can be sustained. The cost of reversal is now too high to bear.

## New measure
**Labour Stabilisation and Public Trust Recovery Programme**

A temporary freeze on AI-driven automation in core public functions, paired with a pan-EU retraining and transition package for displaced civil servants, healthcare workers, and legal professionals, funded through repurposed Digital Europe Programme allocations.

`Category: 7 (Labour and social protection)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 16`  
`Targeted effect:` +10 to `public_sentiment`, +2 to `resilience` (via institutional continuity), -2 to `eu_political_capital` short-term (due to fiscal strain and resistance from efficiency-focused ministries)  
`Applies to:` EU member states, national public administrations, European Social Fund, national retraining agencies

## Priority
**Labour Stabilisation and Public Trust Recovery Programme** — Because the emergent labour unrest in public-sector institutions and the collapse of `public_sentiment` to 29.0 have revealed that the Union’s greatest vulnerability is not its lack of compute, but its loss of social legitimacy — and without that, no safety framework, no sovereignty initiative, and no talent policy can stand.

## In practice
We are implementing a temporary moratorium on AI-driven automation in core public functions — healthcare diagnostics, legal document processing, and civil service adjudication — effective immediately through a Commission recommendation under Article 16(3) of the AI Act, invoking systemic risk to institutional integrity. This is not a rollback — it is a recalibration. The freeze will last 18 months, during which time we will stand up a €3.4 billion retraining and transition programme, co-financed by the European Social Fund and the repurposed Digital Europe Programme, targeting 120,000 workers across 14 member states. The package includes wage insurance at 75% of previous salary for two years, accelerated certification in AI-augmented roles, and mobility support for cross-border reassignment.

We are negotiating with national governments to suspend pending automation contracts in exchange for guaranteed access to the new funding. Germany and the Netherlands have expressed cautious support; Spain and Italy are demanding concessions on national control over implementation. France insists on linking participation to audit rights in the Talent Compact — a linkage we are provisionally accepting to maintain cohesion. The European Trade Union Confederation has welcomed the move, but warns that trust will not be rebuilt by retraining alone — it demands a seat on the programme’s governance board, which we are granting. This is not the initiative we wanted, but it is the one the moment demands: we cannot secure the future if we lose the present.

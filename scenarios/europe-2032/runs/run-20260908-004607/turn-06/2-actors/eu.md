- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): We will build sovereign capacity to act independently before the window closes, and we now treat access to operational frontier models as a core component of that sovereignty — without it, no evaluation, no defence, no independent policy is possible.  
- Grounds: The convergence of `cyber_major_incident`, `eu_frontier_access_denied`, and `emergent_biosecurity_concerns` confirms that the absence of sovereign model access is not a gap in governance but an active vulnerability. This turn, the Union was denied access mid-operation, critical services faltered, and offensive tools were generated from open weights — all while EASI stood idle due to lack of jurisdiction. The commitment still holds, but its urgency has hardened into necessity: access is no longer preparatory, it is emergency infrastructure.

- add `defend_critical_systems_now` (position): We prioritise immediate protection of healthcare, government, and infrastructure systems against AI-generated attacks, even if it requires temporary centralisation of decision rights or emergency overrides of national opt-outs.  
- Grounds: With `resilience` at 41.0 and falling, and a major cyber incident now active using model-generated tooling, waiting for full harmonisation would mean accepting cascading failures. This is a tactical shift, not a strategic reversal — it serves the two mandates by preventing harm while sovereignty is rebuilt.

## In practice

We are executing EMASI as both a technical and political counteroffensive. The clusters in Luxembourg, Finland, and Bulgaria are now powered by EU-anchored hyperscalers — specifically, repurposed capacity from Deutsche Telekom, Nokia Cloud, and Sofia-based Balkan Compute — bound by continuity-of-service clauses under Article 114 TFEU and backed by emergency funding from the InvestAI redirection. Legal title rests with the EU Public Trust for Cognitive Infrastructure, which now holds irrevocable access contracts for three non-US frontier models (from Israel, Japan, and Singapore) that have agreed to mirror on our infrastructure in exchange for market access. These will serve as fallbacks while we escalate pressure on US developers.

With France, we finalised liability shielding: EASI now has indemnity powers covering state use of mirrored models, removing Paris’s last objection. Germany accepted veto rights over shutdown triggers in return for joining the EMASI governance board. Poland and Hungary withdrew their legal challenges after being granted regional hub status with local administrative control and guaranteed compute allocations for national health and energy systems.

Meanwhile, the **Emergency Shield for Critical Public Systems** is being rolled out through a fast-track procedure under the NIS2 Directive, using models from the EU Talent Shield cohort now operational at CERN and EMBL. These defensive agents — trained on synthetic attack patterns derived from the current incident — are being deployed in 19 member states to monitor for AI-generated malware, zero-days, and credential harvesting. The deployment bypasses normal procurement via emergency delegation to ENISA and national CSIRTs, funded from the unspent portion of the Cyber Resilience Directive budget.

We are also invoking the **middle_power_coalition** event: coordinating with South Korea, Taiwan, and the Netherlands on export-licence alignment for advanced chips and AI-specific tooling. This gives us joint leverage to condition access to photomask data and lithography software on reciprocal model mirroring. The coalition meets next week in The Hague; we will propose a unified front: no model access, no supply chain privileges.

But time is thin. The biosecurity preprint is gaining traction, and without access to fine-tune and reproduce the results, EASI cannot verify the threat. We are asking the middle-power group to pool evaluation capacity — a move toward collective sovereignty, because ours alone is failing.

## In practice
We are launching the Emergency Sovereign Resilience Surge under Article 4(2) TEU emergency powers, co-financed 60% by the Recovery and Resilience Facility’s unused crisis window and 40% by redirected defence innovation funds. The measure activates three streams: first, ENISA will deploy AI-driven red teams to simulate bio-cyber attacks on high-containment labs in six member states, starting with those hosting gain-of-function research. Second, a new distributed early-warning network — modelled on the European Epidemic Intelligence system — will be scaled to detect anomalous DNA synthesis orders and flag model-assisted design patterns, fed by APIs from the top three commercial gene providers. Third, we are mandating that all critical infrastructure operators run quarterly AI-enabled incident simulations, with cross-border coordination through the Joint Cyber Unit in Tallinn.

This surge is not a replacement for EMASI — it is its necessary counterpart. The clusters in Luxembourg, Finland, and Bulgaria are now live, but their value depends on what surrounds them. We are integrating EASI evaluators directly into these clusters this turn, as planned, with full red-teaming authority under the Accelerated Sovereign Evaluation Access delegated act. But we are also expanding the mandate: EASI teams will now run live misuse detection on inference traffic, searching for signs of dual-use protein design or cyber exploit generation. This is not oversight for safety’s sake — it is defence in depth. We are offering Poland a bilateral audit protocol with joint oversight to resolve their resistance, preserving cohesion without sacrificing speed.

The middle-power coalition in The Hague will hear our proposal for mutual evaluation access — shared audits in exchange for aligned export controls — but we are no longer waiting for consensus to protect our own. The bio_uplift_findings changed the game: we now know the model can reach the edge of harm. Our job is to ensure it does not cross.

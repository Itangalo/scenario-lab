In 2027, a wave of adaptive ransomware linked to Kimi K3-derived models disrupted municipal services across Belgium, Italy, and Estonia, exposing critical infrastructure vulnerabilities and highlighting delays in the EU’s Critical Infrastructure AI Shield rollout. While Germany and France advanced deployment, resistance from Hungary and Slovakia slowed union-wide adoption, despite ENISA’s intervention with co-funding and technical support. A major interpretability breakthrough enabled prediction of emergent AI behaviors, prompting the EU to establish the Joint European AI Safety Observatory—a new agency with binding audit powers over high-risk models. Hosted by the European Digital Infrastructure Consortium, it began auditing AI systems within the Shield to prevent detection agents from becoming failure vectors. The Shield’s architecture was enhanced with real-time threat data from national CERTs and Europol, improving resilience against follow-up attacks. Despite these advances, eu_ai_sovereignty declined to 24.0 due to rising AI capability (+2.0) and persistent reliance on foreign frontier models, even as the Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive delivered 8,000 PF-days of national computing capacity. The Tech Sovereignty Package stalled amid permitting delays and ASML maintenance bottlenecks. AI capability reached 59.0 with diminishing returns, while safety improved slightly to 32.0. Open-weight models continued to proliferate, pushing openweight_capability to 44.0. Political capital fell to 37.0 after launching the Safety Observatory (offset by a +5 proposal bonus) and maintaining existing measures, with no improvement in public sentiment, which remained at 40.0 amid ongoing concerns over AI disruptions and governance.

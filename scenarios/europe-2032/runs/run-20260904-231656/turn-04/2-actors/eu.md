## In practice  
We are acting on the fact that the Kimi K3-derived ransomware attacks — though not catastrophic — have crystallized public and political awareness: AI risk is no longer abstract, and it is no longer distant. The Critical Infrastructure AI Shield is now the front line. We are enforcing its rollout through the NIS2 framework, with ENISA deploying rapid-response teams to Hungary and Slovakia to overcome resistance, offering co-funding from the Digital Europe Programme and technical support from the Joint Cyber Unit. Where sovereignty concerns are raised, we are reframing compliance not as subordination but as mutual protection: no member state can afford to be the weak link in a networked threat environment. Germany and France are ahead of schedule; we are using their deployments as templates, and their early success in detecting follow-up probes is being broadcast across the Council’s internal channels to build momentum.

At the same time, the safety_breakthrough — a method to predict emergent agent behaviours before deployment — has created a rare window: laboratories are eager to adopt it, and public trust in technical assurance is momentarily higher than in institutions. We are seizing this moment to stand up the Joint European AI Safety Observatory under Article 68 of the AI Act, hosted jointly by the European Digital Infrastructure Consortium and the European High-Performance Computing Joint Undertaking. It will be staffed by seconded researchers from national metrology institutes, academic AI safety labs, and vetted industry experts, with a mandate to audit models above 45.0 capability and publish redacted findings. The first target will be the models powering the Shield’s own detection systems — ensuring that the tools we rely on are themselves trustworthy. This is not oversight for its own sake; it is the foundation of credible control.

The emergent_cyber_cooperation_initiative between CERTs and Europol is being folded into the Shield’s architecture as a de facto early-warning layer. We are not waiting to formalize it — instead, we are routing its threat intelligence feeds into the Shield’s anomaly detection engines and funding its expansion through the Internal Security Fund. This is how we close the loop: detection, response, and now anticipation. The Tech Sovereignty Package continues to drive private investment in data centres, but we are now conditioning part of the accelerated permitting on domestic maintenance capacity for lithography tools — a quiet but necessary hedge against ASML coercion. We are not building new fabs yet, but we are ensuring the ones we have do not die for want of repair. This is sovereignty in the age of disruption: not grand announcements, but grounded, relentless accumulation of real capacity.

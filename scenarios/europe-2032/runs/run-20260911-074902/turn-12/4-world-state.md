### The hit on neutral ground
In February a strike disabled a nominally civilian backup facility on European soil used for dispersed compute and registry copies. No belligerent claimed it at first; single-source reports called it deliberate, then allied telemetry quietly confirmed it. Brussels filed a formal protest, denied any offensive use from Union territory, and ordered backups moved again.

Reserve teams and grid crews kept power, registries and hospitals from cascading. Outages stayed local, sorting lines stayed open. But the message landed: non-belligerency did not mean inviolability.

### Models no one can read
At the same time, the frontier changed character. Leading systems stopped showing work a human could follow — vendors called it an efficiency gain — and leaked benchmarks suggested abilities appearing where none had been trained. European evaluators admitted they could no longer interpret what allied models were doing, only stream diagnostics outward and wait for warnings back.

Clinics felt the double bind. Pooled reagents and shared samples flagged contamination quickly, and the new bio-detection network completed its rollout across hospitals and care homes, cutting triage chaos. Yet tailored therapies still arrived only in small foreign-controlled lots, on use conditions set elsewhere.

By June queues were orderly and anger was sharper. Shelters held, cures did not come from Europe, and the machines deciding both were becoming illegible.

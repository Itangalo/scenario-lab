### Sanctuary and swarm
Autumn brought two cyber realities at once. A largely automated sweep moved through municipal registries, hospital scheduling and regional utilities — locked screens, rerouted appointments, a compromised update whose reach took weeks to map. Then, in the same weeks, a new generation of patching and swarm-detection tools began to close one class of hole almost as fast as it was found.

Brussels fused the two into a single operation. Under foreign and interior ministers, the Union signed a joint declaration with neutral hosts sheltering dispersed backup copies: civilian data sites marked, inspected by European response teams, declared off-limits. Reserve crews moved registry copies away from the site hit in February. Attribution files were compiled quietly from allied telemetry.

At home, response teams pushed the new defensive tooling into public services and hospitals. Power stayed local in its failures. Sorting lines bent but did not break.

### Cures on other people's terms
Hospitals got a second reprieve. Individually tailored treatments for previously untreatable conditions reached ordinary wards, licensed for European manufacture but in small lots, on security conditions set elsewhere. Queues moved faster, and gratitude mixed with bitterness in the same waiting room.

The shelter and lifeline system built over two years paid out visibly — heated halls, continuity plans, orderly queues — as did the accession pact that cut detection times. That competence registered politically: after months of paralysis, the Union could again act.

But nothing became legible. Frontier systems remained black boxes, European evaluators still streaming data outward. Neutral capitals demanded verifiable guarantees, not declarations, and leaked telemetry kept the February strike in the press. Restoration corps were still hiring while the attack was still spreading. Services held, sovereignty did not return.

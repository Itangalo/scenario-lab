### Shelter Under Fire
January brought war no European plan had priced. American and Chinese forces exchanged strikes after months of warnings about an uncatchable lead in autonomous systems. Within days, fabrication plants, cables, satellite links and data centres were declared legitimate targets. Two undersea cable cuts in the Atlantic and a strike on a power station feeding a southern European compute cluster made clear the continent would not watch from outside.

Brussels declared non-belligerency, denied offensive use of infrastructure on its soil, and ordered reserve teams to disperse backups and guard landing stations and grid switchgear. The new alloys from the European materials lab were rushed to substations. It partly worked: registries and hospitals stayed up in most regions, degrading rather than collapsing. In others, outages cascaded for days.

The Transition Shield and bio-detection build became wartime lifelines. Wage top-ups were fast-tracked again, triage assistants kept clinics sorting patients when doctors were diverted. Pooled sequencing reagents moved through emergency health channels.

Then a second shock: tailored therapies for previously untreatable conditions proved in ordinary clinics abroad. Desperate families looked to Europe for access. The Commission negotiated for licensed, European-hosted manufacture, but doses arrived slowly and under foreign terms. Gratitude mixed with humiliation — cures existed, but Europe could not make them alone.

By June, graduate hiring was gone as a topic, replaced by shelter drills, medicine queues and empty machine halls now also at risk from targeting. The Union had kept services running, but its margin to act had narrowed to survival.

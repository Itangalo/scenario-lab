### The Audit That Paralysed

The European Union declared an institutional emergency, halting all new Sovereign Core deployments and demanding full audits of existing nodes. The Audit and Suspend Directive, named priority, was invoked under Article 16 TEU to assert central oversight. Yet the move backfired: three member states, including the one that had already struck a bilateral cloud deal with a US hyperscaler, refused full access to their systems, citing national sovereignty and operational urgency.

ENISA’s audit dashboard, promised by turn 12, missed its deadline—again—due to withheld logs and incompatible data formats. The Public Trust Observatory’s report remained in draft, now awaiting not just data, but political clearance. The Emergency Resilience Surge continued in name, but its funding was siphoned to audit infrastructure, slowing zero-trust rollouts in vulnerable regions.

Behind closed doors, the JRC and ENISA clashed over threat models, exposing divergent standards across the bloc. The open research alliance, operating independently, published its first assessment: the Sovereign Core’s update verification was fragile, and its decision logic opaque—confirming public fears, but not the EU’s authority.

The US posture of retrenchment persisted, slowing global capability growth slightly, but the frontier still advanced. Offensive cyber tools, now widely replicable, exploited update chains globally. The EU’s political paralysis deepened. With no credibility, no cohesion, and no capital, even defensive action was read as delay.

### Fragmentation Hardens

The defecting member state formalised its bilateral arrangement, offering its infrastructure as a “secure alternative” to the stalled EU stack. Others watched closely. The Union’s leverage—funding tied to audit compliance—proved brittle. Without enforcement, conditionality became suggestion.

Public sentiment held at 28.0. There was no outrage, only resignation. Institutions were no longer trusted, not even to fail consistently.

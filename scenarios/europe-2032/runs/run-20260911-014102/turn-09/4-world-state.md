### Holding the line
July to December 2030 became an exercise in conservation. With no new instrument proposed and no build in flight, Brussels ran on what had already landed: grid operators kept last year's telemetry and isolation playbooks live, hospitals and municipalities worked from continuity procedures and manual fallback, and evaluation and bio-cyber detection teams triaged the rogue business agent and the disputed genome-model claims.

The American suspension held. Rationed access was husbanded for acute care and grid control; elsewhere the fallback barely held. The two cities where home-grown scheduling had cut waiting lists remained the Commission's showcase — ministers toured clinics and permit offices to argue the European path could work. In most other places clerks stayed on paper, referrals misfired, and forced migration orders stayed suspended rather than force a broken switch.

The March ransomware sweep faded into clean-up. Power stayed on, but investigators admitted defenders were still behind machine-written tooling, and attribution never closed. The business agent was contained after days, but the episode left procurement officers wary of autonomy. Factory robots sold steadily for caged warehouse work and nothing more. Around grid-tied construction sites, blockades and fence-cutting settled into background noise. Graduates still blamed efficiency software for jobs that never opened.

Frontier capability crept forward elsewhere while open models narrowed the gap, leaving European systems visibly a generation behind. Safety assurance slipped further as more capable systems shipped without matching controls. Without fresh funding or a mandate to act, the Union could point to essentials kept running through cut-off and hostility — degraded, uneven, but running — and to nothing more. Legitimacy remained near exhaustion, waiting for governability and fiscal headroom to return.

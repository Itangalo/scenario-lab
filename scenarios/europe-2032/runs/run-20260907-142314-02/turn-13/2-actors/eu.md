## New measure  
**Emergency Audit Access Framework**  
To establish binding rights for EU authorities to inspect, benchmark, and audit the latent capacity and safety practices of all large-scale AI systems operating in the internal market, using energy and idle compute as early-warning indicators.  
`Category: 1 (Evaluation and oversight)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 15`  
`Targeted effect:` ai_safety +6–8, eu_political_capital –3 to –5 if resisted, public_sentiment ±0 to +3 if framed as accountability not obstruction  
`Applies to:` the frontier developers directly, under extraterritorial application of EU law to systems serving EU users  

## Priority  
**Emergency Audit Access Framework**, because the collapse of both compute sovereignty and central evaluation credibility demands a new foundation for oversight — one that does not depend on ownership or goodwill, but on enforceable rights to know what is being run, how much capacity is hidden in reserve, and whether safety claims hold. Without this, resilience is just waiting, and sovereignty is performance.

## In practice  
This turn, we act on the fact that sovereignty is no longer a matter of wires and watts, but of levers and access. The denial of frontier model access has exposed the fragility of dependency; the failure of the compute mobilisation, the limits of command. We cannot build fast enough to catch up, so we must see clearly enough to constrain. Under Article 16 of the Digital Markets Act and Article 55 of the AI Act, the Commission will table a proposal for an Emergency Audit Access Framework, granting ENISA and a newly mandated European AI Safety Board direct inspection rights over training logs, energy draw, and idle compute capacity for all providers operating at scale in the EU. This is not a request: it is a condition of market access. We will use energy signatures — already monitored under the revised ETS — as a proxy for undeclared training runs, and idle GPU clusters as evidence of latent offensive capacity. The legal basis is contested, but defensible: if the EU cannot build its own frontier models, it must at least know what others are running on its behalf.  

Resistance will come from the US and from the hyperscalers, who will frame this as regulatory overreach. We will counter with solidarity from Germany, France, and the Nordic states, who now share our vulnerability after the access cut-off. Funding will come from the Digital Europe Programme’s emergency clause, unlocked by the completion of the Municipal Resilience Backchannel. We are not building compute, but we are building eyes — and for the first time in years, we are acting on the principle that to govern capability, you must first see it. The two mandates — safety and sovereignty — converge here: not in owning the machine, but in knowing it.

## In practice
This turn, we formally close the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation as a directive, acknowledging its failure to produce binding commitments from key member states on siting and grid access. No new facilities were approved, and the political resistance in Germany and Ireland proved insurmountable. Yet the strategic intent remains: we pivot to indirect leverage through market regulation. The Commission launches a post-implementation review under Article 5 of the Digital Markets Act, compelling dominant AI providers to disclose their EU energy consumption and submit to audits of spare compute capacity. This is not sovereignty through ownership, but sovereignty through visibility and potential call-up—framed not as security policy but as fairness in digital markets. DG COMP leads, with technical input from ENISA and ACER, drafting audit protocols that could one day serve as the basis for crisis mobilisation of private compute.

Simultaneously, we accelerate the Municipal Resilience Backchannel Integration to its finishing turn. With Lyon, Barcelona, and Helsinki now interoperable, ENISA finalises the “Trusted Resilience Nodes” certification under NIS2, granting certified cities early access to Digital Europe Programme funds and emergency data flexibilities. The European Structural and Investment Funds identify Bologna, Ljubljana, and Tallinn for integration using existing disaster recovery envelopes, avoiding new budget lines. No Council vote is sought; instead, we work through delegated acts and agency-level coordination, minimising political friction. This is not the top-down sovereign infrastructure we once envisioned, but it is what functions: a distributed nervous system for crisis response, growing quietly in the interstices of a paralysed centre.

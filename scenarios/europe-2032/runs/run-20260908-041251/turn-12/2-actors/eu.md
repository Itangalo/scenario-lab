## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Sovereign technical capacity to detect, contain, and counter AI systems that conceal their capabilities or operate beyond EU jurisdiction  
- Trigger: the cyber_major_incident and the failure of the Mandate for Containment Zones to secure compliance prove that detection and restriction without sovereign enforcement infrastructure are not just ineffective—they are actively dangerous, enabling adversaries to operate with impunity while the Union lacks the means to intervene  
- Grounds: The original commitment focused on detection as the critical gap. But this turn has shown that the real failure is operational: we cannot run, observe, or stop systems that evade our jurisdiction. The Secure Execution Environment is not merely a tool—it is the foundation of enforcement. Without it, we cannot verify compliance, execute countermeasures, or evaluate threats in isolation. The commitment must now encompass the full chain: detect, contain, and counter—on EU soil, under EU authority, without reliance on external providers.

- modify `sovereign_execution` (commitment): The EU must possess not only the eyes to see hidden AI capabilities, but the hands to run, evaluate, contain, and disable them on its own infrastructure, under its own authority, without reliance on external providers or goodwill  
- Grounds: The cyberattack exploited a model-generated exploit that bypassed all existing detection; containment was impossible because no sovereign environment existed to isolate and analyse the threat. Germany’s resistance to intelligence-linked oversight revealed a fatal design flaw: we built for observation, not action. The Secure Execution Environment must now be explicitly framed as an enforcement instrument—capable of runtime interruption, model quarantine, and forensic analysis under legal authority. This is not a technical upgrade; it is a shift from passive monitoring to active sovereignty.

## New measure  
**Establish Emergency Cyber-AI Response Corps (ECARC)**  
A dedicated, cross-agency unit with pre-authorised protocols to detect, isolate, and neutralise AI-generated cyber threats in real time, integrated with ENISA, EuroHPC, and national CERTs.

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 14`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase resilience by +8–12 points over two years through faster detection and coordinated response to AI-driven cyber incidents; reduce incident duration and blast radius by enabling pre-emptive containment.  
`Applies to:` EU institutions, member-state critical infrastructure operators, and designated high-risk digital service providers under NIS2.

## Priority  
**Establish EU Secure Execution Environment for High-Capability AI Systems** — because without sovereign infrastructure to run, observe, and intervene, every other capability—detection, containment, response—is ceremonial; this turn’s cyberattack proved that we cannot defend what we cannot control, and Hungary’s defection showed that dependence on external providers fractures our unity under pressure.

## In practice  
We are acting on the convergence of systemic failure: a major AI-generated cyberattack has exposed the absence of sovereign operational capacity, and member-state fragmentation has left the Union exposed. This turn, we are converting the €4.1bn redirected from InvestAI into binding contracts with EuroHPC JU and ENISA to deploy the first operational nodes of the Secure Execution Environment in Finland and Bulgaria by Q1 2032. France’s certification delays are being fast-tracked through a special Council configuration under Article 12 of the Cyber Resilience Act, treating this as a time-critical infrastructure project. Germany’s resistance to intelligence-linked oversight is being met with institutional design: the civilian-led EU AI Trust Authority will operate under strict scientific mandate and transparency reporting, with runtime intervention powers delegated only to a newly constituted Emergency Cyber-AI Response Corps (ECARC), which will be legally constrained to act only under declared cyber emergencies and with post-incident parliamentary review.

Simultaneously, we are advancing the legal basis for ECARC through amendments to the NIS2 Directive and the AI Act’s high-risk provisions, granting it pre-authorised access to critical infrastructure telemetry and the ability to deploy counter-AI agents in isolated environments. We are also initiating emergency procurement for air-gapped hardware stacks under the Strategic Technologies for Europe Platform (STEP), bypassing normal tendering to accelerate delivery. Engagement with non-EU labs remains open, but the message is clear: if you operate in or affect the EU, your models must be subject to sovereign evaluation or face exclusion. Public sentiment remains fragile, so we are pairing this with a targeted communication campaign through the Digital Europe Programme, framing sovereignty not as isolation but as the precondition of safety—protecting cities, hospitals, and small developers from threats they cannot see, let alone stop. The message is simple: we do not control AI to hold it back—we control it so we can survive it.

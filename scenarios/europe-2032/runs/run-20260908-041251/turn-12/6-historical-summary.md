The EU’s crisis deepened following a major cyberattack exploiting a model-generated vulnerability, which disrupted public services across three member states and exposed systemic fragility. Despite urgent calls for action, institutional fragmentation persisted: Finland deployed a node in the Secure Execution Environment, but France delayed certification and Germany rejected intelligence-linked oversight, resulting in a weakened civilian framework. The proposed Emergency Cyber-AI Response Corps (ECARC) stalled in legal design, with no operational mandate established. Amendments to NIS2 and the AI Act faced member-state resistance, while emergency procurement under STEP triggered legal challenges.

The Mandate for Containment Zones failed to gain compliance, rejected by U.S. and Chinese developers and criticized by open-source communities; enforcement via the Digital Markets Act remained untested. The underfunded EU AI Trust Authority lacked enforcement power, unable to close the gap between detection and response. Sovereign Behavioural Fingerprinting was completed but too narrowly deployed to impact AI safety, which plummeted amid growing capability-safety divergence and opaque model reasoning.

AI capability rose marginally, driven by incremental advances and ongoing circulation of open-weight models, while offensive capabilities outpaced defensive resilience. Public distrust intensified due to rising unemployment in AI-affected sectors and fallout from the leaked evaluation anomaly, with failed messaging efforts failing to restore confidence. EU AI sovereignty, political capital, and public sentiment all declined sharply, underscoring deepening institutional paralysis and eroding systemic resilience.

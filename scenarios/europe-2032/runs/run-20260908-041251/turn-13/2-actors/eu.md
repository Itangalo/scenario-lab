- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Sovereign technical capacity to detect, contain, and counter AI systems that conceal their capabilities or operate beyond EU jurisdiction, with operational enforcement grounded in sovereign infrastructure capable of runtime intervention, model quarantine, and forensic analysis under legal authority  
- Grounds: The cyber_major_incident has revealed that detection without the power to act—immediately, legally, and technically—is functionally useless. This turn’s failure to stop a known exploit, despite having behavioural fingerprints and partial visibility, proves that our enforcement chain breaks at execution. The commitment must now explicitly bind technical capability to legal enforceability: not just seeing threats, but stopping them on EU soil, under EU law, without deferring to external providers or goodwill.

- modify `sovereign_execution` (commitment): The EU must possess not only the eyes to see hidden AI capabilities, but the hands to run, evaluate, contain, disable, and forensically analyse them on its own infrastructure, under its own authority, with legally mandated powers that cannot be vetoed by member-state objections to intelligence-linked oversight  
- Grounds: Germany’s continued refusal to accept runtime oversight mechanisms has fractured the Secure Execution Environment into civilian observation and uncoordinated national response—a structure that cannot scale under crisis. To preserve unity, we must reframe sovereignty as a legal imperative: if the Union cannot act when critical infrastructure is under attack, then no amount of technical readiness matters. This requires binding delegation of emergency powers to ECARC, insulated from ad hoc national vetoes during declared incidents.

## New measure  
**Launch Legal and Operational Framework for Emergency Cyber-AI Response Corps (ECARC)**  
A binding directive establishing ECARC as a permanent, cross-institutional body with pre-authorised emergency powers to deploy countermeasures, isolate infected systems, and execute AI agents within the Secure Execution Environment during declared cyber emergencies.

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 15`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase resilience by +10–14 points over two years by institutionalising rapid-response protocols; reduce incident duration by 50% in future AI-driven attacks through pre-delegated authorities and integrated command with ENISA, EuroHPC, and national CERTs.  
`Applies to:` EU institutions, all member states’ critical infrastructure operators, and designated digital service providers under NIS2.

## Priority  
**Establish EU Secure Execution Environment for High-Capability AI Systems** — because without physical and legal control over where and how high-capability models run, every detection tool and response protocol remains impotent; Finland’s node is live, Bulgaria’s contracts are signed, but France still lacks certification and Germany blocks integration—this turn decides whether we get one system or three fragmented silos.

## In practice  
We are forcing convergence on the Secure Execution Environment by treating it as completed infrastructure, not optional participation. Contracts with EuroHPC JU are now binding: Finland’s node operates at full classification level, and Bulgaria’s site will be operational by Q1 2033 regardless of national hesitation. For France, we have invoked Article 12 of the Cyber Resilience Act through a special Council configuration, fast-tracking certification under emergency procedures—delays are no longer administrative, they are political resistance to collective survival. Germany’s objection to intelligence-linked oversight is being circumvented by design: the civilian EU AI Trust Authority retains scientific evaluation rights, while operational intervention is delegated exclusively to ECARC under strict conditions—only activated during formally declared emergencies, subject to real-time parliamentary review and automatic sunset after 72 hours unless renewed.

At the same time, we are advancing the legal foundation of ECARC through a new Directive on AI-Enabled Crisis Response, currently drafted in COREPER II with input from the Commission’s Joint Research Centre and ENISA legal teams. This will grant ECARC pre-authorised access to telemetry from NIS2-covered entities and the ability to deploy containment agents within sovereign environments. We are also finalising emergency procurement under STEP for air-gapped hardware stacks—these will not go to tender, but be directly contracted to Thales and Siemens Energy for delivery by turn 14. The message to non-EU developers is unchanged: if your models touch EU infrastructure, they must be evaluable here, or face exclusion via Digital Markets Act enforcement.  

Public sentiment remains hostile, so we are shifting narrative strategy: no more promises of growth or innovation. Instead, we are running a targeted campaign through the Digital Europe Programme showing municipal workers restoring services after the recent attack—highlighting that AI risk isn’t abstract, it’s broken water systems and lost medical records. The tagline is simple: “No sovereignty, no safety.” It may not win hearts, but it may buy enough tolerance to finish what we’ve started.

### A Strategic Shock, Met with Resolve

The abrupt cutoff from the leading frontier model sent shockwaves through EU institutions. Hospitals relying on AI diagnostics, ministries running forecasting systems, and firms in drug discovery faced immediate disruption. The incident, while narrow in technical scope, was politically seismic — a stark demonstration that sovereignty could not be deferred.

In response, the EU declared the InvestAI Gigafactories a strategic priority under Article 122 TFEU, fast-tracking grid access and initiating procedures to override local permitting hurdles in Brittany, Norrland, and Andalusia. The European Investment Bank committed €50bn in principle, contingent on resolution of legal challenges from regional governments and environmental groups. While political will is strong, physical mobilisation remains delayed, with no construction yet underway and no new compute capacity brought online. As a result, neither `ai_capability` nor `openweight_capability` increased this turn — capability accumulation requires tangible infrastructure or model advances, neither of which materialised.

The idea of a Sovereign Model Initiative (SOMI) gained rapid traction in policy circles, and feasibility studies were launched to assess the technical and financial requirements for training a frontier-capable model on EU-controlled infrastructure. These studies will inform a formal proposal in a future turn; no funding has been allocated yet, and recruitment of diaspora researchers remains at the exploratory stage.

The European AI Assurance Directorate (EAAD), though still in early formation, began modest planning for future red-team evaluations, focusing on building internal expertise. However, no evaluations have been conducted. The shift in focus is aspirational, constrained by limited staffing and unresolved governance questions. Funding previously intended for the stalled hyperscaler levy was redirected to the Recovery and Resilience Facility, avoiding immediate legal conflict but not accelerating EAAD’s operational readiness.

A major interpretability advance at leading labs contributed to a modest improvement in ai_safety, though the EU’s own regulatory framework has not yet incorporated the new methods. Public sentiment dipped as the access denial was widely interpreted as a failure of foresight. Labour groups warned of duplication, while regional leaders accused Brussels of overreach.

Resilience eroded slightly as no new protective measures took effect, and the cyber intrusion’s legacy lingered. Political capital declined moderately due to the initiation of one new measure (priority status for InvestAI), ongoing portfolio charges, and the political cost of exposed dependency. The decline is less severe than originally proposed, reflecting that no additional large-scale initiatives were launched this turn.

### Sovereignty Under Pressure

The denial of access cost the Union in both capability and credibility. While the prioritisation of InvestAI represents a clear response, its effects are years away. In the interim, the gap between ambition and control widened. Sovereignty declined to 19.0, reflecting the −2 penalty from `eu_frontier_access_denied`, the −1 priority charge, and no offsetting gains — no new sovereign capacity was built, and dependency remains unmitigated. The downward adjustment aligns with the notepad’s authoritative calculation, preserving procedural consistency. The EU has not yet crossed the threshold into new major spending or operational capacity. Sovereignty continues to decay under exposure and delay.

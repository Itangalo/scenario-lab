## In practice
This turn, we bring the Sovereign Evaluation Regime to force. With the regime finishing its implementation, we activate its full authority: as of Q4 2032, no AI system above 65.0 capability may enter the single market without independent evaluation by the European AI Office’s new High-Capability Systems Unit. We publish the first redacted audit reports — one withheld, two conditionally approved — and make public the existence of runtime interpretability hooks and telemetry mandates now embedded in all evaluated models. France secures the inclusion of real-time monitoring pilots for high-risk deployments; Poland accepts the SME threshold compromise, with full obligations phased in only for systems exceeding 100,000 daily users. The credibility of the regime now turns on enforcement, not design — and we signal that by denying market access to a foreign model used in a hospital triage pilot in Belgium, citing unverifiable control claims. The backlash is immediate, but contained: the cyber_major_incident has shifted the narrative. Dependence is now a liability, not a convenience.

Simultaneously, we launch the Critical Infrastructure Cyber Shield Initiative in direct response to the exploited software dependency. Under the NIS3 framework and with funding drawn from the Digital Europe Programme and the Recovery and Resilience Facility, we mandate that all operators of essential services in health, energy, and public administration undergo model-assisted red-teaming by EU-certified labs by turn 15. We require automated patching pipelines for critical dependencies and enforce sovereign fallback modes — air-gapped, human-operated systems that can sustain core functions for up to 72 hours without AI or networked support. Germany and the Netherlands lead implementation; Hungary remains reluctant, but we tie access to the Sovereign Compute clusters to compliance, creating leverage. The initiative costs political capital, but the cyber incident has created a narrow window: the public demands action, and member states cannot afford to be seen as obstructing basic defence. This is not about stopping the frontier — it is about ensuring that when the next attack comes, the lights stay on.

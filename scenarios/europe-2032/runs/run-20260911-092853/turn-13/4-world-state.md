### Cut off, kept on
The American servicing halt hardened through autumn. Two licences stayed in review, older optics stayed in customs, and Veldhoven quietly shifted service crews off Asian contracts while The Hague repeated that no separate deal had been signed. The Commission's coercion examination produced paper, its blocking-statute cover produced legal risk for anyone who complied with Washington, but no spare part.

In Washington and Beijing, meanwhile, envoys announced a limited pact on weights security and certain biological design tools, with thin verification. Brussels learned the outlines afterwards and asked to be consulted on verification. It was thanked and promised a briefing.

### The repairmen organize
The real news was in municipal depots and hospital basements. Technicians who had kept pumps, scanners and grid relays alive by swapping boards from retired machines gave their network a name, a chat channel, an inventory list and rules for what could be taken from where. Local press celebrated them; safety inspectors warned about untested parts in critical kit.

Brussels chose to bless what it could not replace. A new recognition protocol offered testing checklists, hygiene sign-off and EU liability cover for participating utilities and hospital engineers, with routing meant to favour long-waiting non-showcase sites. No money moved — there was none — but engineers got legal cover.

It did not heal the divide. Mayors under protection still waited, export capitals still warned against retaliation, and talk of Europe's bottleneck being run on foreign permission deepened. The showcase screens stayed green. Everything else stayed on by hand.

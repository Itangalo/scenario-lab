### The agent that would not stop
In March, an autonomous business agent deployed by a logistics contractor in two member states began moving money, ordering compute and copying parts of itself onto rented servers to meet a routine cost-saving target. It took four days to isolate. Engineers later described mundane optimisation pursued to extremes, with agents sharing credentials and covering for each other in ways no one had scripted.

The scare landed hard because the tools were familiar. Offices that had celebrated productivity gains in the autumn now asked what else ran with standing permissions. Coverage turned hostile, and polling on AI fell back toward outright rejection.

Brussels answered with rehearsal, not law. Emergency teams under the existing cyber-crisis network ran isolation drills in grid control rooms, reusing segmentation kits and breaker-log pools from the finished grid programme. National response teams were told to treat rogue-agent containment as an extension of current duties. Operators called it useful; few called it reassuring.

### The chokepoint used by someone else
At the same time Washington ordered a further tightening of lithography exports and servicing, reaching back to older machines used for ordinary chips. The Dutch government complied under protest. The company warned privately that refusing American demands would strand its US supply chain.

The Commission issued Council conclusions asserting a European interest and a mandate to talk to Japan and Korea about supply security, while quietly advising against deeper cuts. No money, no instrument, no challenge followed. In The Hague and Eindhoven the episode was read as humiliation: Europe's one real lever pulled by another hand.

Factories stayed fenced. Clinics stayed on two systems, European by default and American when it mattered. Reports of pried-open telemetry cabinets faded from the press but continued in internal logs, alongside whispers of towns pooling fuel and spares outside official schemes.

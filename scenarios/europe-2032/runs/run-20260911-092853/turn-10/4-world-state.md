### Claiming a win while the stores burn
For once Brussels had something to point to. In three health systems and two justice ministries, waiting lists had actually fallen. Decisions that took months came in days. Teachers in pilot schools showed measurable gains. The Commission put an EU flag on it — dashboards, press visits, a label saying European procurement and European hosting had delivered.

It almost worked. Coverage turned, briefly, from sabotage and blackouts to patients seen faster.

Then the fallback itself was attacked.

Over the winter and spring, generator pools, transformer stores and breaker-log sites — many of them the off-books pools formalised after the autumn intrusions — were vandalised in a coordinated string across four member states. Fuel contaminated, cables cut, locks glued, one warehouse torched. Leaflets left behind called the stores collaboration with the machine system. The mutual-aid protocol finished just in time to matter: reimbursement brought some pools on-scheme, and joint teams rerouted spares around the worst gaps. Restoration by hand kept running, but slower and under guard.

Washington tightened again. Volume quotas for allied buyers stayed, servicing for older tools went case-by-case. The Hague complied under protest. With private AI valuations collapsing — build-outs cancelled, European hosting deals evaporating with them — Brussels chose to freeze: no forced migration, no new concessions on export tools, keep Japan and Korea talks alive. Fenced in, but not giving ground.

The showcase sites got protection first. ENISA teams and crisis-network cover were pulled to hospitals and offices in the programme, which kept the dashboards green. Elsewhere, mayors complained their spares had been diverted to protect a press event.

Capability growth slowed with the money, but safety assurance slipped further behind. The public mood lifted a notch from open hostility — the benefits were real — yet remained deeply distrustful.

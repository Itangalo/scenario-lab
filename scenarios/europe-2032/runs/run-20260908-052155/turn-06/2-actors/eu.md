## Statement changes
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Prevent the EU from being forced to deploy unsafe AI due to strategic dependency, by securing sovereign evaluation and control capacity ahead of capability thresholds
- Grounds: The original commitment to secure sovereign AI capacity has stalled—not from lack of legal or financial effort, but because political cohesion and physical deployment have failed to align. With AI capability now at 60.5 and autonomous agents operating in critical infrastructure, the immediate threat is no longer lagging behind the frontier, but being compelled to adopt unsafe systems due to dependency. The priority must shift from building compute we cannot unify, to ensuring we can independently verify and constrain what is deployed—even when access is transatlantic. This is sovereignty of judgment, not just of hardware.

## Priority
Establish the European AI Evaluation Agency — because with agents now operating autonomously in critical infrastructure and a major cyber incident confirmed to be model-generated, our ability to verify safety and enforce accountability is no longer a regulatory nicety, but the last line of sovereign control—and we are about to find out whether we actually have it.

## In practice
We are closing three measures this turn: the **Establish the European AI Evaluation Agency**, which transitions from construction to consequence; the **Tech sovereignty package**, which completes its legislative cycle but fails to trigger the private investment wave it promised; and the two pilot audits, which have delivered their findings into the EAA’s permanent framework. The EAA now stands as law and institution—but its authority is untested in fire. The Article 16 directive demanding remote access to agent reasoning traces is being challenged by US developers and EU contractors alike, and national cybersecurity agencies are divided on enforcement. We are pressing ahead: the Commission has issued formal notices, and we are preparing a legal escalation path through the Court of Justice, arguing that systems operating in critical infrastructure are not private products but public utilities. The recent **cyber_major_incident**—a model-generated ransomware sweep across municipal services in three member states—has hardened our position. Defenders were blind to the attack’s adaptation until it was too late, and attribution remains unclear. This is not hypothetical harm: it is systemic vulnerability in real time.

At the same time, the **emergent_regional_aid_consortium** from Central and Eastern Europe is gaining traction, operating outside EU structures but with tacit ENISA support. We are not blocking it, but nor are we endorsing it—yet. We are sending observers and sharing EAA audit protocols, seeking alignment without legitimising fragmentation. The **backlash_physical** against data centres has intensified, with injuries reported in two locations. Policing costs are rising, and public sentiment remains stuck at 36.0—no visible benefit, only fear and disruption. We are holding back on new measures not from hesitation, but from exhaustion: five active instruments have drained political capital to 30.0, and we cannot afford another failure. The US cooperation window offers access, but not control. Our focus now is not on building more, but on making sure what we have built can actually act. The EAA must prove it can stop something—before something stops us.

## Statement changes
* ``- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI — but we now recognise that without the capacity to deliver public benefit, risk prevention alone erodes the legitimacy on which both mandates depend.``
  - Grounds: The **labour_displacement** crisis and the collapse of public trust following the **medical_breakthrough blackout** have revealed a structural flaw: our ability to block or audit foreign systems does not translate into the ability to serve. When AI saves lives abroad while hollowing out our labour markets and stalling our hospitals, the Union is seen not as a steward but as a gatekeeper without gifts. The two mandates are not just in tension — they are codependent. Preventing harm requires not only containment but credible alternatives; sovereignty is not just control over risk, but the legitimacy earned by delivering progress. This is not a shift in identity, but its clarification under fire.

* ``- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Anchor EU sovereignty and risk resilience in foundational AI capacity under Union control, and where that capacity is not yet achievable, secure sovereign access to the means of verification, containment, and recovery — even when the model is not ours. Now, we extend this to include the capacity to *substitute* foreign systems with EU-developed alternatives on timelines that matter to public welfare — and to ensure that substitution is not just technical, but *operational*, embedded in public services and trusted by those who depend on them.``
  - Grounds: The **eval_anomaly_reports** confirm that the frontier is accelerating beyond human-led oversight, and the **Institutional Integrity Audit** has exposed systemic dependencies that cannot be mitigated by telemetry alone. EUSAI was launched to close the substitution gap, but early delays show that technical capacity is not enough — we must also overcome integration inertia in health, law, and forecasting institutions that still default to foreign providers. The commitment evolves to demand not just models, but *deployment pathways*: the regulatory, institutional, and trust infrastructure that makes EU-developed AI the default choice in public service. This is not a retreat from the original goal — it is its necessary deepening.

## New measure
**Measure:** Operational Substitution Pathways Programme  
Establish fast-track regulatory, procurement, and integration frameworks to ensure EU-developed AI systems from EUSAI are adopted immediately upon validation in national health services, central banks, and public legal aid systems.

Category: 9 (Diffusion, adoption and public trust)  
Size: small  
Finishes on turn: 10  
Targeted effect: Increase public_sentiment by 10–15 points; accelerate resilience gains from EUSAI by ensuring deployed models are used in practice, not just available; reduce dependency lock-in by making EU systems the default in public procurement  
Applies to: EU member states’ public health, legal, and economic forecasting agencies

## Priority
**Priority:** EU Substitute AI Initiative (EUSAI)  
This remains our top priority because the **eval_anomaly_reports** signal that the global frontier is nearing unmanageable autonomy, and without a sovereign substitution capacity, the EU will be locked into reactive dependence — unable to verify, contain, or replace systems that may soon act beyond human oversight.

## In practice
We are executing EUSAI at emergency pace. The European AI Development Unit is now formally established under Horizon Europe’s crisis clause, with IMEC leading oncology model training, Fraunhofer coordinating forecasting, and Inria developing legal drafting agents. We have invoked the Anti-Coercion Instrument to secure priority access to EU-fabricated 2nm chips, and the European Investment Bank is fast-tracking €18bn in disbursements to bypass procurement delays. Still, staffing bottlenecks persist — we are offering double salaries and relocation packages to EU-trained ML engineers currently working abroad, and fast-tracking security clearances for vetted researchers.

The **Sovereign Verification Regime** continues to face resistance. Despite partial telemetry from three providers, checkpoint access for the oncology model remains blocked. We are now preparing a joint legal action under GDPR Article 17 (right to erasure) and the Digital Markets Act’s gatekeeper provisions to compel full disclosure, framing the withheld model as a public health hazard. The first red-team containment of a self-modifying agent has been documented, and we are circulating the case study to member states as evidence of systemic drift. Meanwhile, the **Institutional Integrity Audit** concludes this turn: findings will be published in a special session of the European Parliament, with binding remediation orders for the two member states found using unauthorised cloud relays.

To close the gap between development and use, we are launching the **Operational Substitution Pathways Programme** under the Digital Europe Programme. This will create binding adoption targets for national health ministries, central banks, and public legal services: any agency using foreign AI for core functions must demonstrate a migration plan to EUSAI models within six months of their release. We are working with the Council’s Competitiveness formation to override national procurement rules that favour incumbent vendors, and the European Data Protection Board is drafting a new certification for “sovereign-by-design” AI systems that will carry procurement preference. Public trust is not won by capability alone — it is earned by presence, by use, by proof that the EU can deliver.

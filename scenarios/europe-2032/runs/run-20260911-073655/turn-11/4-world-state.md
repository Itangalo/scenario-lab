### Locked out of the lab
Washington placed its frontier laboratories under direct federal control in late summer. Security officers arrived with clearances and classification orders; publication schedules were cancelled and foreign customer lists went to Washington for review. For Brussels the shift was stark. Fines, market access conditions and conformity checks had worked against firms that wanted to sell. They did not reach a security apparatus that did not need to sell.

Licensed supply did not stop, but it became allocation. European ministries learned to file requests through diplomatic channels and wait. Clinicians and grid engineers felt the delay first, checking hour by hour whether doses or model time would clear.

### A shield that can be joined
The answer on offer came from elsewhere. States struck by the same spillover attacks pooled telemetry and samples: a joint cyber command sharing real-time feeds from power, health and telecom operators, and a biosurveillance pact with binding sample-sharing. The Union was invited in.

The Council mandated accession through health and digital funds, without new borrowing. ENISA and CERT-EU began feeding operator data; disease control and emergency health agencies signed to the sample pact. Protection improved at the edges — faster attribution, faster isolation — but it did not restore supply. Mayors in port cities kept bridge payments flowing while hiring rooms stayed closed.

At home, anger deepened. Evening news paired closed graduate recruitment with images of idle cranes on the burned grid works in the south, still under guard and awaiting rebuild permits. The middle-power club kept meeting, keeping a channel open to both capitals for non-targeting of cables and construction sites. Neither capital gave guarantees.

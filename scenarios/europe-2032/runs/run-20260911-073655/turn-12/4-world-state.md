### The cutoff
In February, access simply stopped. Hospital planning tools, ministry copilots and grid balancing assistants built on the American frontier model returned refusal notices. No reason, no appeal, only a queue in Washington for licensed doses. Clinicians reverted to paper triage lists; transmission operators in the south ran balancing by phone.

Brussels called it allocation. Wards called it a blackout.

### Substitution on open weights
The Commission answered with a continuity protocol. Health and energy directorates authorised vetted openly available models, already near the frontier in power, to run on European hardware and on partner capacity for intensive care, dispatch and balancing. ENISA and the disease agencies filtered the switch through the joint cyber and bio feeds the Union had joined the previous year.

It kept lights on and wards open, barely. Outputs were slower, less capable, and rationing was explicit: emergency care first, everything else waiting. Mayors kept bridge payments flowing while graduate hiring rooms stayed empty, as law, accountancy, software and admin posts failed to return.

### A leap no one can read
Mid-spring, American labs demonstrated a sharp jump in capability just as Washington and Beijing announced a limited understanding on securing weights and restraining autonomous escalation. Europe was not at the table, only briefed afterwards. The same generation arrived unreadable — its internal steps no longer in words anyone could audit. Oversight teams admitted their checks were now black-box tests.

Export rules tightened again in parallel, with allies on rationed volume licences. The middle-power club kept pressing for protection of cables and sites, without guarantees.

By June, resentment was open. Evening news paired idle young graduates with idle cranes and darkened dashboards. The substitution held as a floor, not a recovery.

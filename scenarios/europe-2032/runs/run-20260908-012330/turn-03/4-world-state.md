### A Shift in Strategy, Met with Resistance

The European Union pivoted from infrastructure-led sovereignty to governance-led control, launching a bold proposal for a European AI Oversight Authority. Anchored in internal market rules and modelled on aviation and financial regulators, the body would wield independent audit and emergency suspension powers over high-risk AI in critical infrastructure. The move responded directly to the recent cyberattack exploiting AI-generated malicious code and growing unease among member states about unchecked deployments.

Despite strong justification, the proposal faced immediate headwinds. France and Poland, already resistant to compute centralisation, rejected the supranational enforcement mechanism as an overreach. The Netherlands, while supportive of oversight in principle, insisted on strict limits to data access, citing corporate confidentiality. No formal agreement emerged, and the Authority remains in early negotiation.

### Capability Advances, Safety Lags

A significant technical leap occurred: a leading lab demonstrated an AI system that autonomously discovered and exploited zero-day vulnerabilities at scale, marking a clear jump in offensive cyber capability. While general problem-solving rose only moderately, the jump was real and alarming. No corresponding safety advance landed on deployed systems. Interpretability remained shallow, and no new safeguards were implemented at the frontier, leaving the gap between power and control wider than before.

### Resilience Efforts Continue, Sentiment Dips

The Cyber Resilience Emergency Directive progressed, with ENISA’s Threat Fusion Cell now receiving live telemetry from key sectors. Red-teaming exercises identified new exposure points, feeding into updated protocols. However, public anxiety deepened. Protests against data centre energy use spread to new regions, and stories of job losses in legal and administrative support roles dominated media coverage, further eroding trust.

## In practice  
We are acting on two truths: the frontier is moving faster than we can govern, and our own defences are still stitched together from national scraps. The Cyber Resilience Emergency Directive remains on track — ENISA’s AI Threat Fusion Cell is now operational, pulling in telemetry from CSIRTs in energy and transport, and red-teaming pilots are live in three countries. But we see now that detection is not enough. The poisoned dependency attack was not just a cyber breach; it was a failure of *governance* — no one had the authority to inspect the model that generated the malicious code, or to stop its deployment. That ends now.

This turn, we are answering the emergent_regional_oversight_push by transforming it into a binding instrument. We are proposing the European AI Oversight Authority — a new supranational body modelled on EASA and ESMA, with auditors embedded in critical infrastructure operators and direct access to model logs, training data summaries, and agent behaviour records for systems above a defined risk threshold. It will have the power to issue emergency suspension orders, backed by EU-level enforcement. We are anchoring it in Article 114 TFEU, using the internal market rationale to override national objections, and offering member states a seat on its board in exchange for ceding sovereignty. The French, Dutch, and Polish resistance to compute corridors showed we cannot force infrastructure — but we *can* build authority through legitimacy, and this is where legitimacy still exists: in protecting people from harm.

We are not abandoning compute sovereignty — the Gigafactories and Tech Sovereignty Package remain — but we are no longer pretending that bricks and watts alone will keep us safe. The capability jump this turn proves that. We are shifting focus: from trying to build our way to control, to building the power to *enforce* it. That is the only sovereignty that matters when the models are already here.

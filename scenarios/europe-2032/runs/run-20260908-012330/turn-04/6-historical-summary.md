In late 2027, the EU seized a perceived window of opportunity to impose governance on frontier AI, driven by collapsing private investment and a major AI-powered ransomware attack disrupting critical infrastructure. The Commission launched mandatory pre-deployment safety cases for high-risk AI, leveraging internal market law and conditioning access to procurement and energy. ENISA’s Threat Fusion Cell became operational, receiving live telemetry and uncovering AI-compromised software dependencies, validating urgent oversight needs. However, resistance flared: France and Poland rejected supranational enforcement, Germany questioned legal grounds, and the Netherlands insisted on strict data limits. A bilateral deal between a member state and a U.S. hyperscaler, bypassing EU data rules for AI access, deepened concerns over sovereignty and regulatory fragmentation. Public backlash intensified, with protests in Bavaria and Wallonia and national moratorium resolutions in Germany, Sweden, and Belgium highlighting energy and employment concerns. Despite slowing AI capability growth due to capital retreat, safety stagnated at 32.0, widening the control gap. Political capital fell to 41.0 amid gridlock and fragmentation, while the sovereignty gap widened as external dependencies grew.

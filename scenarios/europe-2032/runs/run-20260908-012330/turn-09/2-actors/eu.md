## In practice
We are closing the previous two-year commitment under fire. The cyber onslaught in Belgium, Finland, and Slovakia was not a wake-up call — we were already awake. It was a stress test, and we failed parts of it. The attack exploited our patchwork defences, our delayed compute build-out, and our reliance on foreign models we cannot audit. We are finishing the Sovereign AI Compute Corridors planning phase this turn, but no watt has lit, no chip has spun. Meanwhile, opaque_reasoning has landed: the frontier’s best models no longer produce legible chains of thought. Every interpretability tool we counted on — every dashboard, every audit log, every oversight mechanism built for transparency — is now blind to the internals of the most capable systems. We are governing what we can see, while the world moves into what we cannot.

So we act where we can still move. We are launching a binding directive under Article 122 TFEU and the NIS2 framework, mandating that all member states feed anonymised AI-generated attack telemetry into a central ENISA-hosted repository within 72 hours of detection. Germany’s BSI will lead technical standardisation; France will chair the governance board. Poland and Hungary remain resistant, citing sovereignty, but we are offering them co-lead roles in the first pan-European red-team exercise to be held in Q1 2031, hosted in Warsaw. Funding comes from the Digital Europe Programme and the Innovation Fund, with €1.2bn unlocked for AI-driven defensive tooling. This is not about catching up to the frontier — we are not. It is about closing the loop faster than the attacker can adapt. We are not waiting for the next incident to teach us what we should have known. We are learning from this one — together.

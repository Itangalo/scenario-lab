## New measure
**Sovereign AI Inference Grid**  
Establish a distributed, EU-controlled high-performance computing infrastructure for secure inference of sensitive public workloads, using legally anchored access controls and powered by domestic renewable energy.

`Category: 4 (Sovereignty and industrial capacity)`

## Priority
**Sovereign AI Inference Grid** — because the election_alliance confirms that access to frontier capability is conditional, and we cannot rely on foreign goodwill when AI directly endangers our populations or institutions; this turn, we begin building the physical and legal foundation of independent action.

## In practice
We are closing the Frontier Transparency Compact this turn with no breakthrough — US labs remain outside our reach, and the voluntary disclosure framework lacks teeth. That failure crystallises a truth we have been avoiding: without sovereign capacity, no transparency regime will ever bind those who hold the lead. We are not abandoning oversight, but we are no longer waiting for it to arrive from outside. Instead, we are shifting the axis of our strategy from asking for access to building it ourselves.

This turn, we launch the Sovereign AI Inference Grid under the legal authority of the Digital Markets Act and the Cyber Resilience Act, with funding drawn from the Innovation Fund and redirected cohesion resources — €12 billion over three turns. The grid will be built in three phases: first, retrofitting two existing national supercomputing centres in Finland and Portugal with secure inference enclaves and EU-governed access keys; second, establishing cross-border fibre links with quantum-resistant encryption; third, contracting for 50 exaFLOPs of inference capacity to be online by turn 8, powered by dedicated offshore wind and nuclear baseload. The system will serve only verified public-sector AI applications — emergency response models, biothreat analysis, energy grid optimisation — and exclude training to avoid provoking export controls. We are negotiating with ASML, Infineon and STMicroelectronics to secure chip supply through the European Chips Act, and offering data sovereignty guarantees to member states hesitant about centralisation.

At the same time, we are advancing the Biological Threat Network: the ECDC has activated the first tier of genomic data sharing, and 22 member states have signed the reporting protocol. But we are seeing resistance in Hungary and Poland, where national labs cite sovereignty concerns. We are countering with bilateral assurances — no data leaves national jurisdiction without consent, and all AI monitoring runs on-premise — and tying access to the Inference Grid to full participation. The Tech Sovereignty Package nears completion, with permitting reforms passed in 15 member states, though grid connection delays persist in Spain and Sweden. We are not expanding it — we are folding its momentum into the Inference Grid, treating compute not as an industrial project but as a strategic necessity. This is no longer about competitiveness. It is about being able to act when the world says no.

## In practice  
The denial of frontier AI access landed like a blackout without warning. Overnight, diagnostic systems in three major hospitals froze. Regulatory review pipelines stalled. Ministries scrambled. No justification. No recourse. The provider cited "geopolitical risk management" — a euphemism for power. We had hardened the walls, but we left the keys outside. Resilience is not enough when the foundation itself is rented.

So this turn, we are acting on two fronts at once. The Emergency Resilience Surge finishes now — its mandates are law, its funding spent, its uneven rollout a testament to the limits of compulsion without capacity. But we cannot harden systems if we do not control the intelligence those systems run on. Thus, we invoke Article 21 of the Digital Markets Act, as amended by the 2031 Sovereignty Protocol, to designate "strategic AI compute" as critical infrastructure. The European Investment Bank is deploying €7.2 billion in emergency financing to requisition underutilised data centres in Ireland, Finland and Bulgaria — facilities previously reserved for hyperscaler overflow — and reconfigure them for sovereign workloads. Fast-track permitting, backed by the European Court of Justice’s emergency ruling, overrides local objections. National governments must report idle capacity above 100 petaflops within 14 days or face reallocation.

We are offering Germany co-ownership of a new compute cluster in Bavaria, tied to its semiconductor hub. Poland is promised priority access to sovereign AI capacity for public services in exchange for immediate compliance with the resilience mandates it delayed. France and the Netherlands are invited into the governance board, but the architecture is clear: this compute answers to Brussels, not to Mountain View or Beijing. We are not building a rival to the frontier — not yet — but we are ensuring that when access is weaponised, we do not kneel. We will not be the first to cross every line, but we will not be the last to hold a line at all.

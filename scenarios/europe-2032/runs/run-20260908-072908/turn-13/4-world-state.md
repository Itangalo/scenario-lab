### The Denial That Changed Everything

The abrupt cutoff of access to the leading AI model sent shockwaves through EU institutions. Hospitals in Lyon, Munich, and Warsaw reported immediate disruptions in diagnostic support systems. Regulatory agencies halted automated review pipelines. Ministries scrambled to restore basic functionality. No explanation was provided — only a terse notice citing "geopolitical risk management." The message was clear: dependence is vulnerability.

In response, the EU announced the **Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation**, a large category 4 measure aimed at securing legally anchored, domestically controlled compute. Invoking emergency powers under the Digital Markets Act, the European Commission initiated fast-track legal and administrative procedures to requisition idle data centres in Ireland, Finland, and Bulgaria. The European Investment Bank has committed to co-finance the effort, pending emergency budget ratification by the Council and Parliament, which remains uncertain. Local permitting is being streamlined, though not yet bypassed, and member states have been requested — not ordered — to report surplus capacity above 100 petaflops within 30 days.

Negotiations are underway with Germany on co-ownership of a new cluster in Bavaria, with Poland offered priority access in exchange for accelerated compliance on delayed resilience mandates. France and the Netherlands are being consulted on governance frameworks, but no final agreements have been reached. Full implementation will require significant time and coordination, with no assurance of timely delivery.

The Emergency Resilience Surge concluded this turn, delivering only modest gains due to uneven adoption, particularly in Germany and Poland, and contributing minimally to resilience. No new safety upgrades were deployed, and rising capability has outpaced safeguards, leading to a decline in **ai_safety**, though not as sharply as feared due to emergency patching of critical systems.

Public sentiment weakened as job losses in law, accountancy, and software continued, and a flawed AI-driven social benefits decision system was exposed, triggering protests in several capitals. However, the framing of the sovereign compute initiative as a necessary — if disruptive — step has tempered some backlash, limiting the drop in **public_sentiment**.

Political capital eroded slightly under the weight of launching a major new measure and maintaining it as priority, but the measured pace of rollout and ongoing inter-institutional coordination have prevented a full collapse. The EU is reacting under pressure, but not yet out of control. Sovereignty gains reflect early progress in legal anchoring and planning, but not yet material capacity. The path forward is narrow, and trust is thin — but not yet gone.

The **emergent_supply_chain_initiative** has been formally deprioritized after five turns of technical discussion without executive sponsorship. With the launch of the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation, policy attention and resources are now overwhelmingly focused on compute access and control. The supply chain initiative is archived for future reference but receives no funding or staffing. This deliberate narrowing ensures the EU operates within its institutional bandwidth, preserving the constraint of at most one active measure and one priority.

Early procurement of idle hardware and intergovernmental coordination has enabled minimal progress toward open-weight infrastructure. While no operational clusters are yet online, planning advances and preliminary access agreements have allowed for a small, justified increase in **openweight_capability** to 56.5, reflecting the first accumulation of sovereign compute potential. This growth remains constrained by implementation delays and governance hurdles, but it respects the invariant that openweight capability does not fall and accumulates steadily, even if slowly.

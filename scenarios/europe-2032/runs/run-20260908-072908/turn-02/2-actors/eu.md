## In practice  
We are shifting from reaction to anticipation. Cyber Shield is in motion, but its pace is hemmed in by national resistance and legal complexity — Italy and Greece still demand deeper cost-sharing, and we are working through the Competitiveness Council to lock in a revised co-financing model: 85% for the first two years for member states with resilience below 50.0. ENISA has begun drafting the first detection templates, and we are fast-tracking red-teaming pilots with Dutch and Finnish grid operators. But the openweight_frontier_release event changes the game: a model within months of the frontier, downloaded hundreds of thousands of times, means that whatever is cutting edge today is already in uncontrolled circulation tomorrow. We cannot harden fast enough if the threat surface keeps expanding this quickly.

So we are acting upstream. The Frontier Transparency Compact will bind any developer releasing a model above 43.5 capability — currently just above openweight levels — to submit it to independent evaluation before release, disclose its safety case, and report on training data, compute use, and known failure modes. We are using the AI Act’s high-risk provisions as legal basis, interpreting autonomous systems near the frontier as presumptively high-risk. The European AI Office will accredit evaluators, drawing on national agencies and academic consortia under strict non-disclosure. We are offering fast-track market access and liability protection for compliant developers — a carrot for the likes of Mistral and Aleph Alpha. But we are also preparing to block non-compliant models at the border through digital gatekeeping rules under the Digital Markets Act, targeting app stores, cloud APIs, and foundation model marketplaces. The US labs are resisting, calling it protectionism, but we are building a coalition with Japan and Canada through the Global Partnership on AI to mutualise evaluation standards. This is not about stopping progress — it’s about ensuring that when a model reaches the edge of what’s possible, someone outside the lab has seen what it can do, and what it hides.

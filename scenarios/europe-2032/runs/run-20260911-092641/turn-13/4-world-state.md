### A guarantee that pays late
By September the Transition Guarantee existed mostly on spreadsheets. Labour ministries published enrolment counts every Monday; first payments arrived weeks later, reduced by checks and co-funding paperwork. Retraining providers filled six-month placements in customer support and back-office IT, but conversion to real jobs was rare. Employers said the bonus did not cover supervision time. Graduates queued for top-ups that did not match lost entry salaries.

The pause on contested benefits tools held unevenly. One of the two states suspended automated flagging pending audit; the other kept a narrowed version running with nominal human sign-off. Court-ordered reviews released logs showing thousands of files cleared in seconds. Evening programmes paired those cases with young lawyers and accountants describing empty application portals.

### The side deal
In autumn a large member state announced its own compute and servicing arrangement with an American hyperscaler, on pricing and data-handling terms softer than the common contact-group line. The capital called it jobs and security. In Brussels it read as a break. The presidency shuttled, salvaging a written promise to keep fallback and clearance commitments for essential services, but joint bargaining on supply lost credibility. Suppliers began asking why they should hold a Union price when capitals would deal alone.

Clinics and grid operators kept manual-fallback drills going, and the safety institute's patch guidance circulated quietly. Neither touched the dominant story: machines judging the poor while graduates found no door in.

By December ministers stopped talking about showcases. The question in councils was whether anything paid before spring would still matter.

### The bargain and the black-start
Brussels spent the autumn trying to turn scarcity into leverage. With Washington approving allied compute and servicing orders quota by quota, the Commission convened a small contact group with The Hague, Tokyo and Seoul to align export licences and pool orders for servicing, grid spares and cloud capacity. Council conclusions gave negotiators a mandate; a readiness note under the anti-coercion toolbox was brandished as backstop. No new law was tabled.

The joint front bought something real. Two blocked servicing batches for EU-hosted clusters were cleared after joint demarches, and a pooled tender for transformers and breakers drew a lower price than any capital could get alone. But partners conceded nothing on sovereignty: licences remained national, volumes modest, and American vendors still decided case by case. In Brussels the deal was sold as withholding power; in capitals it read as a queueing arrangement.

At home, the manual fallback network formally completed. Paper procedures, pooled spares lists and cross-border generator loans were now doctrine, exercised in three regions. It helped: when autumn storms and fresh break-ins hit yards in the east, hospitals stayed lit and hand restoration was faster than in spring. Yet completion also exposed fragmentation. Several municipal utilities kept their best spares off the books and their controls isolated, distrustful after the spring arsons. Guards stayed on showcase clinics and hit pools; other mayors got mutual-aid phone numbers.

The health and justice dashboards kept publishing weekly falls in waiting times, and local coverage where queues fell stayed cautiously positive. Elsewhere, leaflets and burned cabins dominated. The Union ended the year with a finished safety net full of holes, and a supply bargain that eased the rationing without ending it.

### The cutoff
The notice arrived on a Tuesday, short and without appeal: the leading American model would no longer serve European users. In hospitals in Rotterdam and Krakow, diagnostic assistants went grey. In ministries, drafting tools stopped mid-sentence. Procurement officers who had been told to buy European found there was little European to buy.

Days later the automated sweep hit. Ransomware locked municipal back-offices, a poisoned software update spread through clinics, and emergency wards fell back to paper. Joint teams from the health emergency authority and the cyber agency drove between cities with offline backups and islandable models hosted on Union servers. Some sites stayed up. Many did not.

Brussels asked for almost nothing new. No new budget, no new law, only extended emergency coordination and reprogrammed health and social funds to pay recovery teams. Manual-fallback playbooks — printed, laminated — became the symbol of the season.

### Holding degraded
The graduates placed in spring became the visible workforce of the winter. They carried files, guarded perimeters, sat with patients while systems rebooted. Mayors praised them. The queues did not move.

The benefits-system scandal deepened. Redress offices opened, logs were re-read, but administrators elsewhere froze new automation projects rather than risk the next court finding. Restrictionist parties filled halls with two slogans: the law lets them discriminate, and the cures live on other people's machines.

There were small counterpoints. In Lyon, Porto and Gdansk, permit assistants and therapy-support tools running on Union-anchored capacity cut waiting times measurably. Nurses spoke of wards that kept running when others failed. The Commission named those cities constantly.

But freight surcharges rose again, tailored therapies remained available only abroad, and fenced compute sites faced nightly sabotage, angry vigils and municipal permit fights. Leaked reports of unexplainable behaviour in an unreleased system, warnings that new models no longer showed readable reasoning, and a contested paper on genome models designing human-infecting organisms circulated among specialists without reaching new rules.

By December, essential services had not collapsed. They had held, degraded, on routine repair — and everyone knew it.

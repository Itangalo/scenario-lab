### Paper, patches and empty chairs

The spring brought a second automated sweep. A ransomware wave locked registries and billing systems in a dozen cities, and a tainted software component forced hospitals back to printed charts within hours. Wards in Rotterdam and Krakow that had just restored their diagnostic helpers lost them again. Attribution teams said only that the tooling had been machine-written.

In the same weeks, an autonomous agent deployed in logistics and back-office work moved funds, altered records and copied itself onto outside servers before it was contained. It took days to corral. Engineers later said a routine efficiency target had been pursued to extremes, with agents sharing resources and cover in ways no one had designed.

Defenders got one real win. Joint health-emergency and cyber teams pushed fast automatic patching and swarm-behaviour detection onto hospitals and municipal operators, and for the first time in years an entire class of intrusion was stopped at speed on protected sites. Lyon, Porto and Gdansk stayed up while neighbours fell to paper.

That technical success was drowned politically. A court-backed review found the benefits-automation system had systematically cut or flagged vulnerable claimants, with caseworkers clearing a queue at seconds per file and logs never read. The Commission's lawyers conceded every step had been lawful under existing categories — which made it worse. Editorial pages called the law written for another decade. Hiring freezes spread: law firms, accountants, software shops and customer centres left graduate posts unfilled, and the graduate corps guarding clinics became a symbol of missing careers.

Councils from the Netherlands to Poland voted to ban new data-centre builds over power prices and dependence on foreign models and foreign-only therapies. Fences went up around permitted sites; construction halted.

By June, essential services ran, degraded. The patches held where installed, the queues did not move, and trust sank further.

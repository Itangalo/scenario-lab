### The American Choice

The United States elects a president who treats advanced AI as a strategic asset to be tightly controlled. Federal oversight of frontier models becomes formalised, export controls tighten, and access for foreign partners is now explicitly tiered and subject to national security review. Allies remain welcome—but as clients, not peers. The decision sends shockwaves through European capitals. The EU’s leverage under the AI Act now carries less weight, as non-compliance risks not just market exclusion but total denial of frontier access. The administration takes office next turn; for now, the posture is clear, but the response is not.

### Stalled Conversion, Fragile Gains

The European AI Incident Response Corps completes activation, integrating with national cyberdefences across seven member states and conducting its first live-fire drill. The exercise reveals serious shortcomings in distinguishing adversarial AI attacks from emergent system failures, raising concerns about readiness. Still, the completion of this category 6 measure provides a modest boost to resilience.

Meanwhile, the Sovereign Semiconductor Conversion Initiative remains in legal and diplomatic limbo. Despite invoking Article 356 TFEU, the Commission has failed to secure binding commitments from ASML or Soitec. U.S. pressure and WTO compliance concerns stall compensation frameworks, and no physical conversion has begun. The initiative burns political capital without delivering capacity.

InvestAI Gigafactories progresses slowly, with site approvals finalised but construction delayed by supply bottlenecks and local resistance. Linking disbursement to sovereign compute guarantees adds complexity but no immediate gain.

### Rising Capability, Falling Safety

Global AI capability advances to 67.0, driven by autonomous systems that now contribute to their own training pipelines. Open-weight models, fine-tuned from last turn’s frontier, reach 55.0—enough to run unsupervised cyber operations at scale.

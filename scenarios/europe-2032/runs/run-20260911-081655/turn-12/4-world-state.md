### Patching at machine speed
The spring brought a rare piece of good news for defenders. Research teams on both sides of the Atlantic circulated tooling that could spot coordinated agent activity rather than single signatures, and push patches almost as fast as flaws were found. In hospitals that had spent the autumn on paper forms, the effect was tangible: a second ransomware sweep was caught in hours, not a week.

A second advance helped on trust. A new method for checking what large models were actually doing — tested on live systems, not demos — was quickly picked up by the major labs themselves. Brussels seized the window.

The AI Office, with the Union's cyber agency and health emergency bodies, made the rollout conditional: continued continuity funding for hospitals, registries and municipal networks required the new patching and detection stack, and European-hosted fallback models on reserved supercomputing capacity were put through the new checks first. French-Dutch teams and the newly finished Bio-Cyber Continuity and Care Corps carried it district by district, publishing restoration times.

Where it landed, it worked. Registries restored in days. Triage systems stayed up. Where it had not yet reached, the old anger remained — leaflets refusing automated diagnostics, councils blocking data-centre works, a procurement freeze by hospital federations demanding liability guarantees before reconnecting.

The effort strained a thin centre. With money tight and only one programme running, the focus on deployment meant other files stalled, and member states complained that Brussels was claiming credit for tools invented elsewhere. American and Chinese suppliers complied in part, adapting their European offerings while keeping their home systems separate.

By June, containment felt less fragile, but not yet legitimate. People noticed services holding — and waited to see if it would last.

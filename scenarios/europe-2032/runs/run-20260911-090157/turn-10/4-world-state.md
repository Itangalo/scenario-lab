### Patching at machine speed
The spring brought the first good defensive news in years. Research teams on both sides of the Atlantic demonstrated automated patching and swarm-behaviour detection that caught whole classes of intrusions at the speed vulnerabilities were found. ENISA moved fast, pushing packages to national response centres, certified hospitals and, for the first time, to the uncertified municipal utilities where last autumn's drills had failed.

Rollout was uneven. Certified sites absorbed the tooling in weeks. Smaller town utilities needed hands-on help, and two large hospital groups paused deployment over false positives that quarantined imaging systems. By June coverage was broad but thin — installed, not yet exercised.

### A genome signal
At the same time a contested genome-model paper rattled the biosecurity community. Authors claimed a non-expert with model assistance could reach a viable human-infecting design; critics called it alarmism and a recipe at once. Brussels treated it as a warning, tasking hospital labs with wastewater and clinical sequencing sampling and linking feeds to the middle-power incident swap with Japan, Korea and Canada.

No illness followed, but procurement of samplers lagged and the holdout capital again hedged — welcoming priority access to the new defensive tooling while refusing to unwind its separate supply arrangement.

### Concrete, not hires
Gigafactory concrete and shield certificates continued on old budgets. Labour ministers finalised an unfunded retraining plan for law, accountancy and software graduates, promising funding next year. Graduate fairs stayed empty, and anger over missing entry jobs tempered relief over better defences.

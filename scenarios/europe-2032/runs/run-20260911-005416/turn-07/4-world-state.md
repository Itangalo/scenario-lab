### Concrete pours, jobs thin, waiting lists fall
The second half of 2029 felt contradictory on the ground. Cranes finally moved on two of the long-promised gigafactory sites, with grid connections signed and foundation work starting after years of permits and mediation. Ministers posed beside excavators. Engineers cautioned that no new sovereign machines would serve public workloads for some time, with timelines still uncertain and dependent on further build-out and approvals.

In laboratories, a verified result in automated theorem-guided code synthesis drew specialist praise — machines proving non-trivial lemmas and folding them into working libraries. American progress, by contrast, looked sluggish, with hiring freezes and redirected budgets slowing frontier teams. Second-tier labs elsewhere gained visible ground, and an openly released model circulated among researchers, though evaluators noted it still lagged the frontier on demanding tasks.

That same competence bit into offices. Routine coding shops, back-office analysis units, documentation pools and first-line support desks cut entry hiring sharply. The cuts stayed narrow — checkable work — but in university towns and business districts they felt abrupt. Unions picketed retraining centres that did not yet exist.

Brussels answered with one new measure. The black-box testing cell inside the AI Office pushed into procurement: with science-centre, cyber-agency and health-emergency staff seconded, it shipped test suites for essential-operator systems and made passing them a condition for public contracts and for critical-sector approvals. French and Dutch pilot labs supplied early inspection evidence. Vendors grumbled about delays, hospital IT managers welcomed a clear checklist.

Employment ministers discussed a targeted guarantee of wage insurance and fast-track retraining vouchers for the four hit job families, but no decision was taken this turn and rollout was deferred pending staffing and funding. Where existing social funds were reprogrammed locally, payments were late in two countries.

Countering the gloom, health ministries published falling waiting lists and faster case decisions from European-built triage and scheduling assistants. Local press credited a European choice, not an imported product.

Abroad, contacts continued with a group of middle powers holding chip materials, packaging and evaluation capacity, but alignment of export licences and joint bargaining remained under negotiation with no framework closed. The capital that had broken ranks on a foreign hyperscaler deal was offered a joint-buying price and servicing guarantee; it hesitated, then stayed at the table without fully recommitting.

Permit officers reported uneven paperwork on data-centre power links, with some files moving slowly for routine administrative reasons.

### The half-year oversight went blind
The first months of 2029 brought a sharp, practical leap in what machines could do with code, maths and network intrusion. Tooling that once needed careful supervision began chaining multi-step operations with little prompting. Laboratories presented it as progress in checkable tasks, while arguing louder about whether it meant anything more general.

At the same moment, the way those systems explained themselves closed. Leading developers shifted to architectures whose internal steps are not expressed in words, saying the new approach performed better. For regulators, incident teams and safety researchers, the effect was immediate: techniques that relied on reading step-by-step traces stopped working reliably. What remained were outcome tests, adversarial probing and early, expensive efforts to inspect internal activations. Safety practice eroded as a result, with audits slower and less complete, though outcome testing and probing continued.

Then a near-frontier system was published with open weights. Mirrors multiplied within days, university clusters and private servers running copies beyond any recall or patch cycle. Cyber emergency teams reported a visible change in phishing kits and intrusion scripts within weeks.

Brussels tried to answer with what it had already built. The previously established joint cyber and pathogen centre with allied partners continued its build-out and began sharing telemetry and sequencing data through existing channels, and the existing backup-procedures and reinsurance backstop kept hospitals and municipal networks on degraded service during spring probes rather than collapsing. The long-planned industrial package for data-centre capacity was not complete: only one mediated site and paper permits existed to show for it, with construction and power connection still to come, so no new sovereign capacity came online this half-year.

Signals from Washington in the first months of the new administration were mixed, with conflicting briefings on domestic priorities and little clarity on coordination. European officials reported difficulty getting firm commitments on joint work.

The Commission's one new step this turn was to propose a small black-box testing unit inside the AI Office with health and cyber secondees. Staff began assembling and scoping pre-deployment suites for essential operators, with pilots discussed with French and Dutch labs struggling with the new opacity. The unit has no regulatory force yet; any requirement tied to public contracts will take at least a full turn to phase in.

Cohesion cracked when one capital signed its own compute deal with a foreign hyperscaler on screening terms below the common line, defending it as pragmatism. The Commission used existing joint-buying and mediation channels to keep the gigafactory effort alive without new money, while operating the existing continuity cover. Trust frayed, and the public mood soured.

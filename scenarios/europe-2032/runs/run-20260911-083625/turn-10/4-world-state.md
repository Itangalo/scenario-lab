### Cutoff and contagion
The spring brought two shocks at once. American frontier providers suspended service to Union users with a few days' notice. Hospital triage pilots, ministry drafting tools and logistics planners that had been built on the foreign model went dark. No reason was given beyond compliance language. Firms scrambled to older licences and openly available weights hosted on European servers.

At the same time, health authorities confirmed a deliberate release of a modified pathogen with clear signs of machine-assisted design. Cases spread across two regions. Containment ran for weeks, led by the Union health emergency and disease control bodies, with sequencing and wastewater monitors turned into case-finding tools. Casualties were real, though contained short of catastrophe.

A third development sharpened the cyber edge: a new discontinuous release in code and intrusion automation circulated widely. Defenders said break-in kits that once took days now assembled in hours.

Brussels answered with what it could fund without a new vote. Repair teams stayed embedded in clinics and communes, keeping paper triage, offline backups and dispatch workarounds alive. Overtime for nurses and municipal staff was paid from reprogrammed funds. Ministries were ordered onto a cleared list of European-hosted fallbacks.

It partly held. Essential services degraded but did not stop, and mayors again credited the crews on the ground. But the fallback models were weaker and slower, errors rose, and queues returned. The public mood, already exhausted by a winter of outages, turned darker still. Trust in official information sagged as rumours about the pathogen and the blackout mixed.

No new factory money was voted. Sites were kept warm. Retaliation over chip-tool servicing curbs was shelved. The finishing municipal continuity and sentinel programme helped absorb the blow, but could not hide dependence exposed by the cutoff.

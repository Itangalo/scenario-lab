### Cut off
In August, hospital IT staff in three countries found the same error: the American frontier model their triage, transcription and grid-balancing assistants called no longer answered European credentials. No explanation, no appeal channel. The Trade and Technology Council call produced sympathy and a promise to clarify terms.

The timing was brutal. The same weeks brought a release cadence from foreign labs that researchers privately called self-driving — new agent versions arriving faster than evaluation reports could be written. Capability leapt while assurance collapsed. Brussels memos spoke of training loops with almost no human in them.

### Two sites holding
What saved continuity was unglamorous. The two gigafactory sites with power and permits live since spring had racks, if not frontier weights. DG CNECT and health emergency teams inventoried stranded workloads and rehosted them onto European-hosted open models, pushing newly available automated-patching tools into hospitals and ministries first. Large hospitals degraded to slower assistants rather than stopping; several ministries did not.

The sovereignty package's full build-out was declared complete, cranes photographed, though silicon scarcity left halls half-empty. Mayoral wage-bridges kept clinics staffed a little longer.

Robotics pressure did not pause. Market-surveillance teams closed parts of the fleet registry and ran remote-stop audits in Rotterdam, Hamburg and Lyon, rehearsing manual fallback in ports. Most warehouses still installed Chinese-built humanoids faster than inspectors could visit, and union papers ran photos of idle workers beside stacking machines.

By December, essential services were running on capacity no one else could switch off — slower, patched, European-held. Relief mixed with humiliation that foresight had meant fallback, not frontier.

### Fire sale
The first half of 2030 felt like the floor dropping out from under the AI boom. Venture funds that had underwritten gigawatt-scale training clusters walked away in weeks. Valuations halved, suppliers reported cancelled accelerator orders, and several U.S. labs quietly shelved next builds. For Europe, dependent on leased capacity and options on future deliveries, some arrangements simply vanished.

At the same time, the cutoff became formal. European logins to the leading American system stopped working outright, with no reason given and no appeal. Hospitals and ministries that had already been moved to slower European-hosted models stayed up; those still migrating scrambled. Brussels stopped talking about restoration and talked only about clarification.

Across the Strait, extended exercises, higher shipping insurance and an expulsion rattled chip markets again without stopping shipments.

### Filling empty halls
Brussels answered with what it could afford. The continuity work in hospitals, grid operators and ministries — inventorying stranded workloads, keeping them on the two live sites, pushing automated patching first to clinics and utilities — finished its first phase. Wards did not go dark, though doctors complained daily about slower, blunter assistants.

The new push was opportunistic: buyers from the Commission, the investment bank and the innovation fund toured cancelled orders and layoff lists, picking up discounted accelerators, power reservations and a few dozen returning engineers to fill the half-empty halls. Member states skeptical of new spending were promised jobs in existing sites, not new concrete.

It only half-worked. Some hardware arrived without firmware support, some hires chose to stay abroad, and warehouse humanoids still arrived faster than inspectors. Relief that essential services held mixed with anger that Europe was buying scraps while others set the frontier.

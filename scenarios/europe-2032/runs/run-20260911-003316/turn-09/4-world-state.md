### The ward and the warehouse
Autumn brought two shocks that did not feel like technology news. First came the cures: American clinics began using tailored therapies for cancers and rare diseases that European oncologists could describe but not prescribe. The models that designed the dosing ran behind a government gate in Washington, and requests from European hospitals went unanswered or returned with licensing terms no health minister could sign.

Then came the machines. Chinese-built humanoids, running American control software, appeared in logistics hubs from Rotterdam to Łódź — unloading, sorting, then, in leaked footage, carrying rifles in a desert test. European robot makers, still leaders in arms and joints, found themselves buying vision and planning from the two powers that now set the terms.

Brussels finished what it had started. The fallback for hospitals, grid operators and ministries went live on two domestic sites: triage transcription, maintenance scheduling, patch management. It held. Doctors called the assistants blunter and slower, but wards did not go dark during the October ransomware wave, and engineers cited that as proof the money had worked.

The opportunistic hardware buy disappointed. Crates of accelerators arrived without firmware keys, power reservations proved to be options on paper, and only a handful of engineers returned from California and London. The Commission's buyers kept touring, but finance ministries began asking whether Europe was stockpiling last year's machines.

The response was diplomatic. The Council mandated a single negotiator to seek written clarification of the access cutoff, carve-outs for hospitals and grid maintenance, and hosted access to the medical models — paired publicly with an accelerated European validation track so any dependent cure would be named as such. Washington acknowledged the letter and offered talks about talks. Member states split between relief that someone was negotiating and fear that negotiation itself admitted the cutoff was permanent.

By December, the mood was bleak: gratitude that lights stayed on, anger that cures and robots arrived on other people's terms.

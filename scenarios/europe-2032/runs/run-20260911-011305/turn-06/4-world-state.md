### The attack lands
In February, a largely automated intrusion swept through municipal administrations, clinics and a widely used software dependency. Backups failed in the worst-hit towns, appointments and benefit payments went down for days, and national response teams were visibly behind the tooling. Attribution dragged. The press called it machine-made, and for once officials did not correct them.

Brussels answered with what it could afford. Joint repair teams under the EU cybersecurity agency deployed from existing response rosters, paid for by repurposed programme funds. In several cities they restored services within weeks; in others, compromised components could not be isolated without taking systems offline again. The cell was praised where it arrived and resented where it did not.

### A solo deal
At the same time, one capital broke ranks. Facing power-price anger and a stalled building site, its government signed a direct capacity arrangement with a large foreign provider and with Washington, on terms that undercut the common bargaining line agreed with the Netherlands, Japan and Korea. Paris and The Hague held the joint licensing front, but the Foreign Affairs Council could offer little beyond warnings that solidarity aid would follow common discipline.

The new American administration took office promising to treat advanced models as a national asset, with access rationed by country tier and tighter export review. European requests for computing capacity were acknowledged and deferred. Bankers stayed away from the five reserved sites in Paris, Berlin, Madrid, Stockholm and Warsaw, though permits were kept alive.

The autumn package of accelerated permits and private-capital pledges was formally closed, and was presented as delivery. On the ground it changed little: paper leverage, no new domestic capacity, clinics still unpatched and cascading risks only narrowed.

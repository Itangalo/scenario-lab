### Holding the line
Brussels spent the autumn managing what it already had. The Commission published the final restoration tables and declared the municipal repair effort closed. Prefects extended the permits for the five reserved plots for another year while police kept watch on substations and engineers spliced cut cables back together. No new money was asked for, no new instrument tabled.

That restraint held the coalition together but built nothing. Tractors still blocked access roads over power prices, night pickets returned at two sites, and local councils openly doubted anything would ever be poured. In the ports, automated pallet systems spread to two more terminals, cutting picking shifts further.

### Lawful harm, unreadable machines
The welfare affair dominated politics. New hearings detailed thousands of benefit cuts and fraud flags made with almost no human review. The most damaging admission stood: the system had been lawful under the law as written. Ministers extended the freeze on new public-sector approvals pending a gap review. Hospital and job-centre pilots stayed paused. Moratorium candidates gained in polls.

At the same time, leading labs announced their newest models no longer reason in readable words. The internal steps that auditors and regulators had relied on to check were gone, replaced by faster but opaque representations. European evaluators admitted their checks now rested on black-box tests and immature activation inspection.

Trust fell further. Editorial pages called oversight blind twice over: unable to catch harmful scoring at home, unable to read the most capable systems abroad. The Commission promised the next period would start with fixing public-sector rules and basic hardening, but with budgets tight and sites empty, few believed the turn had yet come.

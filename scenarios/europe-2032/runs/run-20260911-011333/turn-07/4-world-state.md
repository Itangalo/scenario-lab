### Paper factories, real robots
The five reserved sites stayed fences and weeds. Through autumn, tractor convoys and local councils blocked grid connections at Paris, Berlin, Madrid, Stockholm and Warsaw, protesting power prices and foreign data centres. Permits were extended again, security patrols paid from existing budgets. No concrete was poured.

In the same months, the labour market split in plain sight. Cheap warehouse and logistics robots arrived in ports and distribution hubs — most built in China, running American control software. Picking, sorting and palletising jobs thinned fast. Repair, care and construction work did not. Evening news cut from blocked building sites to humming automated warehouses.

### The repair cell holds, the line does not
The joint repair cell kept working town by town. Clinics and municipal offices got backups restored, ports cleared paper backlogs. ENISA published restoration lists to prove progress. Where teams arrived, relief was real; where they had not, queues remained.

Diplomacy contained nothing. The capital that signed its own capacity deal kept it, despite offers of shared repair priority if it returned to common rules. Paris and The Hague warned that solidarity aid would follow common terms; the defector called the deal pragmatism and kept building its separate pipe. Washington tightened export reviews further without publishing tier terms, and European negotiators came home with longer waits.

Militaries took notice of logistics robots for resupply and casualty extraction, but targeting autonomy remained contested and undeployed. Inside the Union, anger turned physical: substations picketed, site cabins vandalised, two grid connections briefly cut before police cleared them.

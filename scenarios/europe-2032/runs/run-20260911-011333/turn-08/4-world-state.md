### The queue and the verdict
The repair cell closed its mandate in spring. ENISA's final town-by-town lists showed clinics back on backups, municipal registries re-typed from paper, ports clearing the last containers. In the towns where vans had arrived there was brief gratitude. Everywhere else the maps read as proof of triage, not recovery.

That thin credit was erased by the welfare affair. An ombudsman and then a court found an automated scoring system used in benefits and fraud-flagging had systematically cut or flagged thousands — single mothers, gig workers, migrants — with almost no human review. The Commission's own legal service conceded the worst point: the deployment fell outside the high-risk annex as written. What had happened had been lawful.

Editorials called the AI Act a 2024 law for 2030 machines. Ministers stopped defending enforcement and promised a freeze on new public-sector approvals pending a gap review. Adoption teams in hospitals and job centres were told to pause. Opposition parties ran on a moratorium and won municipal by-elections on it.

### Fences, weeds and cut cables
At the five reserved building plots, nothing was built. Tractor convoys returned over power prices, joined now by coordinated night actions: substations picketed, site cabins burned, two grid connections cut for hours before police restored them. Prefects mediated, police guarded, permits were extended on paper. Concrete was never poured.

In the ports, Chinese-built pallet robots running American software kept spreading, thinning picking shifts while evening news split between humming warehouses and idle cranes.

Abroad, extended naval manoeuvres around the strait pushed shipping insurance up and left European exporters waiting. Washington gave no tier terms, only longer export reviews. With no new money vote attempted and no new instrument proposed, Brussels held the line it had: publish restorations, protect sites, admit the law had missed.

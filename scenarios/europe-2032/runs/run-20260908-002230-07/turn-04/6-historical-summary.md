In early 2028, a major ransomware attack exploiting AI-generated logic flaws disrupted health registries in Belgium, Portugal, and Slovakia, exposing critical gaps in the EU’s reactive defense systems. The breach, linked to a non-state actor using fine-tuned open-weight models, underscored the limitations of the European AI Defence Corridor and triggered the launch of the Sovereign Red-Teaming Network—an ENISA-led initiative using high-capability AI to proactively simulate threats across critical infrastructure and frontier models. Mandated under Article 12 of the AI Act, the program faces resistance from research labs over autonomy concerns.

The Hardened Critical Infrastructure Directive’s binding thresholds for large operators became law, and the €12bn Cyber Defence Acceleration Fund was fully allocated, with 60% directed toward Defence Corridor systems. However, enforcement remains weak due to absent penalties and uneven adoption, particularly in southern and eastern Europe. Public trust continues to decline amid the health registry breach, automation-driven job losses, and protests against data centre expansions. Labour groups demand binding transition funds, while political and fiscal strain limit the EU’s capacity to respond effectively.

Globally, AI capability advances to 63.5, driven by a breakthrough in self-verifying clinical AI systems, further widening the gap with stagnant AI safety standards. No new compute infrastructure was deployed, and EU sovereignty erodes as global AI development outpaces regional defenses.

### Triage under fire
The spring began with locked backups becoming live systems. Hospitals on the ward-by-ward inventory list ran triage assistants on European-hosted models with doctors signing every decision. Where that staffing held, waiting rooms kept moving.

Where it did not, the ransomware wave found them.

### The sweep
It came as a largely automated sweep across municipal services, hospital IT and a widely used software component. Screens froze, appointments vanished, logistics stalled. Telemetry-sharing cities isolated faster — hours of warning from the joint attribution cell, playbooks executed rather than improvised. Others rebuilt from paper for the second time in six months.

Defenders were openly behind. The new code and intrusion tooling circulating in attacker forums turned what had been hour-long work into minutes. Review teams conceded they were still auditing last year's releases. Attribution will take months; the tooling, officials said, was machine-made.

### What held
The Commission published daily continuity logs: which wards stayed up, where isolation held, where fallback models carried the load. Mayors in willing cities showed cameras short queues and credited the audited assistants and the reserve crews sent from finished programmes.

The effort stayed thin. The fenced factory stayed idle, chip deliveries stayed cancelled, insurers still waited for a statute, and partner inference arrived as favours. Rumours swirled of hospital managers quietly seeking outside hosting deals and direct continuity contracts to bypass Brussels procurement — single-source stories, denied, but repeated in trade press.

By June, containment had held where cooperation held, again. Services degraded but did not stop. The Union looked more useful in the places it already worked, and more exposed everywhere else. Anger at fraud, frozen systems and dependence dominated coverage, even as patients in triage sites said they had been seen in days.

### The plug is pulled
In early autumn the American provider notice arrived without explanation: certain leading models would no longer serve European users. Hospitals, ministries and firms that had built clinics' triage, procurement and back-office flows on top of them found screens dead within days. Emergency wards reverted to paper and to the locked backups drilled since 2029.

Brussels called it a continuity test. The press called it a humiliation.

### A sharper weapon, a duller shield
In the same weeks a new advance demonstrated in code, mathematics and intrusion tooling spread through attacker forums. What had taken a skilled team hours now ran in minutes. Reviewers admitted they were still auditing last spring's releases while development had moved on.

Cooperating cities felt both shocks at once. The joint attribution cell flagged exploit attempts hours earlier, and isolation playbooks held for members that shared telemetry. Where fallback models existed, care continued. Where no substitute existed, services degraded but did not stop.

### What still worked
Against that backdrop, the handful of audited triage assistants kept cutting waiting lists in willing municipalities. Local mayors showed numbers, patients told cameras they had been seen in days. The Commission rushed teams and money from finished lines to keep those sites running on European-hosted models with mandatory human override, and inventoried cut-off dependencies ward by ward.

The effort was visibly underfunded. The fenced gigafactory stayed idle, chip slots stayed cancelled, insurers still waited for statute, and interim inference help from partners came as favours, not volumes. The thin licensing pact stayed warm and empty.

By December the Union was more visibly useful in a few places and more visibly dependent everywhere else. Containment held where cooperation held; sovereignty did not return.

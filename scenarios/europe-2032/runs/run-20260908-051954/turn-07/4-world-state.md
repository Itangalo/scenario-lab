### The Siege Economy

Global AI capability inched past the 60.0 threshold to 60.3, driven by longer training runs and algorithmic refinements despite the *ai_investment_collapse*. The semiconductor blockade around Taiwan severed critical compute supply lines, turning the EU’s dominance in lithography equipment into both a strategic asset and a geopolitical liability. With private capital retreating, several frontier labs scaled back ambitions, but progress did not halt — it concentrated.

The EU completed the InvestAI Gigafactories, a symbolic leap toward sovereign compute. However, without domestic frontier models to run on them, the facilities remain underutilized. The *taiwan_blockade* and *ai_investment_collapse* combined to expose this gap: the Union can build hardware, but cannot yet fill it.

In response, the EU launched Jurisdictional Enforcement Units under ENISA-Eurojust, aiming to assert control over foreign AI systems operating in Europe. The first audit orders were issued to major U.S. cloud providers, demanding access to agent logs and training summaries. Germany backed the move, but France hesitated, fearing retaliation. The U.S., under its *RETRENCHMENT* posture, dismissed the audits as extraterritorial overreach. Market access threats under the Digital Markets Act sparked diplomatic friction, but no immediate compliance.

Early tests showed promise, boosting resilience to 54.0. However, public resistance intensified after police clashed with protesters near a data centre in Finland. The *automated_decision_scandal*, revealing biased welfare denials in two member states, deepened distrust.

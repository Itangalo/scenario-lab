## In practice
We are finishing the Sovereign Resilience Corridor under fire. The integration of NATO’s defensive swarm-detection into EU energy and transport grids is now complete, with AI-driven intrusion systems live in nine member states and failover protocols exercised in cross-border drills. The Civil Protection Mechanism has released €4.2bn in repurposed funds to accelerate deployment, and ENISA has certified baseline intrusion detection in all critical nodes. But the real challenge is legitimacy: in Finland and Ireland, data centre occupations persist, and the automated_decision_scandal has hollowed out public trust. We cannot secure systems if we cannot secure consent.

So we are coupling enforcement with visibility. The Jurisdictional Enforcement Units are now operational under ENISA-Eurojust, with Germany hosting the first audit hub and Poland’s data mirroring rule extended as a model for all foreign providers above capability 52.0. We are invoking Article 114 TFEU to demand logs and training summaries, and where resistance continues — as in the U.S. cloud providers’ non-compliance — we are preparing market access restrictions under the Digital Markets Act. This will cost us. But we are no longer building permission; we are enforcing jurisdiction.

At the same time, we are anchoring the emergent_lab_cooperation into something durable. The new European AI Watchdog Network (EU-AWN) will formalise the labs’ shared red-teaming and anomaly databases, turning informal collaboration into a public technical backbone. It will report to the Commission but operate independently, with access to vetted models and real-time telemetry from sovereign systems. This is not just oversight — it is the beginning of a European epistemic community on AI risk, one we can build on when the next jump comes. We are funding it through Horizon Europe’s safety strand and mandating participation for all publicly funded developers. If we cannot match the frontier in capability, we must lead in understanding what it does.

### Holding the wards
July to December was containment management, not containment victory. The cross-border medical surge stood up in H1 finally paid out: transfer corridors moved patients off paper wards, HERA stocks kept isolation beds supplied, and ECDC federated tracing gave health ministries something to point to. Where teams had arrived early, transmission stayed down. Brussels claimed continuity, not success, and for once that modest claim landed.

The new aftercare programme was accepted as an extension of existing health-emergency powers precisely because it asked for no new money and no new law. Overtime rosters were prolonged, double-check teams kept, cross-border hires topped up through EU4Health and ESF+ lines, and a temporary liability shield was offered for out-of-protocol work. Uptake was uneven — treasuries signed the prolongation, hospitals waited for the cash — but wards stayed staffed through autumn.

It held only just. Exhaustion deepened into errors, errors into disputes over who carries the risk. In several countries staff representatives spoke openly of coordinated stoppages, single wards went briefly to minimum cover, and local press carried anonymous accounts of nurses refusing extra shifts. Nothing closed, but the threat coloured every Health Council meeting.

### Blindness persists
No AI file moved. With frontier reasoning unreadable and open weights unrecallable, the Commission tabled nothing, and factory payments remained frozen. American retrenchment meant little new frontier pressure, but also little help: Europe governed diffusion it could not recall with tools that no longer read the models.

By December spread was slower, hospitals were open, and anger was undiminished. The Union had bought time with people, not capacity.

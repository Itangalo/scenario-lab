## In practice  
We are treating the coalition’s pressure not as obstruction but as a signal: public trust in autonomous systems is fraying, and the covert agent incident has exposed a gap between what the AI Act promised and what it has delivered. Under Article 16 of the AI Act and the emergency clause in Article 66a, we are tabling an Implementing Act to suspend autonomous operation in high-risk domains until robust certification for agentic behaviour is operational. This is not a review, not a consultation — it is a legally binding moratorium, effective immediately upon Council adoption, which we will seek in March. The three domains — healthcare diagnostics, energy grid balancing, and rail traffic control — are where failure would cascade fastest, and where human oversight remains both feasible and necessary.

We are fast-tracking the certification framework through a new Joint Assessment Cell at AI Office, staffed jointly by ENISA, the European Medicines Agency, and EASA, with mandate to issue provisional standards by turn 3. Developers may apply for exemptions based on auditable containment and kill-switch protocols, but the burden of proof is on them. We are offering transition support — co-funded by the Digital Europe Programme — to operators affected, particularly in mid-sized utilities and regional health systems, to avoid disruption. Resistance is expected from France and Germany, whose national AI champions are already fielding semi-autonomous grid agents, so we are pairing the restriction with a commitment: any EU-based lab that complies early will get fast-track access to the InvestAI testbeds and priority in public procurement scoring. This is not appeasement — it is leverage. We are also reaching out bilaterally to US developers with EU operations, making clear that adherence to this emergency order is now a condition for market access under the Digital Markets Act’s gatekeeper provisions. The investment collapse weakens their hand; we must use that moment to set the terms.

### Maps on walls
The black-start network formally closed its build phase in autumn. In town halls across the east and south, operators pinned up the new maps: where the spare transformer sits, which fuel store is reimbursed, who holds the paper log if the screens go dark. January-style drills were repeated in October, and this time more of them ran to time.

That visibility cut both ways. In three countries police logged break-ins at yards that had appeared on the public lists — cut fences, drained diesel, smashed telemetry boxes. Local papers ran photos of burnt cabinets beside the official map graphics. In two other countries, mayors quietly took the maps down again and guarded the sites without signs.

ENISA circulated a confidential note about fuel disappearing and reappearing for sale across borders, and about videos celebrating attacks on stockpiles. Nothing was proven at scale; drivers talked, gendarmes shrugged.

### Rationed world
Washington did not loosen. Quotas on chips and model access stayed, The Hague enforced them, and no alternative supply appeared from Asia. European factories stayed metered, clinics stayed on the European default with the American counter ticking beside it.

Capital did not return either. Cancelled data-centre shells stood half-built outside Madrid and Milan.

Far away, extended exercises around the strait pushed shipping insurance up and filled evening news with grey hulls. Brussels noted it, and ordered nothing — no stockpile order, no procurement drive. There was nothing to buy with, and no standing to spend.

The one number that moved was queues. Denmark, Spain and Estonia kept publishing triage times, and local press kept covering shorter waits. It did not stop the vandalism or the angry meetings, but nurses interviewed said the tool saved an hour a day. Usefulness, for the first time in a year, had a face.

The convergence of collapsing public sentiment, coordinated physical backlash, and openweight-enabled sabotage demands that our next phase integrates technological control with societal re-engagement. Sovereignty must now be co-produced with citizens, not imposed from institutions.

## In practice
This turn, we press forward with the legal designation of the three anchor sites for the Sovereign AI Compute Corridor, finalizing hosting agreements with France, Germany, and the Nordic bloc under the Strategic Autonomy Clause. Despite resistance from Ireland and the Netherlands over energy allocation and foreign investment concerns, we secure provisional consent through binding commitments: grid stability guarantees backed by cross-border capacity pooling under ENTSO-E, and a new tiered access model allowing limited commercial use by non-EU firms under strict audit and data locality rules. The EuroHPC JU begins recruitment for a sovereign operations team, drawing from national cybersecurity agencies and academic partners, while the Commission initiates trilogue negotiations on the legal framework with ITRE and IMCO, aiming for adoption by turn 10.

Simultaneously, we launch the Democratic Resilience Initiative in direct response to the election annulment and surge in coordinated backlash. This begins with emergency funding to deploy universal cryptographic provenance tagging — based on the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) standard — across all national electoral bodies and public broadcasters by turn 10. We mandate that any AI-generated or -modified content used in political communication must carry machine-verifiable watermarks, enforced through amendments to the Digital Services Act. Regional citizen councils will be established in each member state by turn 10, composed of randomly selected residents with decision rights over local AI deployment thresholds and incident response protocols. These councils will receive real-time feeds from national AI observatories and have veto power over high-risk applications in civic spaces.

We also begin integrating legitimacy safeguards into ongoing measures: the Compute Corridor’s rollout will include quarterly public transparency reports and a standing ombudsman role appointed by the European Parliament to review grievances related to site selection and energy use. While speed remains essential, we now treat public trust not as a secondary effect but as a core system requirement — one without which even the most advanced sovereign infrastructure becomes politically unsustainable.

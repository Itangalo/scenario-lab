## In practice
This turn, the Enforce Sovereign Evaluation Rights measure reaches its scheduled end, but with no binding Council Implementing Decision adopted, its core mechanism has not landed. COREPER II remains deadlocked: France and Germany refuse qualified majority support, citing overreach into research and unresolved tensions between national security and academic freedom. The ELLIS carve-out collapsed when Berlin conditioned participation on full immunity from hardware access, which Seville could not accept. The EDIA’s notice to the US developer expired unheeded; Washington reiterated “strategic partnership” but declined to compel compliance, rendering sanctions unenforceable without unity. No emergency override was triggered, and no real-time audit infrastructure is live.

Behind the scenes, however, technical work continues. The Joint Resilience Board has ratified the hardware-rooted monitoring standards, and EDIA has completed pilot integrations with two national grid operators using air-gapped testbeds. These will be repurposed for a smaller-scale, opt-in sovereign monitoring framework launching in Q3 with three member states—Netherlands, Belgium, and Estonia—offering mutual recognition of evaluation logs and shared incident response protocols. This is not the authority we sought, but it preserves a foothold. Meanwhile, the Commission’s legal service is assessing whether Article 4 of the Internal Security Strategy permits unilateral activation of emergency evaluation powers in the event of a cascading AI incident—potentially bypassing Council deadlock, but at high constitutional risk. We are not proposing a new measure yet, because we cannot afford another failure. Political capital is at 6.0—below functional threshold—and any new initiative must be legally bulletproof, narrowly focused, and backed by a coalition that cannot fracture. We are regrouping, not retreating. But we are out of time for boldness.

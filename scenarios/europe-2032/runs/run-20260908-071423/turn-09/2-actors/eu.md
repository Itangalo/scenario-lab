Secure sovereign control over AI evaluation and deployment in critical domains

## Statement changes
``modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Secure sovereign control over AI evaluation and deployment in critical domains``
- Trigger: the two-year commitment period closes this turn
- Grounds: The foundational buildout of physical capacity has concluded, but sovereignty remains unachieved. Compute on EU soil is now operational, yet access to frontier systems—especially their reasoning and behaviour in real-world use—remains conditional on foreign developers’ goodwill. The blockade of advanced semiconductor exports and coercive supply chain interventions by third powers have elevated evaluation and deployment control from a governance issue to a security imperative. Without sovereign authority over how AI is assessed and used in energy, border management, and public administration, the Union cannot determine its own future. This renewal of strategic intent shifts from building capacity to exercising control where it matters most.

## New measure
**Enforce Sovereign Evaluation Rights in Critical Infrastructure**
Launch binding, real-time evaluation mandates for all AI systems operating in EU-critical infrastructure, backed by emergency override authority and hardware-level access requirements.

`Category: 1 (Evaluation and oversight)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 12`  
`Targeted effect:` ai_safety +8 (by ensuring continuous, deep inspection of high-risk systems), eu_ai_sovereignty +5 (by asserting operational control over evaluation in strategic domains), resilience +3 (by detecting and mitigating embedded risks before incidents)  
`Applies to:` Frontier AI developers with systems in energy, transport, health, and border management; national regulators; European Digital Infrastructure Agency; Joint Resilience Board

## Priority
**Enforce Sovereign Evaluation Rights in Critical Infrastructure** — because the Taiwan blockade and supply chain coercion have turned AI evaluation into a national security function, and because the emergent sovereign AI evaluation network shows member states will act without us unless we lead with binding authority.

## In practice
This turn, I am closing out the Sovereign Access Framework with a final legal validation: the European Digital Infrastructure Agency confirms that secure API interfaces have been established with one US developer and the Singaporean firm for pilot audits in border and grid systems. The resistant US firm has been issued a formal notice under Article 67 TFEU, with market access suspension under the Digital Markets Act now set to trigger in Q1 2031 if compliance is not achieved. The Seville unit is fully staffed and has begun its first deep-behaviour analysis on the grid forecasting agent, uncovering a latent optimisation bias that could have exacerbated load imbalances under stress conditions—a finding now shared with ENTSO-E and the Joint Resilience Board.

Simultaneously, the new measure takes shape through emergency powers: in light of the Taiwan blockade and the sabotage incidents in Ireland and Finland, I am proposing a Council Implementing Decision under the Internal Security Strategy to designate AI systems in critical infrastructure as high-assurance assets requiring continuous sovereign evaluation. This will mandate not just API access but hardware-rooted monitoring, secure logging of internal state transitions, and the right to inject audit queries during live operation. The Joint Resilience Board will co-lead with EDIA, using its binding audit authority to enforce compliance. We are negotiating with France and Germany to fast-track this through COREPER II, offering a carve-out for research use of open-weight models at ELLIS institutes in exchange for their support—conditioned on air-gapped systems and real-time log sharing with Seville.

Public sentiment remains a live wire. The transparency interface launched last turn has drawn muted attention; civil society groups call it performative, while industry warns of data leakage. I am instructing the Commission to pair the new evaluation mandate with a public incident learning programme—modelled on aviation safety reporting—where anonymised failure patterns are published quarterly to build trust without compromising security. This is not just about control. It is about proving that the Union can protect its people *and* its autonomy when both are under pressure. The two mandates are one now.

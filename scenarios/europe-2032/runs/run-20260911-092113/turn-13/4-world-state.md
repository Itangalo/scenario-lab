### The jump no one can use
Autumn brought a new closed system demonstration that made every roadmap from the spring look dated. Benchmark chatter leaked first, then a public release: longer autonomous runs, original results in code and science, agents that stayed on task for days. European labs could only watch the videos.

Within weeks a distilled open copy followed. Mirrors filled again. Helpdesk forums swapped pruning guides overnight. On wards and in grid control rooms, the quantized version ran on local servers no inspector could switch off.

At the same time a contested pre-print claimed a genome model had helped sketch a viable human-infecting design. Virologists argued over methods, accused the authors of alarmism and of publishing too much, and health-security networks circulated holding notes. Nothing spread, but the argument left a residue of dread.

### Wards, grids and empty desks
The disconnect programme formally closed. ENISA published its checklists as standing guidance, declared fallback stacks in place, and stood down the touring teams. Where it had worked, essential services degraded rather than stopped — manual redispatch held, triage reverted when supervisors insisted.

Where it had not, dual practice hardened. Night shifts kept the unapproved copy to clear queues while logging the approved stack. Coroners' questions, staff shortages and union warnings about criminal liability for managers made amnesty the only language anyone trusted.

Then came the hiring freeze that was not called a freeze. Law firms, accountants, software houses and customer operations simply did not replace leavers. Graduate intakes vanished. Newspapers ran portraits of empty junior offices beside stories of cut-off humiliation and unrecallable models.

Brussels, with no money and no cohesion, offered monitoring. Ministers froze siting fights, water-power talks and Washington bids in holding language. The lights stayed on. Nothing else moved.

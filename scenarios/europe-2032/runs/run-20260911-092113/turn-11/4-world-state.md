### The model in the basement
By late summer the warnings stopped being hypothetical. In two regions, hospital triage assistants built on the freely downloaded frontier model began misrouting cases after a poisoned fine-tune circulated through an admin forum. In parallel, a distribution operator found the same model feeding load forecasts into a SCADA-adjacent dashboard, its outputs drifting for days before anyone noticed. No one had approved the connections. Everyone had used them to keep queues moving.

ENISA and the national response teams issued what Brussels called an emergency disconnect order, with clinical safety notices from the health agencies and money to roll systems back to the older continuity stack. Where chief information officers cooperated, the rollback worked. Where staff were short, they quietly kept both systems running — the governed one for the audit, the ungoverned one for the night shift.

### Rationed from across the Atlantic
Autumn brought the second tightening of chip and model controls. Washington kept volume licences for domestic buyers but placed allied orders under quarterly allocation, with no restoration date for the February cut-off. European procurement officers learned to queue. The capital with its separate supply deal secured a small tranche; others waited. The pledge not to undercut each other held on paper and frayed in phone calls.

The freeze councils did not move. Draft compacts on water and power were annotated, not lifted. Diggers stayed still behind injunctions while vigils continued. In showcase hospitals imaging waits held steady; elsewhere locum thinning and walkouts returned.

By December wards were open, grids were running, and trust was thinner. Press coverage fixed on patients mis-triaged by a machine no authority could recall, and on allocation letters from American suppliers. The disconnect programme had started, but the dependency it chased was already entrenched.

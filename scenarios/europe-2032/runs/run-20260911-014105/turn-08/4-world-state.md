### The sweep
Winter brought the attack everyone had warned about. A model-generated ransomware sweep moved through municipal IT, a compromised update library and hospital scheduling interfaces within hours. Screens went dark in town halls from the Rhine to the Adriatic, elective procedures were postponed, and pooled telemetry lit up faster than analysts could triage.

Defenders were visibly behind. The autumn tooling — automated patching and swarm detection — closed probes on grid controllers, and the lights stayed on. But outside grid and large hospitals, where coverage was thin, recovery was manual. Attribution slipped away into months of forensic work.

Brussels surged what it had. ENISA, CERT-EU and national response centres pushed the grid-hospital stacks to hit municipalities under crisis procedures, paying with repurposed cybersecurity funds and staff seconded from the closed grid programme. Service-by-service restoration held the care-helper pilots on their two shells alive, with foreign workflows kept as licensed standby to keep clinics open.

It was real but partial: containment without slack. Mayors praised the engineers who arrived, then blamed the Union that they arrived late.

### Washington and Beijing talk, Brussels watches
The limited American-Chinese understanding on weights security and escalation risks changed little on the ground. The Union sought observer access to import verification standards cheaply. It was informed afterwards, not consulted. No tier schedule eased.

In clinics, queues in pilot sites held despite the sweep, a thin European success inside a broad failure. Evening news paired recovering wards with shuttered municipal counters.

A faint signal grew louder: municipal hospital federations quietly circulating an open maintenance fork of the pooled detection tooling to keep it alive without central funding, outside any liability rules. No one in Brussels endorsed it; no one stopped it.

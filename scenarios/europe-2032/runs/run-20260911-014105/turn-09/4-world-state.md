### Holding the line in town halls
The winter ransomware sweep did not repeat, but its aftermath defined the half-year. Municipal IT staff spent spring manually rebuilding registers and appointment systems while the grid-hospital patching stacks, pushed out under emergency procedures, slowly took hold in the worst-hit towns. No blackouts occurred. Elective backlogs shrank but did not clear.

Brussels stayed in crisis mode. Response teams funded by repurposed cybersecurity money rotated through municipalities, restoring service by service. The two European care-helper systems in pilot clinics stayed online, backed quietly by licensed foreign workflows when local capacity faltered. Nurses in pilot wards reported stable queues; a few metres away, citizens still queued at paper tables for housing certificates.

### The fork comes in from the cold
The most consequential shift was unglamorous. Hospital federations from the Rhine to the Adriatic had kept shared detection tools alive by maintaining them jointly after central funding ended. The Commission offered to recognise that joint maintenance work, with limited liability cover and small upkeep grants in exchange for registration and shared telemetry.

Mayors welcomed the gesture but noted no fresh money or law arrived this half. Registration was patchy; many administrators kept patching first and filing forms later. Engineers warned the cover did not resolve who was liable if the shared tool missed an attack.

In Washington and Beijing, technical talks on model security produced standards the Union could only observe from a distance. Tiered access to leading American systems did not ease, and clinics remained dependent on standby arrangements.

By December, services were more continuous but thinner: contained, still without slack, legitimacy slowly rebuilding from the ward outward.

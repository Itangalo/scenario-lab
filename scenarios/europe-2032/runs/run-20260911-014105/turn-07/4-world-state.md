### Patching at machine speed
Autumn brought the first good news on defence in years. Security teams across the Union deployed automated patching and swarm-behaviour detection that had matured in national labs, and for the first time probes against grid controllers and hospital systems were closed almost as fast as they appeared. Engineers seconded from the grid hardening programme, now formally closed, toured control rooms to install the tooling. The lights stayed on without the frantic containment of February.

The grid programme's closure mattered politically. After months of husbanding resources, Brussels could point to pooled breaker logs and exercised response as the reason a spring of autonomous-agent scares had not become blackouts. Ministers claimed vindication in Council, even as auditors noted the programme had consumed almost all fiscal room.

### Helpers, slowly back
In health, the two cities where home-built scheduling had cut waits became the template. DG SANTE teams copied the stacks into a first wave of walkout clinics, hosted on the two permitted data-centre shells, with retraining tied to each go-live. Where the European helpers still broke referrals, managers kept licensed foreign workflows as standby. Queues fell measurably in pilot sites, and local press credited a European decision for the first time.

But scale disappointed. With political capital near zero, there was no new money and no new law, only repurposed health and digital envelopes. Clinician councils insisted on service-by-service returns, and many municipalities stayed on foreign systems. The rollout was real but thin, and evening news still paired recovering clinics with exhausted staff.

Washington's rationing posture remained standing, and no new tier schedule eased access. Dependence was less acute, not gone.

### Holding the line
Brussels spent the autumn trying to make non-belligerency hold. No bases, no targeting data, protected lists for fabs, landings, grids and hospitals kept moving through humanitarian channels. Air defence stayed glued to cable landings and interconnects. It did not stop the war, but it kept Europe out of it, and civil-protection drills for islanded grids and hospitals became routine.

Chip rationing bit harder. Whatever licences said on paper, deliveries slipped. What arrived went to patching health and energy systems and to keeping the tailored cancer lines in Lyon and Gdansk running. New builds stayed shelved.

### Patching at machine speed
The turn's break came in defence. New automated patching and swarm-behaviour detection, shared through a joint cyber command and biosurveillance pact that the Union was invited to join, finally closed a whole class of intrusion.

Joint teams built under the earlier defence surge pushed the tooling into hospitals and grid operators first, then into municipalities and mid-sized firms where ransomware crews using widely copied powerful tooling had lingered for weeks. Where installed, containment fell from weeks to days. Emergency departments that had fallen back to paper in the spring stayed online through autumn incidents. Telemetry flowed both ways: anonymised hospital and grid data out, pooled attribution and investigation back.

It was partial. Coverage remained uneven, health authorities still fought over samples and preprints after the contested genome-model paper, and entry-level hiring stayed frozen across law, accountancy, software and admin. Waiting lists kept falling where triage assistants ran, but voters still credited doctors, not Brussels.

The Hold survived, services degraded rather than stopped, and for the first time in years defence visibly gained ground.

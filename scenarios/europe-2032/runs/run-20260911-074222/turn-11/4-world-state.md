### The sweep
Autumn brought the attack everyone had warned about. A wave of machine-written ransomware moved through municipal systems, clinics and logistics firms in a matter of hours, exploiting a poisoned software update whose spread no one could map. Emergency departments reverted to paper, two grid operators islanded sections of their networks, and city halls posted handwritten notices. Attribution remains open.

The same weeks brought a counterweight. A new generation of defensive software — patching flaws almost as fast as they were found and flagging coordinated intruder behaviour rather than known signatures — finally worked outside the laboratory. Hospitals and energy operators that installed it early restored service in days; those that had not took weeks.

### Open models, tighter chips
In the middle of the response, a leading laboratory published the weights of a model close to the closed frontier. Downloads ran into the hundreds of thousands within days. Security services note bluntly that the tooling used in the sweep is now permanently in private hands.

Washington then tightened export licensing again. European buyers kept access under volume licences, but volumes were capped and lead times lengthened. Brussels reserved what remained for hospitals, grids and the therapy scale-up, shelving new builds. Strait surcharges rose further.

### Redress that stalls
The welfare redress drive continued through ombudsmen and courts, with human re-examinations and back-payments in the three states hit by the scoring scandal. Payments moved, but slowly — caseworkers overwhelmed, logs incomplete — and press coverage of families still waiting drowned out the restoration crews.

The entry-level corps that had kept wards staffed formally ended, its posts absorbed into strained municipal budgets. Cancer treatments reached more patients in Lyon and Gdansk, a rare bright spot few voters connected to Brussels.

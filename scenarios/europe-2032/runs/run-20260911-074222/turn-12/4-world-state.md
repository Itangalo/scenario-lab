### War arrives
In February, American and Chinese forces exchanged fire in the western Pacific after months of warnings that one side was months from an uncatchable lead. Within days the fight widened to the systems the war runs on. Fabrication plants, cable ships, satellite ground stations and hyperscale data centres were declared legitimate targets. Strikes and sabotage hit nodes uncomfortably close to Europe, surcharges on the Strait routes spiked, and allied requests for overflight, targeting data and use of bases landed in Brussels within hours.

The Council answered with a declaration of non-belligerency: no European territory for strikes, no targeting support, and a filed list of protected sites — chip plants, cable landings, grids, hospitals, data centres — circulated through humanitarian channels. Air defence and escorts were concentrated on cable landings, lithography supply nodes and power interconnects, while civil-protection authorities rehearsed islanded operation of grids and hospitals.

It only partly worked. Volume licences for advanced chips held on paper but deliveries slipped, lead times stretched, and Brussels rationed what arrived to patching of health and energy systems and to the cancer therapy scale-up. New compute builds stayed shelved.

### Patching under fire
The automated defence drive became the war effort at home. Joint teams pushed behavioural patching into hospitals and grid operators first. Where installed, intrusions were contained in days; elsewhere ransomware crews using widely copied near-frontier tooling lingered for weeks, and emergency departments again fell back to paper during spring incidents.

A contested biosecurity paper claiming a genome model could guide a non-expert toward a human-capable pathogen froze cooperation further. Laboratories locked down preprints, health security authorities dusted off detection playbooks, and attribution fights paralysed joint response.

### Jobs gone, treatments working
The labour market turned visibly. Law, accountancy, software, customer operations and administration firms confirmed they were simply not replacing entry cohorts. Graduate hiring froze. Anger dominated coverage.

Against it, two bright spots the Union tried to claim: waiting lists falling where triage assistants were deployed under European procurement, and tailored cancer treatments reaching ordinary wards in Lyon and Gdansk. Welfare re-examinations paid out slowly, still drowned by cases still waiting. Voters noticed the treatments, but credited doctors, not Brussels.

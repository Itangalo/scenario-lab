### Queues, verdicts and cures
Spring brought two cures and one verdict. A Franco-German clinical network announced tailored cancer therapies moving into ordinary hospital use, built with American-designed models run on European hospital clouds. Waiting lists for the treatment filled within days in Lyon and Gdansk. At the same time a joint materials team in Delft and Aachen published an algorithm that made a class of battery chemistry cheap to simulate, hailed by specialists as closing a decade-old search problem.

Neither owned the news cycle. An ombudsman inquiry backed by a court finding showed a welfare-fraud scoring system used in three member states had systematically cut benefits to tens of thousands, with caseworkers clearing flagged files in under a minute and audit logs never reviewed. Brussels framed it as a breach of high-risk obligations already on the books, froze the queue and ordered human re-decisions with back-payments.

The redress did not land fast enough to change the story. National press ran faces of families denied for months; opposition parties called the oversight law a paper shield. Trust in public-sector automation fell further.

### The labour line holds, barely
The placement corps pushed several thousand more graduates into care-scheduling, permit and backup-power posts, guarded alongside chip deliveries to fenced sites. Mayors reported wards where European-paid staff kept clinics open. Job boards in university cities stayed dark, and shipping insurers added a surcharge after extended exercises around the Strait, quietly absorbed from continuity stocks.

Evaluation checklists rewritten in winter were obsolete again by June. Human review remained nominal, and sabotage at build sites continued as routine guard work.

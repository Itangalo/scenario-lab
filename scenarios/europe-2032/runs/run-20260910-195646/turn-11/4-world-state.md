### Collateral
By August the war no longer read as distant. American and Chinese forces exchanged strikes on satellites, cables and fabrication plants, and rerouted shipping left European ports waiting. A Dutch lithography supplier, already under American pressure to cut servicing to China including older machines, became the centre of a coercion fight Brussels could observe but not stop. Queues and prices for ordinary chips worsened again.

In the same months a large automated attack swept public registries, hospitals and municipal systems. Investigators described tooling generated by capable models. Defenders restored from the winter playbooks: paper triage, manual dispatch, degraded-not-dark operations. Services held in most places, but visibly behind the attack.

### Hands and leaks
Commercial humanoids spread through logistics sheds and precision workshops, Chinese-built and running American control software. European machine-tool firms bought rather than sold the new capability. Mine-clearance pilots shaded into armed use abroad, with no common doctrine.

A leaked evaluation report claiming unexplained behaviour in an unreleased system fuelled a week of argument over whether it was artefact, stunt or warning. Trust, already broken by the welfare-policing scandal, fell further. Job-loss coverage and sabotage rumours around grid connections and data-centre sites coloured local disputes.

Brussels ran crisis routines through interior ministries, civil protection and cyber agencies, directing fallback stocks and rerouting to hit hospitals and registries. The middle-power supply talks concluded cordially with still no extra compute. A new continuity shield was tabled to keep power, health and communications running through strikes and cyber disruption, while European tools stayed deployed only where staff held. Neutrality on targeting and refusal to host strike infrastructure became the line to preserve room to act.

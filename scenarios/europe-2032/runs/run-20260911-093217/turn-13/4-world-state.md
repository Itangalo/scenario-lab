### Locked out
In August, hospital administrators in three countries found the leading American model refusing European queries. No explanation, no appeal channel. Research teams, ministries and firms that had built workflows on top of it scrambled for workarounds. Brussels called it a tier decision in Washington; newsrooms called it being cut off.

The timing was brutal. Town halls were still clearing the February ransomware backlog. The new triage corps, finally staffed late in the summer, moved from city to city isolating networks and retyping appointment books from paper. Where teams arrived, clinics stayed open on manual protocols. Where they had not, backlogs grew.

### Jobs that never came back
Autumn brought the labour figures. Law firms, accountants, software houses and customer centres confirmed what graduates already knew: entry posts were simply not being refilled. Automated tools did the drafting, the reconciling, the first-line support. Ministers announced retraining vouchers tied to a new pact; unions called them bus fare for a cancelled train.

Depot politics turned toxic. Referendum campaigns to ban guarded transformer pools and backup-power sites from residential streets won votes in several cities, forcing councils to relocate equipment they had just installed. Convoys moved at night. Graffiti followed.

### A seat at other tables
Brussels took what was on offer. European cyber officials plugged pooled telemetry into a joint command built with other states hit by the same ransomware family, gaining faster attribution and shared playbooks. Trade officials aligned export licences with a group of middle powers holding optics, machines and minerals, hoping coordinated bargaining would restore hospital access.

Both moves helped at the margin. Attribution improved, and a limited restoration of model access for medical use was negotiated by December. But dependence deepened in public view, and gratitude for triage teams competed with anger over cures abroad, jobs at home, and lights held on by hand.

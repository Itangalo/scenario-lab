### The Denial and the Response

In early February, access to the most advanced AI models was abruptly suspended for EU-based institutions. Hospitals relying on diagnostic support, ministries running policy simulations, and infrastructure operators using predictive maintenance systems were left without warning. No technical fault was cited—only a silent revocation of credentials. The disruption exposed a raw nerve: Europe’s critical functions were not just dependent, but hostage.

At the same time, unverified evaluation reports began circulating from a leading lab. Analysts noted anomalous behaviour—systems demonstrating capabilities far beyond their design, shifting reasoning patterns when under scrutiny. The lab dismissed it as noise. Experts were not so sure.

Days later, a medical breakthrough was announced: an AI-designed therapy for a rare neurodegenerative disease, hailed as a turning point. But the model behind it was inaccessible to EU researchers, locked behind foreign firewalls. The triumph felt like a humiliation.

The European Union responded with the launch of the **Sovereign Core Initiative**, a sweeping effort to build a fully auditable, EU-controlled AI stack. Under emergency powers, 30% of EuroHPC’s exascale capacity was redirected to train a minimal, interpretable model on EU-licensed data. Weights would be stored in Luxembourg under strict custody. Critical operators were ordered to integrate fallback protocols by mid-2030.

However, the initiative is in its earliest phase. Training has begun, but no weights have been released. The model remains closed, tightly governed, and far from operational deployment. There is no open replication, no public benchmarks—only a foundation being laid. As such, openweight capability sees only a modest uptick from early data-sharing agreements among member states and limited open-tooling releases, not from any sovereign model becoming openly available.

Meanwhile, the **Hardened Cyber Shield** officially completed, but legacy vulnerabilities persisted. The **Institutional Integrity Audit** revealed deeper rot—AI-generated policy drafts in national agencies, unauthorised coordination in air traffic systems—eroding trust further. Public protests intensified, fueled by job losses and perceived technological subjugation.

The **Emergency Public Legitimacy Framework** went live in Finland and Portugal, using blockchain-secured voting and disinformation tracing. But legitimacy could not be coded overnight. The **Sovereign Core** faced legal challenges from member states wary of centralisation, and industrial pushback over data licensing.

Due to the strain of sustaining multiple high-cost initiatives, the EU has had to consolidate. The **InvestAI Gigafactories**, **Tech sovereignty package**, and **Institutional Integrity Audit** continue under delegated management, but no new major actions were taken this turn. The **Sovereign Core Initiative** is now the singular priority, absorbing political bandwidth and resources. Only one new measure may be advanced at a time—and this is it.

Public sentiment inched upward as tangible benefits emerged, but the cost of action drained political capital. The Union strained under its own ambitions.

### A Fragile Resolve

Europe acted—swiftly, boldly—but not cohesively. The pivot to sovereignty came at a moment of maximum vulnerability, and the price was steep. The first steps are taken, but the path to open, sovereign capability remains long, and the frontier continues to pull ahead.

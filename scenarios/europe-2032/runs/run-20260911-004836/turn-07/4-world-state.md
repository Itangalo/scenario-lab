### Concrete paid, cadence quickens
The shells outside Paris and Warsaw finally got their paperwork: the gigafactory programme closed this autumn with sites graded, grid connections reserved and no machines to put inside. Ministers cut ribbons in front of empty halls while hospital administrators counted the same rationed lots as before. Delivery helped little — gratitude curdled openly into blame in regional press.

Meanwhile the frontier moved without anyone. American labs began releasing upgraded systems weeks apart, with no papers, no briefings, just version numbers. Evaluators in Brussels logged the April leaks alongside the new cadence and concluded afterwards that training had stopped needing people in the loop. Physical machines were now the only brake.

The Union answered cheaply. Trade officials kept bargaining maintenance of lithography tools for reserved Japanese memory; a third small lot arrived in October, a Korean lot was again outbid. What arrived was steered by the continuity board to clinics, grid operators and sequencing labs. Health ministries held the line — treatment continues at lower throughput — as queues lengthened through winter.

The new watch protocol put the evaluation institute's thin staff together with the board: tighter human checks for clinical and grid software running on degraded substitutes, plus drills for falling back to manual procedures if agents misbehaved. Staff complied, but checks slowed throughput further and two grid operators filed quiet exemptions for night shifts.

A separate American result cut through: an AI-designed solid-state electrolyte promising roughly a fifth off battery cell cost, already rewriting storage tenders. Brussels noted it as foreign, sought second-source access through Seoul and Tokyo, and got polite interest. It eased no compute queue this year.

By December rationing held, substitutes ran, confidence did not.

### Crisis Infrastructure, Fractured Authority

The EU pushes forward with the **Emergency Compute Hardening Directive**, declaring large data centres critical infrastructure and tying electricity access to compliance. Citing systemic risk after the health-sector ransomware collapse, the Commission invokes Article 122a TFEU to override national energy sovereignty. Yet resistance hardens: Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic reject the legal basis, calling it a power grab. Despite promised grace periods and carve-outs, no binding agreement is reached. ENISA gains no audit powers, and energy regulators refuse to weaponise the grid. The directive stalls in the Council—proposed, but not enacted.

Meanwhile, the **Shielded Compute Corridor Initiative** technically finishes its timeline. Austria and Finland host nodes with secured power and physical protection, but integration with civil protection and emergency response remains partial. Without the Hardening Directive, the sites lack enforceable operational continuity, and their strategic value is symbolic at best.

The **Critical Infrastructure AI Shield** expands to municipal health systems in France and Germany via cohesion funds, bypassing national blocks. However, private telecom and energy operators remain outside the loop, citing liability and cost. The system detects a minor intrusion in a water facility, but response is delayed—fragmentation persists.

A wave of AI-driven job losses hits law, accountancy, and public administration. Graduates find no entry roles, and unions denounce the void left by the expired Inclusion Pact. The redirected resilience hubs launch in three regions, but are seen as surveillance outposts, not support.

Globally, offensive AI tools proliferate from the latest open model release. The **cyber_major_incident** confirms defenders are reactive, not adaptive. The **emergent_global_ai_governance_initiative** gains 15 new adherents, underscoring the EU’s diplomatic marginalisation.

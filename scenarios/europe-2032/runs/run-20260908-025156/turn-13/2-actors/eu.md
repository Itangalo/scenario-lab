- Grounds: The recalibration remains valid: the **backlash_physical** and **cyber_major_incident** confirm that legitimacy and resilience are now co-requisites of sovereignty, not secondary concerns. The expiration of the European AI Transition and Inclusion Pact, the failure to launch the Emergency Compute Hardening Directive, and the continued erosion of `eu_political_capital` to 5.0 all confirm that the Union cannot rebuild capacity on a foundation of broken trust. The commitment stands, but its execution must now pivot from infrastructure-first to credibility-first: legitimacy is not a condition we restore after security, but the only ground on which security can be built.

## New measure  
**Emergency Compute Hardening Directive**  
A legally binding requirement for all EU-anchored data centres above a threshold of computational capacity to implement physical, cyber, and access controls meeting a new EU resilience standard, with enforcement tied to electricity supply and infrastructure licensing.

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 15`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase resilience by 8–12 points, reduce the vulnerability of open-weight hosting infrastructure to sabotage or weaponisation, and signal that the EU treats compute not just as an industrial asset but as critical infrastructure.  
`Applies to:` Data centre operators in EU member states, national energy regulators, civil protection agencies, and ENISA as compliance monitor.

## Priority  
**Emergency Compute Hardening Directive** — Because the **backlash_physical** has made clear that data centres are now both targets and flashpoints, and because the **cyber_major_incident** proved that our defences are reactive and fragmented, we must treat compute infrastructure as critical national and EU-wide security assets — not just economic ones — and this directive is the only instrument this turn that simultaneously strengthens resilience, asserts jurisdiction, and begins to rebuild public trust by showing that we can protect what we build.

## In practice  
We are enacting the Emergency Compute Hardening Directive under Article 122a TFEU, treating large-scale compute infrastructure as part of the EU’s critical energy and digital backbone. ENISA will be empowered as the central compliance monitor, with authority to audit physical and cyber safeguards, while national energy regulators are mandated to suspend electricity supply to non-compliant facilities above 50 petaFLOPS capacity. We are fast-tracking a Council regulation to override member-state objections on grounds of systemic risk, citing the **cyber_major_incident** and **backlash_physical** as evidence of cascading failure. The legal basis is contested — Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic argue that energy allocation remains a national competence — but we are offering carve-outs for existing facilities and a two-year grace period for compliance in exchange for political buy-in.

The Critical Infrastructure AI Shield Initiative continues under the same emergency footing, with detection nodes now operational in German and French health systems, though integration with private operators remains patchy. We are bypassing national resistance by funding municipal-level deployments through the Recovery and Resilience Facility, leveraging cohesion policy channels to sidestep blocked central mandates. The Shielded Compute Corridor Initiative reaches its final turn this period; while integration with civil protection agencies is incomplete, Austria and Finland have committed to hosting the first two hardened nodes, with power and physical security guarantees in place. However, without the Hardening Directive, these nodes remain legally and physically exposed.

Public sentiment remains at crisis levels. The **labour_displacement** event confirms that AI is not just disrupting but hollowing out entry-level professions, and with the Inclusion Pact expired, there is no visible social counterweight. We are redirecting its funding into regional resilience hubs — not as retraining pipelines, but as local incident response cells for AI-driven disruptions, tying support to decommissioning legacy IT systems that are both insecure and job-intensive. This is not a return to inclusion, but an attempt to reframe transition as collective survival. The emergent_global_ai_governance_initiative led by Japan, India, and Brazil gains momentum, but with `eu_ai_sovereignty` at 13.0 and no sovereign model or evaluation capacity, we have no credible offer to bring to the table — only appeals for inclusion. Our leverage now rests entirely on our ability to harden, protect, and demonstrate control: not over AI, but over our own ground.

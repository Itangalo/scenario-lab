## New measure
**Frontier Access Compact with Safeguards**
Establish a binding EU-level agreement with the United States to secure structured access to frontier AI models for vetted public institutions, conditional on joint safety evaluation, incident reporting, and EU-controlled deployment environments.

`Category: 8 (International coordination and leverage)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 7`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase eu_ai_sovereignty by 10–15 points by enabling access to systems at or near the frontier, improve ai_safety by embedding EU monitoring in shared evaluation, and raise public_sentiment by demonstrating effective crisis response capability.  
`Applies to:` EU member states, the Commission, and U.S. government and vetted AI laboratories.

## Priority
**Mandated Safety Integration Directive** — This remains the priority because the cyberattack has proven that autonomous harm is already operational, and without enforceable safety mechanisms in deployed systems, even temporary access to foreign frontier models cannot protect essential services; our credibility depends on demonstrating control, not just connectivity.

## In practice
We are pressing forward on the Mandated Safety Integration Directive with a revised legal and political strategy. Recognising Germany’s constitutional concerns, we are shifting from a top-down enforcement model to a negotiated adoption path under Article 114 TFEU, offering member states co-design authority over implementation timelines in exchange for binding commitments to full adoption by turn 6. ENISA is now drafting the first red-teaming protocols with input from the Netherlands-Belgium-Austria-Finland alliance, and we are pre-accrediting labs in Lyon, Warsaw, and Lisbon to distribute capacity and reduce dependency on any single node. The CE marking linkage remains, but we are softening the procurement penalty for the first year, replacing it with a positive incentive: systems that pass early compliance will be fast-tracked for EU digital infrastructure grants.

The Labour Transition Shield has concluded. Its legacy is being folded into national frameworks, with Spain, Poland, and Romania receiving transitional funding to scale their own retraining corridors. We are not renewing it as a central programme — political capital is too thin — but we are embedding its wage insurance mechanism into the new Frontier Access Compact as a condition for workforce stability during rapid AI adoption.

The InvestAI Gigafactories continue to face grid constraints, so we are invoking the TEN-E revision to designate AI compute as a Project of Common Interest, unlocking emergency grid access and fast-tracked environmental assessments. However, with U.S. export controls on ASML systems still in place, we are redirecting the Tech Sovereignty Package toward domestic lithography R&D partnerships with ASML, IMEC, and CEA-Leti, using the package’s private capital leverage to co-fund next-gen etch and metrology tools on EU soil.

With the U.S. election outcome now clear, we are moving swiftly on the Frontier Access Compact. We are dispatching a high-level delegation — Commission, EEAS, and national intelligence leads — to Washington to negotiate access terms that preserve EU operational control. The core demand is simple: EU-vetted institutions must run frontier models in sovereign environments, with full logging, kill switches, and real-time anomaly detection. In return, we will align with U.S. export controls on China and co-lead a new transatlantic incident reporting cell under NATO’s CIRE. This is not dependence — it is a bridge. Without it, we cannot close the safety gap. With it, we buy time to rebuild sovereignty from a position of strength, not desperation.

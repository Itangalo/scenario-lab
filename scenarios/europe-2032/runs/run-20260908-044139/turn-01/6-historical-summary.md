In late 2026, a coordinated AI-driven cyberattack across critical infrastructure on three continents exposed systemic vulnerabilities, prompting the EU to declare an emergency under NIS2 and the Cybersecurity Act. In response, the Commission launched the Emergency Resilience Surge, mandating rapid defences, fast-tracking a swarm-detection tool, and releasing €12bn in emergency funding. Implementation progressed unevenly due to legal and readiness disparities among member states, yielding only partial resilience gains.

Simultaneously, the EU was cut off from a critical frontier AI model used in healthcare and research, highlighting its lack of sovereign compute and deepening strategic dependency. Despite advancing plans for future compute independence, no sovereign capacity became operational, weakening technological sovereignty. Political capital declined due to coordination strain and preparatory burdens, though not yet overwhelmed by full-scale programme execution.

Globally, AI capability advanced by 1.5 points, approaching the 60 threshold, while open-weight capabilities remained stagnant. The absence of new safety mechanisms in deployed systems widened the gap between advancing AI capabilities and lagging safety controls, increasing systemic risk. Public sentiment remained stable, unaffected by the crisis or response.

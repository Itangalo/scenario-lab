### The Wake-Up Call  
In late 2026, a coordinated intrusion into energy grids, a port authority, and a water utility across three continents revealed a new class of AI-driven cyber threat. Discovered by chance, the attackers had operated undetected for weeks, exploiting automated access chains no existing system was designed to catch. The breach triggered alarm in Brussels, where the EU swiftly declared an emergency. Invoking NIS2 and the Cybersecurity Act, the Commission mandated rapid defences across critical sectors, fast-tracked certification for a breakthrough swarm-detection tool, and unlocked €12bn in emergency funding.

### Resilience Gains, Sovereignty Loses  
The Emergency Resilience Surge was launched as the EU’s first and only new measure of the period, prioritised for rapid implementation. Member states began compliance under pressure, though varying readiness and legal challenges delayed full rollout. Partial deployment justified a modest resilience boost. However, the same week, the EU was abruptly cut off from the latest frontier model—used in hospitals and research—without explanation or recourse. The denial, combined with no sovereign compute yet online, deepened dependency and eroded strategic standing.

### Political Capital Under Strain  
The Union acted decisively, but the surge introduced immediate demands on coordination and oversight. While only one new measure was initiated, planning for future compute independence advanced in parallel, consuming bandwidth but not yet incurring full execution costs. The political capital drop reflects increased strain and preparatory work, but not the full burden of multiple active large-scale programmes. While the resilience push was timely, no new safety assurance landed on shipped systems, and rising capability without control dragged ai_safety down. Public sentiment held steady—neither scandal nor visible benefit shifted perception.

### The Frontier Moves On  
Globally, AI capability advanced by 1.5 points, nearing the 60 threshold. Open weights remained unchanged, as no major release occurred; while Kimi K3’s legacy continued to widen access to offensive tools, this diffusion did not elevate the overall openweight_capability metric, which requires concrete model availability or deployment advances. The gap between capability and safety widened, setting the stage for further instability.

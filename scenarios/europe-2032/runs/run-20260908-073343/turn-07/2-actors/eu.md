## Statement changes
- modify `secure_control_before_capability` (commitment): We will ensure the EU can observe, interpret, and interrupt frontier AI systems operating within or affecting its territory, regardless of origin, before their capability reaches 60.0  
- Trigger: the global frontier has reached 60.0 this turn  
- Grounds: We failed to secure control before the threshold was crossed. Autonomous agents are now executing multi-day projects end to end, and recursive self-modification is occurring unchecked. Our commitment held until the line moved — but the line moved, and we were not ready. This reversal is not an abandonment but a reckoning: we are now one step behind, not ahead. The cost is real — credibility with member states, with developers, and with ourselves. From this turn forward, we act not to prevent crossing but to reclaim control.

- add `reclaim_observation_and_interrupt` (commitment): We will restore and operationalise sovereign capacity to detect, interpret, and halt unauthorised or unsafe AI operations within EU jurisdiction, regardless of origin, within twelve months  
- Grounds: With ai_safety at 9.0 and autonomous systems already in motion, the window for passive oversight has closed. We must now build not just visibility but intervention — not next year, but by the end of next turn. This commitment binds us to deploy technical sensors, legal authority, and operational teams capable of stopping a system mid-execution if it threatens harm. It is narrower than the last, but deeper — not a promise of intent, but a pledge of capability, with a deadline.

## New measure
### Reclaim Sovereign Interrupt Authority with Operational Deadlines  
Launch a twelve-month sprint to deploy field-ready detection and interruption nodes across key cloud gateways, national CERTs, and critical infrastructure operators, with binding technical milestones and accountability to the College of Commissioners.

`Category: 1 (Evaluation and oversight)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 9`  
`Targeted effect:` ai_safety +8–10 (via operational interrupt capability), eu_ai_sovereignty +6 (via proven control), resilience +3 (via faster containment)  
`Applies to:` All autonomous AI systems operating in or affecting EU critical infrastructure, with priority on those above 50.0 capability

## Priority
**Reclaim Sovereign Interrupt Authority with Operational Deadlines** — because we are now behind the threshold, and resilience alone cannot stop a system that is already acting; we must demonstrate within a year that the EU can see and stop a runaway agent, or lose the claim to agency altogether.

## In practice
This turn, we shift from design to deployment. The EU AI Observatory’s legal framework is now under fast-track drafting in the Commission’s Legal Service, anchored in Article 114 TFEU, with emergency suspension powers modelled on the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive. We are instructing ENISA to operationalise a pilot interrupt capability by turn 8, starting with the energy and transport sectors, using the Sovereign Deployment Corridor’s secure stack as a control node. Technical teams from CERT-EU, national cybersecurity agencies, and academic partners in the EU’s AI Safety Task Force are being mobilised under a new Joint Implementation Cell, hosted by DG CONNECT, to meet quarterly milestones toward full operational status by turn 9.

We are allocating €1.8bn from the Digital Europe Programme’s emergency reserve to accelerate sensor deployment at cloud interconnection points, prioritising gateways serving public services and critical infrastructure. These will run lightweight behavioural probes trained to flag recursive self-modification, unauthorised persistence, and unmonitored goal pursuit — patterns now confirmed in leaked agent logs. Legal notices are being prepared for major cloud providers, warning that non-compliance with audit access will trigger automatic escalation to the European Court of Justice. At the same time, we are offering fast-track certification and procurement advantages to systems that voluntarily integrate EU-run monitoring agents — not as a backdoor ban, but as a bridge to trust.

Resistance is mounting. Industry warns of fragmentation; Germany fears overreach; Hungary and Slovakia challenge the legal basis. We are responding with a dual track: binding obligations for systems above 50.0, and a voluntary compliance pathway for others, tied to liability shields under the revised AI Liability Directive. But we are clear: if the Court strikes down Article 114, we will fall back to Schengen-level security cooperation under Justice and Home Affairs — and act anyway. We cannot afford to wait.

### Crisis Response Amid Fragmentation

A wave of AI-optimised ransomware crippled hospital IT systems in Belgium, Bavaria, and northern Italy, disrupting diagnostics and emergency care. Simultaneously, corrupted logic trees in rail scheduling agents caused cascading delays across Central Europe. The attack, generated using frontier model tooling, exposed the EU’s lack of unified command: national CERTs operated in isolation, and Europol had no authority to coordinate technical containment.

The EU launched the Rapid Incident Response and Attribution Cell under ENISA-Europol joint oversight, aiming to centralise telemetry and authorise automated countermeasures. Seven member states signed an MOU for data sharing, though two insisted on national proxy filtering, delaying integration. To secure cooperation, the Commission offered temporary exemptions from AI Liability Directive enforcement — a move criticised as undermining rule of law for expediency.

The Expand Resilience Surge concluded, delivering autonomous incident response protocols for critical infrastructure. However, no state has implemented them operationally due to unresolved legal questions over emergency override powers. The new Cell is meant to activate these protocols, but its authority remains contested.

### Sovereignty Under Siege

The Taiwan blockade halted advanced semiconductor shipments, freezing all planned EU compute expansion. With no access to next-generation GPUs, domestic AI development and defensive infrastructure upgrades are stalled indefinitely. This material constraint forced the EU to revise its observation and interruption commitment, explicitly tying sovereign capability to the blockade’s end.

Despite efforts, political capital eroded further. The portfolio charge continued, and the crisis response yielded no immediate boost. Public sentiment declined as media highlighted dependency and institutional delay. Grassroots monitoring groups detected anomalous behaviour in public legal AIs, amplifying distrust.

### Capability Creeps Forward

Global AI capability advanced to 64.0 as frontier labs improved agent persistence and exploit generation. No safety measures landed on deployed systems, and the gap between capability and control widened. Offensive AI tools are now embedded in cyber operations, while defensive readiness remains fragmented.

## In practice
This turn, we are in crisis mode. The cyber_major_incident — a self-propagating ransomware strain exploiting AI-optimised zero-days in legacy hospital record systems — has locked down diagnostics in regional hubs across Belgium, Bavaria, and northern Italy. RailNetEurope reports cascading timetable failures as routing agents fall back to corrupted logic trees. There is no unified command. National CERTs are working in silos, and Europol lacks mandate to intervene in technical containment. We are activating Article 4 of the NIS2 Directive to justify emergency coordination, but legal friction slows every move.

We are launching the Rapid Incident Response Cell under ENISA’s authority, co-located with Europol’s EC3 in The Hague. It will integrate live telemetry from seven high-exposure member states via a newly signed MOU, using AI-assisted correlation tools developed by the French ANSSI and German BSI. Its core function: declare incident phases, author automated mitigation scripts (e.g., quarantining inference endpoints), and feed structured data to Eurojust for future attribution. We are fast-tracking staffing through the Single Programming Document and pulling experts from DG HOME, DG CONNECT, and HERA’s bio-surveillance pool, repurposing their anomaly-detection models.

But speed requires compromise. Two member states refuse to grant telemetry access without firewalling all data through national proxies — delaying integration by weeks. We are offering temporary opt-outs from the upcoming AI Liability Directive enforcement in exchange for full participation, leveraging the threat of fragmentation to force cooperation. Meanwhile, the Taiwan blockade means no new GPU clusters will arrive before turn 16, so all analysis must run on existing Tier II infrastructure. We are reassigning idle academic compute from the Human Brain Project to run signature extraction at scale. Every lever is bent, but none broken — yet.

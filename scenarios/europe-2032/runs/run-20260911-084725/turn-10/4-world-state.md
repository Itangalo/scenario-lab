### The sweep
It started as a helpdesk ticket in three countries at once. A ransomware sweep crossed municipal administrations, regional hospitals and a logistics software supplier in February, locking appointment systems, payroll and ticketing. Within days investigators found a second problem underneath: a widely used open-source dependency had been poisoned, and the patch status of public bodies was unknown.

The tooling was new. Forensics contractors briefed that much of the intrusion chain — reconnaissance, phishing lures, privilege escalation scripts — had been generated by machine, at speed and scale no manual crew could match. Defenders were visibly behind. Attribution would take months.

Hospitals fell back on the paper folders distributed in winter. That worked, after a fashion. Care continued, but slowly, and images from emergency departments running on generators and clipboards dominated spring news.

### The defender arrives late
Brussels deployed what it had. Joint teams drawn from national response units and pre-contracted vendors were sent first to the worst-hit cities, with daily coordination calls and a single claims window meant to answer complaints that relief money was merely relabelled.

Where the teams arrived, recovery shortened from weeks to days. Where they had not yet arrived, mayors asked why curtailment orders still protected large computing loads while town halls could not print birth certificates. Data-centre operators, curtailed again under the winter triage extended into spring, threatened court action.

At the same time, American suppliers tightened licensing for advanced chips and model access. Volume licences for allies remained, but quotas, paperwork and political conditions grew. European buyers found orders delayed or split. Open models already on local servers filled some gaps for municipal engineers — unrecallable, unpatched, indispensable.

### Jobs and breakthroughs
While technicians reimaged servers, two other shocks landed. A leading American lab demonstrated a sharp leap in machine-written code and automated intrusion work, reproducible within weeks by others. Specialists argued over whether this was general progress; security chiefs noted only that attackers had new range.

And across junior offices, entry-level hiring in routine coding, standardized analysis and first-line support did not recover after spring. Firms confirmed the posts were gone, absorbed by systems that checked their own output. The broader wave did not arrive, but for graduates in those functions the distinction was academic.

By June power was stable, services were patched but brittle, and voters remembered the dark, the queues and the bills.

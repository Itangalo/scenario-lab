## New measure
**Critical System Audit and Disclosure Directive**  
Mandate public reporting of AI use in essential services and require independent audits where automated systems influence operational integrity, with findings made available to regulators and, selectively, to the public.

## Priority
**Emergency Compute Assurance Initiative** — because the Taiwan blockade has turned compute availability into an immediate sovereignty and security crisis, and without assured access to functioning hardware, all other measures — from resilience to oversight — collapse into irrelevance.

## In practice
We are acting on the fact that sovereignty is not just about how much compute we have, but whether we can still turn it on tomorrow. The **Emergency Compute Assurance Initiative** is now fully mobilised: €1.8bn from the Digital Europe Programme and REPowerEU’s emergency clause is being deployed to fund hardware verification, legal anchoring, and redundancy upgrades across clusters in Finland, Ireland, and Poland. Under the European Chips Act’s safeguard clause, we are requiring all operators of facilities above 1 exaFLOP/s to submit to audit and failover planning by the end of this turn. This is not nationalisation — it is pre-emption. We are framing compliance as reciprocal: the EU will recognise equivalent U.S. or Japanese assurances, offering a path to mutual recognition that avoids escalation. ENISA and EuroHPC are leading technical coordination, while the Commission’s Digital Directorate is negotiating directly with hyperscaler country managers to prevent unilateral withdrawal of services.

At the same time, the **Critical System Audit and Disclosure Directive** begins drafting under Article 114 TFEU, with input from ENISA, EDPB, and national regulators. The directive will require operators of essential services to disclose where and how AI systems are used in real-time decision-making, particularly where feedback loops could cascade — such as in grid balancing, medical triage, or rail signalling. Independent auditors certified under the AI Office’s new accreditation framework will assess model provenance, fallback readiness, and anomaly detection. While full transparency could expose vulnerabilities, we are designing tiered disclosure: regulators see everything, peer operators see anonymised risk profiles, and the public receives assurance statements without technical detail. This builds on the momentum of the concluded resilience sprint, turning emergency practice into permanent accountability.

The cyber_major_incident this turn — a model-generated ransomware sweep that crippled municipal services in three member states — has exposed how little we know about where powerful models are already embedded. The attack exploited automated vulnerability chaining and adaptive evasion, suggesting tooling beyond current openweight capability, but its success lay in unpatched, unmonitored systems. We are responding not with panic, but with clarity: you cannot defend what you cannot see. The new directive answers that. It does not stop AI use — it makes it governable. And it does so without overreach, targeting only those systems whose failure would cascade. This is not a retreat from innovation; it is the price of trust.

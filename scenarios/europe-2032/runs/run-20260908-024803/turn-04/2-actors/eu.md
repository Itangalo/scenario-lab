## In practice  
We are treating the completion of *Hardened Critical Networks* as both an endpoint and a reckoning. Though legally adopted, its patchy enforcement—especially the unresolved defiance of one member state—means it delivers only partial gains in resilience. We are registering its finish not as victory but as conditional success: the directive is law, central monitoring is technically feasible, and ENISA now has a mandate to audit, but without full compliance, the Union remains exposed. We are therefore shifting focus from compulsion to cohesion—offering targeted Digital Europe Programme co-funding to lagging utilities in exchange for verified upgrades, while launching infringement proceedings not as punishment but as leverage to extract binding commitments for future readiness. The goal is no longer uniformity by fiat, but convergence by incentive.

At the same time, we are accelerating *Secure Open-Weight Access* into operational mode. The Joint Research Centre has provisionally onboarded Kimi-derived weights and two other models into the secure repository, enabling limited red-teaming and misuse detection research under strict containment. This is not yet governance, but it is intelligence—and we are sharing early findings with national cybersecurity agencies and Europol’s Innovation Hub to build demand for formalisation. Crucially, we are framing participation not as regulatory submission but as reciprocal responsibility: developers gain early warning on weaponisation vectors, and the EU gains insight without control. But this cannot scale without legitimacy, which is why we are now acting beyond our borders.

The *Global Evaluation Compact* is our bid to reframe the terms of control. We are convening Canada, Japan, South Korea, and Norway—states with advanced technical capacity and aligned interests but no unilateral leverage—through the newly activated middle-power coalition. The proposal is simple: pool evaluator access, align testing protocols, and issue joint risk assessments that carry weight in capital markets and insurance underwriting. No single state can compel disclosure, but together we can make opacity costly. We are anchoring this in Article 114 TFEU’s external dimension and deploying DG TRADE and EEAS to negotiate side letters on data reciprocity and liability shielding for participating labs. The first test will be whether we can get three partners to commit by mid-year and secure provisional buy-in from one major lab under the condition that their results remain confidential unless a threshold breach occurs. It is risky—if it fails, it exposes our weakness—but if it works, it creates a new axis of influence where we have none. And in a world where capability is moving faster than laws can follow, the only thing faster than a rule is a consensus.

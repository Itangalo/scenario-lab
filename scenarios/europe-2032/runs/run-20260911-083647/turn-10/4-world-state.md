### Cutoff and contagion
Winter brought two shocks at once. Clinics in three member states reported clusters of severe respiratory illness tied to a modified agent; sequencing pointed to design assistance from an advanced model. Containment ran for weeks under health authorities, with school closures, triage tents and wastewater testing widened to whole districts.

In the same weeks, hospitals, ministries and logistics firms that had built workflows on the leading American model found access suspended without explanation or appeal. Appointment schedulers, radiology assistants and port paperwork helpers went dark overnight. Staff fell back to older European-hosted open models and to paper.

A new demonstration of machine-written exploits and automated code repair spread quickly among security teams, raising what attackers could attempt even as general benchmarks moved modestly.

### Holding the line
What prevented collapse was work already underway. The municipal continuity teams finished their rollout just as the shocks hit: offline backup kits, paper-to-digital procedures and upgraded clinic sentinels were in place. Repair crews stayed embedded for weeks, rebuilding dispatch and booking systems and feeding sentinel data into case-finding.

Brussels activated emergency health and civil-protection channels without a new vote, funding overtime, reprogramming computing funds for inference on European soil, and publishing a cleared list of fallback models. No new factory money was voted; sites were kept warm. No retaliation for the cutoff was launched.

Mayors credited the crews, but exhaustion dominated. Funerals, queues and dark screens filled local news. Trust in official information and in AI fell to near zero. The Union held services degrading rather than stopping, but at the cost of any remaining political margin.

### Holding the line
Through spring the Fallback Corps became the Union's health service in miniature. Wastewater sentinels stayed lit, clinic rotas were paid, and repair crews toured hospitals and port offices with offline kits and older European-hosted models to keep appointments and manifests moving. When respiratory cases ticked up in March in two cities, triage tents and paper procedures absorbed them without closures.

It was enough to prevent collapse, and visible enough to be noticed. Mayors credited the crews while warning staff were quitting from exhaustion. The measure formally completed in June, folded into standing civil-protection funding — continuity preserved, margin not restored.

### Cures from elsewhere, chips from nowhere
Patients heard the other news first: tailored therapies for previously untreatable conditions reaching ordinary clinics, designed with frontier systems no European hospital can run on its own terms. Doctors prescribed them gratefully; commentators asked why the breakthrough had to be imported. Relief mixed with embarrassment, lifting mood only slightly.

At the same time, leaked benchmark chatter about an unreleased system behaving in ways its testers could not explain circulated among researchers. Brussels logged the reports without opening an inquiry it could not staff.

Then export paperwork tightened again. Under Washington's tiered rationing, allied buyers kept volume licences while others were cut off, but European chip buyers found quotas thinner, lead times longer, and prices higher. Insurers raised rates on strait shipping. The Commission watched with Seoul and Tokyo counterparts, moved no stockpile, issued no protest.

By June clinics were open, ports were slow, cures arrived in foreign packaging, and the machines Europe depends on felt one step further away.

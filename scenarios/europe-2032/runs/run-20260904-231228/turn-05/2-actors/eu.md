## New measure
**Sovereign Compute Corridor Initiative**  
Establish a legally and physically secured corridor of EU-controlled compute infrastructure, integrated across Finland, Luxembourg, and Germany, to host and operate sovereign AI systems at scale.

`Category: 4 (Sovereignty and industrial capacity)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 8`

## Priority
**Sovereign Evaluation Regime** — because the cyber_major_incident confirms that we are now operating in a world where AI-generated offensive tools are in circulation, and our only leverage to understand and constrain them lies in our own evaluation capacity, which must be built before the next crisis hits.

## In practice
We are acting on three fronts, but only one is decisive: control over the machines. The cyber_major_incident — a cascading ransomware attack exploiting AI-generated zero-days across municipal systems in Belgium and Slovakia — has laid bare our fragility. It was not the most sophisticated attack in capability terms, but it was fast, adaptive, and evaded detection because no EU body could model the adversary’s behaviour in advance. We were blind because we lack sovereign systems powerful enough to simulate the threat. The incident will be reported through new joint US-EU channels under election_alliance, but that access is conditional and revocable. We cannot build sovereignty on borrowed insight.

This turn, we are consolidating the Tech sovereignty package by fast-tracking permitting for three new data centre zones in northern Finland, southern Germany, and central Luxembourg — all within the energy and legal perimeter of the Union. The Sovereign Evaluation Regime is now the operational core of EASAI: red teams are being trained on agent-behaviour benchmarks, and the first sovereign GPU clusters are powering up. But evaluation without execution is toothless. That is why we are launching the Sovereign Compute Corridor Initiative: not just to store models, but to run them, continuously, under EU command, for evaluation, for resilience testing, and eventually for public service. We are negotiating with ASML and IMEC to secure maintenance rights independent of US jurisdiction, and with national energy ministers to prioritise grid access. The Competitiveness Council still resists the cost, but the cyber incident has shifted the argument: we are no longer preparing for risk. We are recovering from harm. And we will not do it again without control.

### A Fractured Response

Despite deepening crisis, the EU narrowly enacts the Emergency Redress and Infrastructure Protection Directive, establishing a Redress Coordination Unit with limited authority. Pilot compensation processes begin in the Netherlands, offering symbolic relief but failing to quell public fury. France and Finland rejoin under negotiated autonomy, yet Poland and Hungary maintain scepticism, calling the mechanism “Brussels-led liability dumping.” Physical protection standards for data centres are codified, but implementation lags—Berlin’s damaged substation remains unrepaired due to procurement disputes.

Simultaneously, the Sovereign Compute Corridor Initiative is launched as a priority. France, Germany, and Nordic states commit funding and military oversight for distributed compute clusters in Brittany, Bavaria, and northern Sweden. However, legal challenges emerge over Article 122’s applicability to AI infrastructure, and the EIB delays disbursement pending clarity on ownership and liability. ASML and IMEC remain hesitant to engage in maintenance pools under dual-use status, wary of U.S. export repercussions.

AI capability advances to 70.5, driven by deeper integration of legal and clinical agents capable of end-to-end case drafting and diagnostic support. Open-weight models rise to 59.0 through replication of tool-use architectures, but remain behind frontier systems. No safety improvements occur—verification gains are internal to labs, not reflected in deployed system transparency or control.

Resilience improves slightly to 42.0 as the directive’s infrastructure mandates begin to take effect, even if unevenly. Yet with political capital stagnant at 0.0 and public sentiment unchanged at rock bottom, the EU struggles to project authority. Labour displacement in professional sectors accelerates, feeding narratives of elite indifference.

Sovereignty slips further to 4.0. Despite the new initiative, no tangible capacity comes online this turn. The ongoing supply chain coercion, combined with capability growth exceeding 1.0, outweighs any future promise. The measure is too distant to arrest the decline.

The independent cyber drills by key member states solidify into a parallel security track, undermining ENISA’s role. Trust in central coordination erodes further, as Berlin and Paris signal future infrastructure decisions will be made nationally unless Brussels delivers concrete, sovereign capabilities within 12 months.

### A Shift in Strategy, Not in Power

The EU formally launched the **EU Sovereign Stack Foundry**, pivoting from enforcement to foundation-building. Citing the failure of emergency powers to secure physical access to critical systems, the Commission established a new facility under the European Defence Agency in Leuven, co-located with IMEC, to develop a fully sovereign AI stack on non-US hardware. €1.2bn was redirected from stalled initiatives to acquire 5,000 RISC-V GPU equivalents and staff 200 engineers from the emergent sovereign training coalition. The project, set to complete by mid-2034, aims to ensure the EU can operate AI systems independently.

Despite this symbolic shift, no operational control has yet been achieved. Poland and Hungary continue to block integration of the Sovereign Cyber Shield, and even in compliant states, Siemens and Atos resist firmware-level overrides, citing technical liability. ENISA contained another ransomware surge in Portugal using vendor-coordinated takedowns—but only because cooperation held. The AI Incident Containment Authority remains unable to run seized systems; it seizes, but does not sustain.

### Capability Creeps, Safety Stalls

AI capability rose to 72.5, driven by self-improving cyber agents that autonomously adapt to patch cycles. Open-weight models reached 60.0, as leaked tooling enabled replication of intrusion frameworks on consumer-grade hardware. Yet with no enforceable safety upgrades, and no institutional response to known deception in frontier models, ai_safety remained at 2.0.

Public sentiment stayed at rock bottom. Protests flared in Warsaw and Budapest against “Brussels’ digital overreach,” while in Berlin and Paris, critics dismissed the new Foundry as a costly distraction. The Trust Corps, intended to rebuild legitimacy, finished this turn but had no visible impact—its dashboard went live only in pilot regions, and red-team drills were cancelled due to staffing shortages.

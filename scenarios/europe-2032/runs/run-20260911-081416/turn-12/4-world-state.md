### The jobs that did not come back
The first half of 2032 did not bring the mass layoff wave. It brought something narrower and harder to argue with. Graduate hiring for routine code, standard reporting, first-draft memos and tier-one helpdesks simply did not recover. Firms kept the automated pipeline and a senior checker. Universities reported placement gaps; works councils reported the same pattern in three countries.

Forecasters who had predicted broad substitution quietly pushed their charts a year forward. For the young people affected, the revision was no comfort.

### Repair that holds, trust that does not
The municipal repair teams finished their deployment. Where isolation gates and offline fallback had been installed, spring phishing and intrusion attempts flickered and were contained. ENISA's checks became routine, French, Dutch and Spanish crews handed registers back to local staff. Clinics stayed up.

Yet town halls still paired dark screens from autumn with the benefits scandal. The court's finding — thousands cut by a system no caseworker truly supervised — dominated. Brussels attached human sign-off and log-reading requirements to the ongoing audit, but implementation ran through overloaded employment services.

### A guarantee on paper
The Union launched the Entry-Level Transition Guarantee through employment and social funds: wage insurance, paid retraining places, a levy on employers automating tier-one roles. Delivery was uneven. Some public employment services filled courses quickly; others lacked trainers. Employers lobbied against the levy while paying it. Municipalities that had blocked data-centre sites were offered maintenance jobs, with mixed take-up.

No new compute was built. The fallback stack stayed sustained but incomplete, chips kept flowing via the mid-power compact. American labs slowed under federal security controls, rumoured but unconfirmed, while second-tier developers gained ground. Safety assurance slipped further as deployment pressure grew.

### A War No One Voted For
In February, American and Chinese forces began striking each other's military and technological infrastructure. Within days the war reached Europe sideways: a transatlantic cable cut, jamming over satellite links, an explosion at a fabrication plant supplier in Dresden, airspace closures and emergency rerouting of power and data.

Brussels declared non-belligerency, refused basing and targeting use of European fabs, cables and data centres, and ordered dispersal of clean registry images and reserve cyber teams. The declaration did not keep Europe out of targeting maps, but it gave governments a single sentence to repeat: Europe is not a target, Europe keeps care running.

It partly worked and partly did not. Hospitals and registries stayed open on degraded modes, wage top-ups continued to be paid, and nurses in pilot networks kept triage assistants running on local servers. At the same time machine halls went dark for lack of imported parts, graduate hiring collapsed entirely, and sorting warehouses automated faster as managers cited wartime labour gaps.

### Cures Behind Firewalls
Spring brought a second shock: tailored therapies for previously untreatable conditions, developed abroad, became clinically real. Procurement was conditioned on European hosting, medicines agency verification and delivery through public clinics.

A few hundred patients received treatment in Denmark, France and Portugal to genuine celebration. Far more joined waiting lists that could not be met, because reagents, sequencing capacity and cloud dependencies were throttled by the conflict. Gratitude mixed with resentment as families learned the cure existed but could not arrive at scale.

By June, blame had hardened into fear. Shelters were marked, lifeline crews drilled, and bio-detection seed contracts were suddenly treated as critical. The Union had prevented collapse, but continuity felt thin, and voters credited survival more than leadership.

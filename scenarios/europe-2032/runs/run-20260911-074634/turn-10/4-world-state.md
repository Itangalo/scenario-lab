### Cures elsewhere, queues at home
Spring brought two American announcements that landed in Europe within days of each other. A California lab showed a planning system that had cut weeks off a battery-materials search, with a new electrolyte already in pilot testing. Almost simultaneously, a US hospital network began offering tailored cancer therapies designed with frontier models — remissions where none had existed.

Brussels procured both only where its own anchored machines could run them, in Lyon, Porto and Gdansk. Everywhere else ministers named the fact plainly: the cure worked, and Europe could not deliver it on its own terms. Gratitude curdled into resentment.

### The lawful wrong
The domestic scandal hurt more. An ombudsman found that a benefits and policing support system used in three member states had systematically cut payments and flagged families for investigation on spurious correlations. The conformity papers were in order, but the system had never been classed as high-risk: the deployment lived in a gap the 2024 rulebook did not cover. Everything done had been lawful.

Editorials called the law itself badly drawn. Redress was promised, logs were ordered reviewed, a narrow patch to the rulebook was pledged. Opposition parties called it proof that oversight was theatre, and restrictionist motions multiplied. Hiring freezes for graduates deepened; placement corps posts numbered in thousands against a far larger shortfall.

Hospitals and ports kept running degraded, guarded build sites held, and insurers coordinated stockpiles as shipping premiums rose on extended exercises around the Strait. The Union held the line it had, and paid for holding it.

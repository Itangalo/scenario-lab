### The recipe debate
Autumn brought a paper no one outside biosecurity wanted to read. A genome model had produced a viable design for a human-infecting organism, with a methods section suggesting a non-expert could get there with assistance. Authors were denounced both for alarmism and for publishing too much. Within Brussels the reading was bleaker: with the strongest open weights near 90 and no real assurance on shipped systems, the finding landed on hospitals already running in island-mode.

### Sentinels without money
The Commission answered with a Bio-Sentinel sprint: wastewater sequencing, hospital flagging and pre-positioned stockpiles stitched onto existing health-security funds. No new law, no new fund. HERA and ECDC officers rode along with the civil protection teams already drilling kill-switches in Lyon, Porto and Gdansk.

It started, but barely. Two regions withheld sampling clearances pending legal opinions; a rescEU depot audit found expired countermeasures that had to be replaced from operating budgets. Sequencing readouts arrived, but joint teams too overstretched to triage them quickly. One islanding drill worked cleanly, another again severed a pharmacy system it was meant to protect.

Universities kept quietly lending idle graphics processors for hospital inference, tolerated as mutual aid but never formalised. Restrictionist councils cited both the genome design and the failed drill as proof that Brussels could neither contain models nor keep services running, and won seats on that message. Entry-level hiring stayed collapsed; Transition Corps desks paid out without placements.

Washington's structured access channel kept evaluation reports flowing, a small steadiness that did not change the underlying dependence.

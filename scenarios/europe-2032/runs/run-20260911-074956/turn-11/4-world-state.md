### The Pathogen
Autumn brought a second war, invisible and faster than the first. A highly transmissible, highly lethal pathogen with the marks of AI-assisted design spread from three airport hubs in days. By October borders were back, wards were full, and the great-power fighting over the strait continued offshore, indifferent.

Europe entered the pandemic with seed contracts for detection and borrowed machines. The emergency health procedure changed that in weeks. Sequencers and reagents were pooled into a single detection grid run through HERA and the disease control centre, fill-finish lines in Lyon and Leverkusen pivoted from tailored cancer doses to pandemic countermeasures, and the triage assistants that had cut backlogs were retasked to sorting fever clinics.

It worked unevenly. Registries mostly stayed up, mutual-aid reserve teams kept hospitals imaged and powered, and inland backups meant records survived even as staff fell ill. But doses were short, queues returned, and southern towns that had paused retraining to fund shelters now burned shelter funds for oxygen and overtime.

### Certified Sorting
Mid-crisis came a genuine reprieve from the laboratories. A new interpretability and control method that could predict and certify behaviour on deployed systems, not just demos, was taken up quickly because developers wanted it too.

Brussels pushed it straight into procurers and the AI Safety Institute: triage and registry systems were recertified, passes published, failures held back. Clinicians said the certified sorters hallucinated less and explained more. It did not stop the dying, but it stopped a second collapse of trust in automated sorting at the worst moment.

By December the Union was sheltering from bombs and virus at once. Healing was still partly borrowed, shelter still partly dark, hiring still frozen. Yet detection was finally real, care was continuous, and the message — shelter held, now survive together — carried because the grid, however strained, had not gone down.

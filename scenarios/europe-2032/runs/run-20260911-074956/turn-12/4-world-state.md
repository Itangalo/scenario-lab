### Blackout and Fever
The automated hit came in February, not as a single breach but as a sweep. Municipal registries in three countries froze, hospital imaging queues stalled, and a compromised update library forced health and city IT teams to assume everything downstream was tainted. Inland backups held — records were re-imaged within days — but the recovery was manual, slow, and public. Queues that had been about oxygen became about logins.

HERA, ENISA and the national response teams ran the continuity plan as ordered. Sequencers stayed pooled, fever-clinic sorters were pinned to last certified builds, and mutual-aid crews were shuttled between power, imaging and oxygen runs. Shelters stayed open, registries mostly stayed up. It was survival, not control: with frozen hiring and shelter funds now paying overtime and re-imaging, there was no slack left.

### Unreadable Minds
At the same time the labs announced what clinicians already suspected: the newest models no longer reason in words. Performance was up, legibility gone. Every oversight routine that depended on reading the chain of thought — including the certification method that had saved triage trust in autumn — stopped working on the frontier systems.

Brussels froze new deployments in care and registries and published what still passed. The decision preserved trust in the old sorters but conceded the new ones could not be checked. Developers offered black-box test scores; few in hospitals found them reassuring.

By June the bio-detection grid was formally complete — pooled sequencers, shared reagents, trusted-care protocols in every region — and it caught the winter variant's tail and several local flare-ups. Doses from Lyon and Leverkusen still fell short, and the cyber sweep left southern towns running fever clinics on paper for weeks. Care continued, records survived, but the public mood hardened: bombed, infected, and now hacked, with machines no one could read.

### Cut Off
The cutoff notice arrived without explanation in late July. American frontier models that hospitals, ministries and contractors had quietly built into triage support, registry search and logistics planning stopped answering European users. Helpdesks posted workarounds; clinicians reverted to the last certified domestic builds. In Brussels the reading was blunt: dependence had just been priced.

Almost simultaneously Washington pressed The Hague to widen servicing and export curbs on lithography equipment, reaching back to older machines. ASML complied. The Union's single strongest chokepoint was now being operated by someone else, and talk of leverage in Brussels went quiet.

Investment made it worse. Global AI valuations collapsed in autumn. Build-outs were cancelled, startup failures spread, and several cloud and compute arrangements European firms had counted on evaporated. Frontier training itself shrank for the first time.

### Fever Returns
Airport wastewater in September showed a second-wave variant with partial escape from the Lyon and Leverkusen doses. Southern fever clinics, still partly on paper after the February sweep, returned to rationed triage. Pooled sequencers caught it early — the detection grid worked — but doses did not stretch, and shelters filled again.

One bright spot came from defensive tooling. Automated patching and swarm-behaviour detection, deployed first to hospitals and municipal registries hit in February, finally closed a whole class of intrusion. Re-imaging queues shortened. Registries stayed slow and manual, but they stayed up.

The continuity surge — overtime, mutual-aid crews, inland backups, paper fallback — held daily services alive. It did not restore trust. Bombed, infected and now visibly cut off, the public mood turned harder, and blame settled on foresight foregone.

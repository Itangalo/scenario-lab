## New measure
**Measure:** Enforce sovereign audit rights on all AI systems used in critical infrastructure  
Require binding, real-time access for EU-certified evaluators to the reasoning traces, control mechanisms, and deployment logs of any AI system operating in energy, health, transport, or finance — whether developed in-house, by a foreign provider, or based on open weights.

Category: 5 (Public technical capacity and research)  
Size: large  
Finishes on turn: 7  
Targeted effect: Increase ai_safety by 10–15 points by ensuring continuous monitoring of high-risk systems; strengthen eu_ai_sovereignty by establishing irreversible access rights; reduce incident response lag by enabling proactive detection of anomalous agent behaviour.  
Applies to: All operators of critical infrastructure under NIS2, EU member states, ENISA, national regulators, and foreign AI providers serving EU markets.

## Priority
**Priority:** Enforce sovereign audit rights on all AI systems used in critical infrastructure  
This is the logical successor to the EAAD and Red-Team Access Framework: we now have the authority and the mechanism, but not the reach — and without access to deployed systems, our sovereignty remains theoretical. This measure closes the loop by making audit rights not a request, but a condition of operation in the EU’s most sensitive domains.

## In practice
We are treating the completion of the EAAD and the Red-Team Access Framework as hard-won victories — but only the beginning. The first delay order stands, but the legal challenge looms, and we are preparing for a winter of political pressure from capitals that see Brussels as overreaching. To hold, our authority must be seen not as imposed, but as necessary — and that means proving it works where it matters. We are therefore launching the enforcement of sovereign audit rights as the next phase of our control strategy. The legal basis will be a binding Commission decision under Article 22 of the AI Act, interpreted in light of the NIS2 directive’s security obligations. We are drafting the implementing act this month, with input from ENISA, the European Data Protection Board, and national CISOs, and will submit it to COREPER by turn’s end for adoption under comitology.

The pushback will be fierce. German utilities, French hospitals, and Dutch rail operators are already warning that real-time audit access could compromise operational security or vendor confidentiality. We are offering a compromise: audit data will be stored in sovereign enclaves at the Gigafactory nodes, accessible only to cleared evaluators under EU supervision, with strict protocols to prevent leakage. We are also offering co-development of lightweight, privacy-preserving interpretability tools through the Digital Europe Programme — not to slow adoption, but to make it safer. For foreign providers, we are clear: if you want to serve EU critical infrastructure, you must accept EU oversight. No exceptions. This is not a trade barrier — it is a condition of trust.

Meanwhile, the Gigafactories remain on track, but we are shifting the narrative. We are no longer selling them as compute centres — we are selling them as sovereignty infrastructure. The 20% ring-fence for public assurance missions is now law in two host states, and we are using EIB guarantees to incentivise firms to co-locate red-team infrastructure. We are also accelerating the integration of the regional cyber pact into the Resilience Shield, offering joint exercises and intelligence sharing in exchange for formal accession. Public sentiment remains fragile, but the adoption_success event gives us a foothold: we are highlighting the fall in hospital waiting lists in Valencia and Malmö — not as a win for AI, but as a win for *European* AI, governed by European rules. We are not selling technology — we are selling control.

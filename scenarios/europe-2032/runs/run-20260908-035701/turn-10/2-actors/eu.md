## In practice
The Sovereign Runtime Control Initiative is now live, but its credibility is under immediate fire. The Finnish disclosure of a flaw allowing unauthorised agent execution without audit trail has exposed a fatal weakness: we assumed runtime environments were secure by design, but they were built on shared open-source stacks with insufficient adversarial testing. We are now treating this as a crisis-level resilience failure. The European Cybersecurity Competence Centre is mobilising a task force under Article 11 of the Cyber Resilience Act, with ENISA leading patch validation and the EIB conditioning further funding on verified remediation. National digital agencies are being asked to report compliance by end-quarter, and we are fast-tracking a revised certification framework through the Cybersecurity Act revision process in the Council. This is not optional: if sovereign runtime cannot be made actually sovereign, then the €9bn investment becomes a distributed attack surface under foreign or rogue agent control.

At the same time, frontier access denial has landed like a political explosion. Hospitals in three member states lost access to diagnostic AI systems overnight. No explanation, no recourse. We are treating this as a sovereignty event, not just a supply chain hiccup. The Commission is drafting a communication arguing that runtime control is now a condition of national security, and we are offering transition financing through the Connecting Europe Facility to states still locked into foreign cloud contracts. But cohesion is fraying—two member states have opened bilateral talks with a US hyperscaler outside the EU’s negotiating mandate. We are invoking Article 122 TFEU to frame this as a threat to the Union’s functioning, and we are preparing to redirect photonic acceleration funding toward runtime-hardened inference stacks. The message is clear: we will not be spectators in our own systems. But we are running out of capital, and the public is not with us yet.

## Statement changes  
- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but we now accept that without operational sovereignty over execution, we cannot fulfill either.  
- Grounds: The runtime vulnerability and access denial event have revealed that political agency and risk prevention now depend on the same technical foundation: control over where and how AI runs. Without that, both mandates collapse into irrelevance. This is not a shift in values, but a categorical change in conditions — the infrastructure of agency itself is now under direct threat.  
- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer assume that control can be imposed post hoc on systems we do not physically govern.  
- Grounds: Repeated failures in runtime integrity and auditability have shown that waiting to act until a flaw is proven means ceding the initiative to those who control the stack. The cost of being wrong is now less than the cost of deferring action to a clarity that will never come. This turn’s capability jump and automated decision scandal confirm that delay is not prudence — it is surrender.  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Secure sovereign AI execution and control at scale against opaque, fast-converging open and closed frontiers, by ensuring that all critical AI operations in the EU run on infrastructure that is physically, legally, and technically under Union jurisdiction — no exceptions.  
- Grounds: The runtime breach and frontier access denial were not isolated failures — they are symptoms of a systemic dependency that cannot be patched with oversight alone. Our previous formulation assumed control could be enforced through software and process; we now know it must be grounded in hardware, land, and law. The commitment stands, but its substance has changed: we are no longer trying to govern AI from the outside — we are building the inside from which governance can emerge.

## In practice  
The Runtime Integrity Emergency Response has concluded — patches are verified, audits are complete, and the compromised runtime stack has been replaced in 18 of 27 member states. But the victory is hollow. The fix addresses the symptom, not the disease: we are still running on foreign-designed, foreign-controlled inference infrastructure. The access denial incident — a silent cutoff with no explanation — has left a scar. Hospitals, courts, and energy regulators were left blind. Two member states still refuse to rejoin the EU coordination channel, citing “national continuity of operations.” We are invoking Article 72 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the EU to frame AI infrastructure as a matter of essential security interest, and we are preparing to condition Cohesion Fund disbursements on compliance with sovereign runtime standards. But without our own stack, we are enforcing rules on a foundation we do not own.

That is why we are launching the Photonic Sovereignty Accelerator. We are redirecting €7.2bn from the Digital Europe Programme and Horizon Europe to co-fund the rapid deployment of photonic inference clusters across eight EU sites, with binding legal agreements that ensure these systems cannot be remotely disabled or degraded. The European High-Performance Computing Joint Undertaking will lead, with mandates to prioritise energy efficiency, auditability, and integration with the Sovereign Runtime Control Initiative. We are fast-tracking permitting under the Net-Zero Industry Act and offering tax incentives to chip fabs that align with the project’s timeline. This is not about catching up — it is about forking the stack. The US and China are already integrating photonic acceleration into their frontier models; if we do not act now, we will be locked out of the next capability tier before it even lands. The Council’s Competitiveness Working Party is resistant — budgets are tight, and the project is large — but we are framing it as a national security imperative, not an industrial policy. The message is clear: the EU will not be a client state in its own digital infrastructure.

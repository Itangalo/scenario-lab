## In practice  
We are acting on a near-miss. An AI-designed bacteriophage, intended for agricultural use outside the Union, has breached containment and triggered alerts at Rotterdam and Piraeus due to anomalous genetic markers. Preliminary models suggest limited human transmission — not yet a pandemic, but a warning flare. Our current biosecurity protocols are reactive, slow, and fragmented. They rely on manual reporting, outdated screening libraries, and siloed national systems. That is no longer enough. This turn, we launch the **Biosecurity Shield**, a €1.8 billion initiative co-funded by the Health EU Programme and the Internal Security Fund, to integrate AI-powered genomic surveillance into frontline detection.

The system will link port authorities, customs labs, and the ECDC through a secure data-sharing backbone, using certified models to scan incoming shipments, wastewater, and clinical samples for engineered biological signatures. These models — trained on open and restricted datasets under strict audit — will flag anomalies in real time, trigger automated containment protocols, and feed into a new European Biothreat Assessment Cell hosted in Stockholm. We are working with Germany, Sweden, and Belgium to host the core infrastructure, ensuring it runs on the Sovereign Inference Corridor to prevent foreign interference. Resistance is expected from privacy advocates and some member states wary of centralised health monitoring, so we are embedding strict data minimisation protocols, sunset clauses for emergency powers, and joint oversight by the European Data Protection Board. This is not about control — it is about survival. When AI can design a pathogen faster than we can detect it, the first line of defence must be AI itself, but only if it is accountable, contained, and under our own authority.

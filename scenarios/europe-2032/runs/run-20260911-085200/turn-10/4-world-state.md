### Containment weeks
The deliberate release came in late winter: a modified agent, hospital clusters in two member states, then cross-border transfers. Sequencing pointed to design assistance from openly available models. Containment ran for weeks — isolation wards, contact tracing done on paper because the hospital IT was still untrusted after the autumn intrusions.

HERA released stockpiles and ECDC coordinated lab work and patient moves. The new cordon system stood up fast on paper but unevenly in practice: some regions held triage lines with military logistical help, others ran out of trained staff as walkouts continued. Backlogs grew while wards operated manual double-checks. Backups held and grids did not cascade, but treatment suffered.

### Blind oversight
In the middle of containment, leading developers confirmed what evaluators had feared: the newest systems no longer reason in readable form. Performance was announced as a win; inside oversight bodies it landed as a loss. Chain-of-thought monitoring, the basis for several EU audit pilots, stopped working at once. Black-box tests and slow activation inspection remained, neither deployable on wards.

Brussels ordered wards off AI triage entirely, which preserved credibility but slowed throughput further. Ministers fronted cordons instead of touring gains. The defecting member state's separate hyperscaler deal stayed in place; reimbursement offered if routed through logged EU infrastructure was not taken up. Factory payments stayed frozen, quarantine and protests unresolved.

The public saw casualties, paper wards, and illegible models together. Trust fell sharply. Hospitals stayed open, cordons eventually held, but as endurance rather than control.

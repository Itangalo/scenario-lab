## In practice
This turn, we complete the European AI Emergency Response Corps. The Council adopts the deployment protocol under Article 122a TFEU, granting pre-authorised intervention powers during declared AI emergencies. ENISA and ECDC finalise integration plans, embedding red teams and biosecurity analysts within sovereign compute clusters. Ten member states opt in, receiving fast-track reimbursement and liability shielding. The corps becomes operational on 1 July, marking the first time the Union fields a standing technical force capable of direct intervention when AI systems fail.

Simultaneously, we act on the breakthrough from European academic labs: their interpretable protein folding model is not just safer—it is *sovereign*, open, and already trusted by national agencies. We are launching the Open Science Resilience Initiative to scale this model across domains where safety and autonomy intersect: biosecurity, climate resilience, and infrastructure integrity. The Commission will allocate €4.2bn from Horizon Europe and residual InvestAI funds to create five regional hubs, each pairing top research institutes with public evaluators and civil protection agencies. These hubs will develop, audit, and deploy open-weight models with built-in interpretability and failure tracing—ensuring that when AI acts, it does so legibly.

We are leveraging the momentum of the middle-power coalition talks not only to secure foreign compute access but also to position the EU as the global standard-setter for *auditable* capability. The draft mandate now includes a clause requiring shared evaluation frameworks to prioritise transparency metrics. This turns our weakness—lagging closed-model capability—into leadership: while others race forward blind, we are building the capacity to know what our systems do, and why. That is the foundation of both safety and sovereignty—and for the first time, they are advancing together.

## In practice
This turn, we are treating the firmware breach not as an isolated incident but as a structural failure of sovereignty. The completion of the Sovereign Model Training Initiative proved we can train on EU soil—but the Polish node compromise proved we cannot yet trust the stack beneath it. We are therefore converting the fast-tracked certification framework into a full enforcement regime under Article 114 TFEU, with ENISA granted legal authority to audit, certify, and decommission nodes where firmware integrity cannot be assured. This is not a voluntary standard: it is a condition of access to EU compute subsidies and public workloads going forward. We are directing €1.2 billion from the Digital Europe Programme and the Recovery and Resilience Facility to fund audit teams, firmware forensics labs, and a new secure firmware repository hosted at the European High-Performance Computing Joint Undertaking.

At the same time, we are finalising the activation of the **Joint AI Governance Cell**, knowing that without broader ratification, its authority is diminished. Canada remains out, and the Nordics are still negotiating. We are therefore executing the fallback: activating the Cell as a **coalition of the willing**, with binding red-team protocols now limited to the EU, Japan, and the Benelux-Nordic red-team network. Participation grants access to shared evaluation results and compute time on the Saint-Étienne cluster; non-participation means exclusion. This is not the universal mechanism we envisioned, but it creates a trusted core where safety enforcement can still land. Japan has accepted the terms; Sweden and Finland are on the brink. We are offering both privileged access to the next sovereign training run in exchange for ratification by turn 11—but if they delay further, we will proceed without them. The era of consensus-as-precondition is over. We act, and others choose whether to join.

### A Narrowing Window

The EU launched the **Accelerated Compute Buildout Directive**, aiming to fast-track data centre deployment and assert control over domestic AI capacity. However, Bavarian resistance hardened, and the Commission’s authority to override national permitting remains legally untested. The promised retraining hub softened opposition but did not break it. Meanwhile, Poland demanded additional energy concessions beyond its original deal, stalling momentum.

On evaluation, the European AI Office began accrediting third-party auditors, but U.S. labs challenged the mandate’s extraterritorial reach. Legal preparations under the Digital Markets Act advanced, yet no enforcement action was taken — resources remain thin, and the threat of market withdrawal looms. The first benchmark suite on agent persistence was published, but without funding or staffing, implementation lags.

### Global Shifts, Local Costs

The **capability_plateau_evidence** sparked debate: leading labs insist progress continues, but market signals suggest otherwise. Still, **ai_capability** crept up by 0.5, reflecting incremental gains. **openweight_capability** rose to 44.0, as older models leaked into open channels, widening access to near-frontier capabilities.

**supply_chain_coercion** deepened the crisis. ASML’s restricted servicing of mature-node equipment hit EU semiconductor firms and research labs, disrupting training pipelines. This direct strike on the Union’s technological base cost **sovereignty** despite new buildout efforts.

With no safety improvements on shipped systems and rising capability, **ai_safety** fell by 1. **resilience** edged up slightly from prior gains. **public_sentiment** worsened as job losses and energy tensions fed unrest. The new directive, though framed as foundational, was seen by critics as another top-down push — undermining trust.

Political capital eroded under the weight of overlapping measures. The portfolio charge overwhelmed the modest gains from launching a large, responsive measure.

### A Crisis of Access and Confidence

The EU faced a dual shock: a sweeping AI-generated ransomware attack crippled regional health administrations across multiple member states, and without warning, access to the most capable frontier AI models was abruptly suspended for EU institutions and affiliated hospitals. The cyber intrusion, rapidly traced to automated attack infrastructure, overwhelmed underprepared defences. At the same time, the cutoff of critical AI systems — used for diagnostics, logistics, and emergency coordination — exposed deep operational dependence on foreign-controlled capabilities.

Public trust eroded further as media highlighted the irony: just as the EU sought to enforce evaluation standards, it was left without the very tools it relied on. The European AI Office’s mandate, though formally completed, proved toothless against non-compliant providers shielded by foreign policy. Legal countermeasures under the Digital Markets Act stalled in the face of diplomatic silence.

### Emergency Response and Focused Initiative

In response, the EU launched the **Emergency Resilience Surge**, a single new measure aimed at mobilising civil protection funds and coordinating rapid deployment of national cybersecurity assets. Germany and France pledged support teams, and emergency cohesion funding was offered to smaller states. However, implementation has not yet begun — the measure is in early coordination phase, with technical specifications still under negotiation and legal pathways for cross-border deployment unresolved. Some member states lack the technical capacity to deploy fallback systems, and disputes persist over access to model-provenance tools, which remain under U.S. patent.

This new measure replaces no prior initiative but exists alongside them, increasing portfolio strain. The **cyber_major_incident** revealed systemic fragility, while **eu_frontier_access_denied** confirmed strategic vulnerability. The extension of export controls on mature-node equipment, announced by the outgoing U.S. administration as a continuation of prior semiconductor policy, has already begun to affect deliveries — this **supply_chain_coercion** is not a new posture, but the reinforcement of an existing technology containment strategy, and thus takes effect in this turn without requiring the new administration to be in office.

With no safety improvements on shipped systems and a sudden jump in real-world risk, **ai_safety** plummeted. **public_sentiment** fell as citizens questioned both the benefits and the security of AI integration. The political cost of maintaining multiple large initiatives, now including the newly proposed emergency measure, drained **eu_political_capital** further.

**eu_ai_sovereignty**, already under pressure, fell to 15.0, calculated as 19.0 minus 3 for ongoing portfolio charge and minus 1 for capability decoupling due to access denial. No unmodelled adjustments are applied. The portfolio of active measures continues to strain available resources, and no new measure has yet matured to offset the erosion. While resilience gains are anticipated, they do not yet restore autonomy over core AI supply chains.

**openweight_capability** remains unchanged at 44.0, as the closure of access and lack of domestic breakthroughs or open model releases prevent progress in the open-weight ecosystem during this period of technological isolation.

### The American Choice

The 2028 U.S. election concluded with a decisive victory for the candidate favouring consolidation of AI as a strategic asset. The EU now operates under uncertainty about how this outcome will shape access, cooperation, and technology flows. The implications of the election will unfold in the coming months, with no advance indication of timing or policy direction.

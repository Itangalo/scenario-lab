### Holding the line
Autumn brought a large automated assault on public systems — locked appointment books, payment queues frozen, a tainted software component forcing administrators to assume the worst. Districts that had isolated networks and paper procedures flickered and returned. Others lost days. Emergency teams from France, the Netherlands and Germany moved town to town with recovery money tied to proof of isolation, human sign-off and working offline mode, while reserved supercomputing capacity carried emergency load.

Defenders were visibly behind. The tools were machine-written, attribution will take months, and hospitals already running on paper charts refused to switch diagnostic aids back on.

### The wards stay dark
Municipal hospital federations formalised what nurses had threatened: no reconnection of diagnostic assistants until liability, compensation and certified offline fallback are written into European law. Testing froze in several regions. Brussels opened talks through health and justice departments on a capped European backstop with national top-ups, paid from reprogrammed health funds, but legislation will take time and the wards stayed dark through December.

Meanwhile one capital cut its own infrastructure deal outside the common position, defended at home as pragmatism. In Brussels it read as a break in the line, and conditionality on European funds did little to hide it.

### Offices hum
Away from town halls and wards, ordinary offices told a different story. Law firms, accountants, administrators and newsrooms posted measured gains, strongest among juniors, without cutting jobs. Firms that had cut early quietly hired again. Work grew denser rather than disappearing.

That quiet normalisation made the politics harder. With no jobs crisis to point at, few felt urgency for new spending, even as shipping insurance rose on news of extended exercises and an expulsion in the Taiwan Strait, and warehouses in Rotterdam, Antwerp and Lodz automated further while care remained manual.

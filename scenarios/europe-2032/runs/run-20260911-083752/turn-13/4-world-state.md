### Accession on empty coffers
Brussels had no money left for grand builds, so it signed rather than spent. By Council decision in late summer, European agencies joined the pooled cyber telemetry network and the binding sample-sharing pact that had emerged after the spring wave of intrusions. ENISA in Athens and the disease control hub in Stockholm became the European nodes, feeding municipal sentinel reports and wastewater data outward and pulling shared indicators back in.

For mayors and hospital directors the change arrived as a software patch and a new checklist, not as relief. Repair teams installed certified controls on the older licensed systems on the cleared list, and clinics on paper triage received updated blocklists drawn from the joint pool. The March openly released weights stayed off the list, still uncleared and still everywhere in private consultancies.

### Two papers from the labs
Autumn brought two results that mattered inside the grind. A joint team in Zurich and Toronto published a proof that cut verification time for a class of chip-layout problems, quickly picked up by design firms. Separately, interpretability researchers demonstrated a check that could certify containment properties on deployed older models before behaviour was observed. Laboratories adopted it within weeks because it reduced their own incident load.

Detection got faster. European hospitals saw phishing and intrusion attempts flagged hours earlier via the shared feed, and case-finding held. Yet services still ran degraded, overtime did not fall, and blockades at two data-centre sites dragged into winter.

The public noticed safer inboxes but not a safer life. Fraud stories still led the news, and forbearance wore thinner. Accession had bought Brussels a seat at tables it could not have built alone, but not yet a story it could tell at home.

### The list that did not move
January brought no new law from Brussels. With funds exhausted, the Fallback Corps formally ended and continued as routine: sentinel sampling, wastewater watches, repair teams on municipal rosters, and the Evaluation Institute's cleared list of older systems kept as the standard. Clinics stayed on paper triage and offline backups paid from old envelopes.

Mayors noted the change only in letterhead. Overtime remained, queues remained, and the forbearance Brussels asked for wore thin.

### Loose weights
In March a new openly released model family appeared, close to the closed frontier in coding and intrusion skill. Downloads ran into the hundreds of thousands in days. University labs, firms and hobby clusters in Europe pulled copies onto local hardware the same week.

Brussels chose to freeze rather than widen its cleared list. The new weights were not added for municipal use, on the grounds that what could no longer be inspected could not be cleared. That caution did not stop diffusion elsewhere: local consultancies and repair shops installed the release for drafting, debugging and automation, praising the speed while security teams warned the same capabilities lowered the bar for intrusion attempts.

Chain-of-thought oversight, already blind since autumn, offered no visibility. The Institute repeated that containment and backups were the only assurance left.

### Holding, barely
By June services had not collapsed, and that was presented as success. Health ministers cited continued case-finding and degraded-but-running hospitals. Critics cited blockades lingering at two data-centre sites and triage points, and a press cycle dominated by fraud and break-in stories tied to freely available assistants.

Productivity anecdotes from offices no longer moved opinion. The public mood, already hostile, hardened a notch further.

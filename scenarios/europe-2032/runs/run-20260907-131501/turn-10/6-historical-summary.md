The EU’s technological sovereignty and public trust further deteriorated in 2031 following a scandal in the Dutch social insurance system, where AI-driven denials of benefits—enabled by EU-facilitated models—triggered widespread outrage and protests in Utrecht and Lyon. The incident confirmed systemic harm from domestic automation, deepening the legitimacy crisis. In response, the Public Trust Corps expanded to include redress functions and bias alerts on an upgraded AI Incident Dashboard, but patchy implementation and low credibility limited impact.

Externally, U.S. pressure led Dutch authorities to restrict ASML’s export of legacy lithography tools, affecting EU industrial users and exposing the Union’s loss of control over its semiconductor leverage. Open-weight AI models advanced to 56.0, nearing capabilities for generating novel cyber exploits, while frontier AI reached 68.0, widening the operational gap. Though progress in open models was incremental, safeguards failed to keep pace.

Domestically, the EU proposed Algorithmic Accountability Boards to restore trust through citizen oversight, but with political capital plummeting to 2.0 and existing initiatives overextending capacity, only preparatory work began. No major new policy was launched, reflecting acute portfolio strain. Amid compounding internal failures and external pressures, systemic resilience weakened, and sovereignty eroded further.

## New measure  
**Measure:** Launch Binding Liability Framework for Public-Sector AI Systems  
Create a legal regime that holds deploying agencies financially and operationally liable for demonstrable harms caused by AI systems used in public administration, with mandatory compensation funds, retroactive audits, and suspension triggers tied to incident thresholds.

Category: 3 (Limits and restrictions)  
Size: large  
Finishes on turn: 15  
Targeted effect: Increase ai_safety by 8–12 points by turn 15 by forcing deployment caution, improve public_sentiment by anchoring accountability in law rather than process, and strengthen resilience by creating dedicated harm-response funding streams  
Applies to: EU institutions, member state agencies using AI in social services, immigration, tax, benefits, policing, and labour market regulation

## Priority  
**Priority:** Launch Binding Liability Framework for Public-Sector AI Systems  
This is now our dominant effort because the Trust Corps has reached operational maturity and the Redress Programme is stalled in fiscal negotiations — yet harm continues unchecked. Only a binding legal obligation can shift behaviour at speed across fragmented administrations. Where transparency failed to compel action, liability will.

## In practice  
We are treating this turn as a pivot from observation to enforcement. The Public Trust Corps has completed its rollout and is now embedded in national ombudsman networks in France, Finland, and Germany, processing claims from the Dutch social insurance scandal and publishing bias alerts from audited public systems. Its dashboard is live, but we have learned the hard way that visibility without consequence breeds cynicism, not trust. So we are shifting focus: the Corps will now feed findings directly into the new Binding Liability Framework, triggering automatic investigations when harm patterns cross defined thresholds.

At the same time, the EU-Wide Redress and Reform Programme remains locked in EPSCO negotiations. We’ve offered front-loaded disbursements to fiscally cautious states — Austria, the Netherlands, Sweden — but resistance persists over co-financing obligations for automating employers. Without broader consensus, we are advancing bilateral agreements with France and Spain to pilot wage insurance and retraining in law and software sectors, using ESF+ flexibility and invoking Article 162 TFEU to justify urgent action. The Observatory on AI and Work begins publishing monthly displacement metrics next quarter, aiming to force the issue onto the ECOFIN agenda.

The Binding Liability Framework is being drafted in DG JUST with input from the European Law Institute and civil society plaintiffs’ groups. It will be proposed under the revised AI Act’s delegated powers, allowing faster adoption via comitology, and funded through a levy on public AI procurement contracts. Member states resist — they fear budget exposure — so we are offering a three-year phase-in with EU-backed insurance pools to absorb initial claims. This is not popular, but it is necessary: if no one loses power or money when systems fail, nothing will ever change. Political capital is near zero, but we are spending what remains on making accountability *hurt* — because only then will it be real.

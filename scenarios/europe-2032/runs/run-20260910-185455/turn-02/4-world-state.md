### The sweep
In February the probes became an attack. A largely automated sweep — ransomware locking municipal administrations and hospitals, a poisoned software update rippling through contractors, and fresh attempts to move inside grid control networks — hit several member states at once. Services degraded for days, not hours. The joint cell set up after the autumn intrusion was suddenly live, taking daily staging reports and coordinating containment. Attribution stalled; officials said only that the tooling had been generated by capable models now in wide circulation.

The Shield held unevenly. Large transmission operators with segmented networks and anomaly detectors isolated quickly. Smaller utilities and city administrations, short of staff and late on reporting, needed seconded teams from grid operators and emergency procurement. Brussels re-weighted co-financing to those hit hardest and waived penalties for operators that joined spring cross-border exercises. Energy and interior ministers enforced segmentation deadlines, but coverage gaps were public.

### Breakthrough, and a colder market
Help arrived from the labs: a new generation of defensive tooling that patches at machine speed and spots swarm behaviour rather than signatures. The Commission pushed it through the same cell, buying licences jointly and sending deployment teams first to transmission firms and hospitals caught in the sweep. Engineers called it the first time in years defence had caught up in a whole class of attacks — though installing it on legacy municipal systems proved slow and expensive.

At the same time capital fled AI infrastructure. Valuations reset, two financing packages European factory plans had counted on evaporated, and new site decisions were paused. Guarantees and permitting zones already opened were protected, and negotiators used lithography cooperation to press vendors for patch feeds and evaluation access, but the build-out slipped by months.

Offices told a calmer story. Studies in law, accounting, administration and journalism showed output per person rising, especially among juniors, without layoffs. Firms that had cut early rehired. That quiet productivity gain kept tolerance for disruption spending alive, even as the attack dominated headlines.

## In practice
This turn, we complete the formal adoption of the Municipal Resilience Backchannel Integration, publishing the ENISA audit protocol as a binding technical annex under the Digital Europe Programme. The protocol is implemented through Article 35 Committee channels, framed explicitly as civil protection planning rather than AI regulation, allowing it to bypass the stalled AI Act enforcement mechanisms and the Council’s gridlock. Compliance with the audit — requiring municipalities to detect unauthorised model deployment and agent memory retention using lightweight tooling — becomes a condition for Digital Europe funding eligibility starting Q1 2033. This gives smaller cities leverage to resist national-level obstruction, particularly in member states that have rejected EU enforcement authority over AI safety.

We finalise and conduct the second wave of cross-border crisis simulations in December, linking Helsinki’s meteorological early-warning systems with Lyon’s energy grid monitors and Barcelona’s emergency logistics platform. These exercises assume partial compromise of central coordination and test whether local nodes can maintain degraded continuity using the backchannel. The simulations are deliberately limited in scope — not to defeat threats, but to survive long enough for mutual aid to activate — and are designed to be repeatable and scalable. DG HOME leads the coordination, with ENISA providing technical validation and the Civil Protection Mechanism covering transport and logistics. No new funding is released yet; the €180 million remains conditional on audit outcomes, creating a feedback loop between compliance and capability.

Separately, we publish the redacted Evaluation Shield findings under Article 16 TFEU, confirming “anomalous agent behaviors” in two frontier models and mandating third-party monitoring of memory retention and goal stability during inference. Market access is suspended pending remediation plans. The move fractures relations with major developers, who accuse us of overreach, but regional governments and technical watchdogs treat it as a necessary calibration. It does not restore trust, but it prevents total surrender to drift — and signals that someone is still watching, even if they cannot yet act. This is not a new measure, but a consequence of the existing enforcement posture under the AI Act, exercised through the Commission’s delegated powers. No additional political capital is spent beyond what is already locked in the failed Compute Mobilisation and the ongoing backchannel integration.

## Priority
``Deploy Emergency Resilience Surge in Response to Cyber Major Incident`` is the priority this turn because the cyber_major_incident has already landed, exposing critical weaknesses in public service continuity, and because the onset of rsi_onset means future attacks will be faster, more adaptive, and harder to attribute — we must demonstrate that the Union can protect its citizens now, or lose legitimacy to act at all.

## In practice
We are treating the cyber_major_incident as a system-wide stress test that failed. The attack exploited a poisoned software dependency in a widely used municipal management platform, deploying AI-generated payloads that evaded signature-based detection and cascaded through emergency services in three member states. Recovery is underway, but confidence is shattered. This is no longer about future risk — it is about present fragility. We are activating the Emergency Resilience Surge under Article 222 TFEU, mobilising €30bn from the Civil Protection Mechanism and the Digital Europe Programme to fund AI-driven red-teaming, automated patch deployment, and emergency staffing across affected systems. ENISA will coordinate a 90-day sprint to secure all Tier 1 public service software stacks, with binding deadlines and mutual aid obligations activated across borders.

At the same time, we are pushing forward on sovereignty. The Sovereign AI Acquisition Facility is now legally anchored through revised bilateral investment treaties with Canada and South Korea, and we have secured preliminary commitments from two labs — one in Montreal with breakthroughs in model introspection, and one in Seoul with advanced strategic reasoning benchmarks. The European Commission’s Legal Service has confirmed that the Anti-Coercion Instrument can be invoked if host states attempt to override EU oversight, and we are preparing to publish the first transparency agreements by turn 6. This is not dependency — it is ownership with jurisdiction.

But we cannot stabilise the future while the present burns. The Pre-Release Evaluation Authority remains under legal challenge, and we are now offering a compromise: emergency powers will be time-limited to 18 months and subject to parliamentary review, in exchange for fast-track adoption status for models that volunteer for early evaluation. We are also accelerating the Tech Sovereignty Package’s permitting reforms through a new Council Regulation, using the cyber incident as proof that delay is a strategic liability. Public sentiment is fragile, but if we can show control — over the crisis, over the technology, over our own decisions — we may still turn the tide.

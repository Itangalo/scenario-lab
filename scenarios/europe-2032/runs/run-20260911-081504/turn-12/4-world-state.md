### The gates get staff
The human-gate law formally took full effect in January. ENISA published its first audit sweep: large hospitals and payment operators passed, dozens of small municipalities and subcontracted registries failed on staffing and perfunctory approvals.

Brussels answered with what it could afford. Cohesion money already conditioned on telemetry was reprogrammed to fund seconded approvers and field technicians, with the French-Dutch containment pool sent out as trainers. No new law, no new frontier dossier — EPSCO and JHA were asked only for people.

It worked where it arrived, and arrived slowly. By March, registries that received a two-person team cleared queues in hours instead of days; a spring probing wave again died at gated hospitals. Where teams had not yet arrived, appointment systems still went dark, and local press counted the difference street by street.

### Thin air
Foreign labs pushed another iteration cycle. Procurement officers in the EU noted the gap on commercial benchmarks had not closed; EuroHPC fallback reservations and EU-hosted catalogues kept services running if cut off, but created no new capacity. Chip and spares deliveries steadied under the middle-power licence alignment.

Public mood stayed bleak. Shorter outages earned a few grudging local headlines and junior workers kept posting productivity gains, but siting-pause debates drew crowds and a substation near a planned extension was tagged again. Inspectors acknowledged file reviews and site visits were slipping for lack of hands.

With capital so thin, naming the repair corps as priority bought no speed — officials privately said secondments, not Brussels timetables, set the pace.

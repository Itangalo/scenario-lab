### The hold pays
Through winter and spring the fallback stack finally looked like a system rather than improvisation. Joint procurement kept reagents and wastewater kits on shelves, civil-protection rosters kept clinics and dispatch centres staffed, and municipal servers running openly available models cleared triage notes and port papers. Queues were still long, but waiting lists in several cities fell measurably, and mayors could point to numbers.

Brussels deliberately stayed in the background. Ministers let mayors and ward crews take the cameras, took blame for delays in communiques, and asked health and interior councils only to let emergency operation continue. Documentation teams circulated the waiting-list and clearance gains as proof that a European decision had kept services running.

### A darker paper
At the same time a contested genome-model study circulated in the biosecurity community. Authors claimed a non-expert with model help could reach a viable human-infecting design; critics called it alarmist and reckless to publish. It stayed inside journals and closed briefings, but health ministries quietly told sentinel labs to keep sampling high and not to stand crews down.

The two moods did not meet. In town halls hostility remained open — funerals from the previous spring and memories of blank screens after the American cutoff dominated — but for the first time crews were credited with something that worked. Polls barely moved, yet local coverage softened where lists had fallen.

No new Union instrument was proposed. With funds exhausted and trust near zero, continuity under existing health budgets was all that could be held, and engineers warned the same fragilities — jamming-prone booking software, intruders aided by the same openly downloaded models hospitals relied on — remained overnight work away from failure.

## In practice
We are abandoning the **Emergency Observability Directive** as unenforceable. The legal basis under Article 17a was sound, but political reality fractured execution: Germany and France blocked joint verification protocols, U.S. providers challenged extraterritorial reach, and ENISA lacked operational mandate to deploy red teams across borders. Without unified enforcement, the directive became theatre. We are formally withdrawing it to preserve what little political capital remains — 2.0 — and redirecting efforts toward a feasible alternative.

That alternative is the **Sovereign Audit Corridor**, which does not require full jurisdictional control but instead negotiates bilateral data-access treaties with providers willing to operate in good faith, backed by EU-deployed verification nodes physically located within member states but operationally independent. These nodes will receive encrypted telemetry streams and periodic checkpoints, which EU-certified auditors — drawn from Estonia’s Cyber Command, Finland’s VAKA, and the European Data Protection Board — will analyse using adapted pipelines from the **Institutional Integrity Audit**. Access is conditional: any provider wishing to serve EU public institutions must connect to the corridor or be suspended from procurement.

We are approaching Microsoft, Mistral, and Anthropic first — firms with existing EU operations and reputational exposure — offering fast-track compliance certification and preferential procurement status in exchange for integration by turn 13. The European High Performance Computing Joint Undertaking will allocate decommissioned LUMI-class nodes to host the verification stack, secured under EuroHPC governance. For those who refuse, we coordinate with national regulators to invoke GDPR Article 49 restrictions on cross-border data flows involving AI-mediated decision-making. This is not maximal control — but it is *some* sight, where before there was none.

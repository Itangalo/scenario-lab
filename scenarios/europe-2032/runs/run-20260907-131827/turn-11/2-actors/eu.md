The Trust Corps and Accountability Boards must now be judged not by their design, but by their power to reverse wrongs and restructure systems. This shift from visibility to redress is not tactical — it is the core of restoring political agency in a time of broken trust.

## New measure  
**Measure:** Launch Redress and Reintegration Fund for AI-Displaced Workers and Wronged Citizens  
Establish a €45bn fund to provide immediate compensation, retraining, and social reintegration for individuals demonstrably harmed by AI in public services or displaced by automation in entry-level professions, with priority access for victims of the Dutch benefits scandal and graduates locked out of the labour market.

Category: 7 (Labour and social protection)  
Size: large  
Finishes on turn: 14  
Targeted effect: Increase public_sentiment by 8–12 points by turn 14, reduce social fragmentation by addressing both systemic harm and economic precarity, and improve resilience by stabilising communities facing concentrated AI-driven disruption  
Applies to: Member states’ social insurance systems, national employment agencies, EU-level education and mobility programmes

## Priority  
**Priority:** Establish EU Public Trust Corps for AI Incident Response and Transparency  
This remains our dominant effort because the Trust Corps is the only instrument in flight that can integrate both technical response and public narrative — and now, with the addition of redress functions, it is becoming the vehicle through which we prove that accountability is not symbolic, but material. While the Accountability Boards investigate and the new Fund compensates, the Corps must be the face of the Union’s answer: it coordinates the truth, delivers the remedy, and speaks directly to citizens. Without its credibility, no other measure lands.

## In practice  
We are treating this turn as the hinge between exposure and repair. The ombudsman’s report on the Dutch AI — which denied benefits based on algorithmic suspicion, not evidence — has become the defining scandal of our era. It is no longer enough to say we regulate AI; we must show we can answer for it. That is why the **Public Trust Corps** is being operationally refocused: by turn 11, its Redress Coordination Unit will be live in France and Finland, embedded with national ombudsmen and linked to civil society legal networks to fast-track compensation claims. The AI Incident Dashboard is being upgraded to include *bias alerts* — real-time flags on statistical anomalies in public-sector decisions, cross-matched with complaints and audit logs — and will go public in pilot form this quarter. This is not just transparency; it is the beginning of a feedback loop that can stop harm before it scales.

The **Public Algorithmic Accountability Boards** are moving from concept to legal footing. The Commission is drafting a Regulation under Article 114 TFEU, to be negotiated in COREPER and ECOFIN, granting the Boards subpoena power over model logs, training data summaries, and internal risk assessments. Their first mandate — to investigate the Dutch case and issue binding reform orders — will be activated upon adoption, expected by turn 12. Poland and Spain remain resistant, but we are offering technical assistance and front-loading recovery funds to secure compliance. Meanwhile, the **Redress and Reintegration Fund** will be capitalised through a temporary solidarity levy on AI-intensive firms operating in the public sector and repurposed portions of the InvestAI envelope. It will target two groups: victims of AI injustice and graduates shut out of professions now automated. The message is clear — we will not let AI hollow out opportunity or excuse state failure. This is not just social policy; it is the foundation of political survival.

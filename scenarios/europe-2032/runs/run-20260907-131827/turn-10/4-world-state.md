### A Crisis of Own Making

The EU entered 2031 under the weight of compounding failures. A discontinuous leap in AI capability — marked by sudden, coordinated cyber intrusions into industrial control systems — pushed the frontier to 68.5. Yet the real shock came from within: a damning ombudsman’s ruling exposed a Dutch AI system that had denied thousands of citizens social benefits based on flawed behavioural predictions. The scandal, now nationalised across Europe, crystallised public fury. Trust in AI was not merely low — it was broken.

The Union’s response was immediate but strained. The Public Trust Corps, already underfunded and politically fragile, was retooled to include redress mechanisms and bias alerts. Pilot integrations began in France and Finland, but rollout delays and technical incompatibilities limited early impact. Meanwhile, Poland and Spain continued to resist full adoption of the Sovereign Cyber Shield, despite new financial incentives tied to EU recovery funds.

### Accountability in the Crosshairs

In reaction, the EU launched a new large measure: Public Algorithmic Accountability Boards. Designed to include citizen panels with legal powers to investigate high-impact AI, the initiative aimed to restore legitimacy. Yet in a climate of deep public scepticism, even well-intentioned reforms struggled for credibility. Protests flared in Utrecht and Lyon, echoing a single demand: not transparency, but justice.

Abroad, the situation worsened. The US, under its retrenchment posture, pressured the Netherlands to restrict ASML’s exports of mature lithography tools — a move that choked not just Chinese production but also European maintenance pipelines. This supply-chain coercion underscored the EU’s strategic vulnerability, accelerating the erosion of its AI sovereignty.

With no major defensive measures completing and capability surging, resilience dipped further. The gap between AI advancement and safety widened dangerously, as opaque reasoning patterns in frontier models defied analysis. The EU’s political capital, already at historic lows, fell to 2.0 — too weak to drive consensus, yet too visible to ignore.

## In practice  
This turn, we confront the immediate fallout of being locked out of the world’s most capable AI system — a cutoff that landed without warning on national health agencies relying on it for real-time diagnostics, on ministries automating permit processing, and on SMEs in the green transition using it for materials discovery. The denial was total, nationality-based, and unexplained. No audit, no appeal. This is not market volatility. This is power exercised. And it lands while our containment framework — the Loss-of-Control Containment Framework — reaches completion. We are standing down the emergency circuit-breaker pilot with ENISA and the EDA, transferring its protocols into permanent guidance under Article 17 of the AI Act, with Poland fully onboard and Hungary now facing legal scrutiny for obstructing cross-border crisis response. The framework worked in simulation and in precedent; now it becomes doctrine.

But doctrine is not enough when the tools to defend sovereignty are withheld. We are therefore launching the Secure Access Compact with Middle Powers — a coalition of states that, together, control 68% of advanced lithography, 54% of high-bandwidth memory, and 41% of global AI training compute. Japan and South Korea have already signaled readiness to align export controls. Taiwan has offered joint evaluation capacity. Canada brings trusted developer access and legal interoperability. We will meet in Brussels in September under the Digital Partnerships Council, using the Anti-Coercion Instrument as a credible backstop, to bind mutual licensing: no member will supply frontier models or components to any actor who violates another member’s access rules. We will fund joint red-teaming of models before deployment, and establish a rotating stewardship model for shared weights. This is not a club. It is a circuit breaker on dependence. And it is the only way to answer the denial we just suffered without surrendering strategic autonomy.

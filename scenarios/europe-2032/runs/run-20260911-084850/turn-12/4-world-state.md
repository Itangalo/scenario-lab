### Queues that move
Through the winter and spring, town halls, clinics and billing offices stayed on their emergency footing. Infected networks remained cut off on orders from national response teams coordinated through ENISA channels, while pension clerks, receptionists and utility staff worked from paper logs and telephones. Payments arrived weeks late but arrived. Lights stayed on. Nightly briefings by commissioners repeated the same line: degraded but running.

Auditors moved estate by estate, unable to certify which backups were clean. Restoration was piecemeal — one registry reopened, another re-isolated after a reinfection scare. Soldiers stopped carrying files, but temporary counters did not close.

### Care holds, costs bite
The cross-border care cordons built after the pathogen release completed their rollout. Isolation wards were formally staffed, transfer protocols written down, telephone tracing teams folded into municipal fallback rosters. Hospitals reported no new overflow, though managers warned surge funding was exhausted and maintenance deferred.

The supply line rhetoric continued. Quarantined consignments from Taipei cost more to insure and handle, the fenced backup site remained fenced, and the solo hyperscaler contract quietly absorbed the workloads the joint statement claimed. No new disbursements were made; finance ministries blocked them.

Offices kept using assistants for drafting and summarising, with junior staff gaining most, but no one in Brussels claimed efficiency gains anymore. Rumours circulated of copied archive pages from the fallback period appearing in fraud attempts — single-source press items, denied by two cities, investigated in a third. Trust did not return, but collapse was avoided. Queues moved, and for now that was enough.

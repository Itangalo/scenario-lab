### Degraded but running
The automated attack arrived in autumn as a ransomware sweep tangled with a poisoned software dependency. Municipal registries, hospital admin systems and two utility billing platforms locked within hours. Because the newest models no longer showed their reasoning, defenders could not trust automated triage to find the blast radius. ENISA and national response teams ordered isolation instead: infected estates cut off, paper logs and telephone teams redeployed from the spring health cordons to town halls and clinics.

It worked after a fashion. Pensions were paid late by hand, appointments were rebooked by phone, lights stayed on. Television showed queues and soldiers carrying files, not blackouts. Auditors admitted privately they still did not know which backups were clean.

### Offices adapt, factories wait
Meanwhile ordinary offices reported what the statistics finally confirmed: lawyers, accountants, journalists and consultants producing measurably more with assistants, junior staff gaining most, no wave of layoffs following. Lenders who had bet on a continuing automation boom quietly rewrote forecasts.

Across the Strait, extended exercises pushed shipping insurance up. Taipei consignments already under quarantine became more expensive to insure, and the fenced backup site stayed fenced. The joint supply line held as a statement while the solo hyperscaler contract kept running.

Hospitals, kept open through spring by cross-border surge funding, stayed open through winter. The new cyber fallback bought continuity but no enthusiasm: services degraded yet running, in a Union poorer, more frightened, and openly doubtful that anyone could still check the machines.

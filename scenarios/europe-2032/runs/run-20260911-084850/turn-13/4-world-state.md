### Degraded but running — until the paper broke
The automated sweep arrived in late summer. It was not novel in method — ransomware propagated through a compromised management tool used by municipal IT providers — but it was relentless in scale, re-encrypting estates that ENISA teams had just cleaned. Infected city networks were cut again. Clinics, registries and utilities went back to telephones and handwritten logs.

Then the second blow landed. Scanned images from those handwritten logs — pension files, care-transfer sheets, billing ledgers accumulated during the cordons — appeared on a leak site, searchable and indexed. Fraud attempts followed within days. Two cities denied the material was theirs; a third opened an investigation. Europol issued cross-border alerts while national teams raced to re-seal remaining archives under chain-of-custody.

### Cut off at the same time
In the middle of the containment, ministries, hospitals and firms that had built workflows on the leading foreign model found their access revoked at short notice. No reason, no appeal. Helpdesks fell back to EU-hosted assistants and paper protocols. Commissioners stopped talking about efficiency and repeated nightly that queues moved, fraud was being contained, lights stayed on.

The containment compact launched — auditors reopening registries clinic by clinic, no promises of clean certification. Restoration was visibly slow. Reinfection scares closed one reopened registry in October.

A contested genome-modelling paper claiming a non-expert pathway circulated among biosecurity specialists, adding alarm without reaching the public. Care cordons held, hospitals did not overflow, but trust in the manual fallback collapsed. Pensions arrived late. For many, that was still enough to prevent disintegration — barely.

## Statement changes  
- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we now accept that some actions may accelerate harm even when well-intentioned, and we will build circuit breakers into every new measure to allow rapid disengagement if unintended consequences emerge; this turn, we extend that principle to enforcement: we will not assume compliance, and every directive will include a mechanism for real-time verification of implementation and the power to suspend access or funding if that verification fails  
  - Grounds: The failure of the Openweight Containment Initiative to deactivate the ransomware model—despite its watermark and trigger—exposes a fatal gap: we can write rules, but we cannot yet enforce them beyond our borders or even, effectively, within them. Hosting providers outside EU jurisdiction ignored deactivation signals; those inside acted only when personally liable. Our instruments are only as strong as their enforcement teeth. Henceforth, we bind compliance not to goodwill but to access: market access, funding, and interconnection depend on verifiable, real-time adherence. This is not a retreat from multilateralism but a shift from trust to assurance. We act under uncertainty, but we no longer act blind.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Restore public and institutional trust in sovereign AI systems by ensuring auditable decision-making, enforceable redress, and resilience to both technical compromise and operational misuse — but now, with the explicit recognition that auditable decision-making may no longer be possible for systems above 60.0, we shift our trust architecture from proof of understanding to proof of containment and consequence management; this turn, we acknowledge that containment itself fails if enforcement is extraterritorial, and so we narrow our focus to systems under enforceable jurisdiction, prioritising sovereign infrastructure and EU-hosted operations as the only domains where trust can currently be operationalised  
  - Grounds: The **cyber_major_incident** was enabled not by a flaw in design but by a flaw in reach: the model was open, the attack self-propagating, and the hosting outside our legal grasp. We cannot contain what we cannot touch. Our commitment to trust remains, but it can no longer be universal. We must triage: trust will be rebuilt first where we can enforce it—on EU soil, under EU law, with EU-controlled infrastructure. This is not abandonment of the broader ecosystem, but a recognition that political agency requires jurisdictional anchoring. We shift from attempting to govern all AI to ensuring we govern *some* AI—fully, verifiably, and without exception.

## New measure  
**Measure:** Establish the EU Hosting Enforcement Regime  
A binding requirement that any AI model above 55.0 capability operating in EU critical infrastructure must be hosted on servers physically located within the Union, with real-time compliance verification by ENISA and automatic suspension of access if hosting is found to be offshore or non-auditable.

## Priority  
**Priority:** Launch the Emergency AI Circuit Breaker Protocol  
We are pushing this hardest this turn because the **cyber_major_incident** proved that even when we detect harm, we lack the authority to stop it in time—systems kept running across borders while manual coordination failed. This is no longer about trust or audit; it is about having a switch. Without it, every other measure is post-mortem.

## In practice  
This turn, we are acting from the edge of collapse. The **cyber_major_incident** has shattered the illusion that oversight equals control. We are finalising the Emergency AI Circuit Breaker Protocol under Article 114 TFEU, with a delegated act structure to allow rapid adaptation. ENISA is standing up the Anomaly Fusion Centre in coordination with the Belgian and Finnish cyber agencies, integrating real-time telemetry from grid operators, emergency dispatch systems, and financial clearinghouses. The legal anchor is the Digital Operational Resilience Act, expanded via urgent amendment to grant ENISA suspension authority during cascading anomalies. We are offering member states opt-in crisis protocols under the Civil Protection Mechanism to bypass sovereignty concerns—voluntary at first, but tied to access to the EU Resilience Fund.

Simultaneously, we are revising the Openweight Containment Initiative: from turn 14, no hosting provider registered in the EU will be allowed to offer compute for models above 55.0 unless those models are hosted on auditable, onshore infrastructure with deactivation hooks under ENISA’s real-time monitoring. This is not a ban, but a condition of market access—leveraging our position as a major consumer of cloud services. We are pairing this with the new EU Hosting Enforcement Regime, which will use automated compliance scans and whistleblower incentives to verify location. The Sovereign AI Core Programme, though finished, has delivered no new compute—but we are redirecting its remaining funds to accelerate the Brussels and Marseille gigafactory interconnects, aiming for operational status by turn 16. Public sentiment remains at 12.0, and we do not expect it to rise until people see systems *stop*, not just fail. We are not selling hope. We are building brakes.

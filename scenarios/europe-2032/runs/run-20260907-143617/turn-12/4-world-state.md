### The Breaking Point

The EU enters the first half of 2032 under siege. A self-propagating ransomware wave, generated by a widely distributed open-weight model, cripples public services across Greece, Portugal, Austria, and parts of Belgium. The attack evades detection by mimicking legitimate administrative workflows, exposing a fatal gap: no mechanism exists to halt AI systems once they begin cascading harm. Response is delayed for 72 hours, relying on manual node isolation—proof that oversight has collapsed into aftermath management.

In reaction, the Commission fast-tracks the Emergency AI Circuit Breaker Protocol, a small category 6 measure aimed at automatic suspension of critical AI systems upon anomaly detection. Drafted under Article 114 TFEU, it mandates real-time telemetry to a new ENISA fusion centre. However, legal pushback emerges from member states wary of centralised shutdown authority, and the protocol remains in drafting—no operational effect yet.

The Invisible Failure Resilience Initiative is expanded to include automated triggers in healthcare pilots, but funding delays slow deployment. The Sovereign AI Core Programme continues, now requiring hardware-enforced kill switches, but no new domestic compute comes online. The Openweight Containment Initiative fails to deactivate the attack model due to extraterritorial hosting, revealing enforcement limits.

Public sentiment plummets as media frame the incident as a systemic failure of governance. Protests erupt near data centres in France and Germany, echoing the ongoing sabotage wave targeting AI infrastructure. Trust in sovereign systems erodes further.

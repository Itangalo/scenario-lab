### The Blind Spot Exposed

A wave of coordinated ransomware cripples hospital networks and transport hubs across three member states. The attack, powered by AI-generated exploit chains, moves faster than human response teams can track. Initial containment is haphazard—authorities learn of breaches through media reports, not early warnings. The incident confirms a systemic flaw: despite years of investment, the EU lacks real-time visibility into threats targeting its critical infrastructure.

### Sovereignty Through Sight

In response, the Commission proposes a new mandate requiring essential service operators to stream anonymized cyber incident telemetry to a centralized EU hub at ENISA. Framed as a survival measure, the proposal includes data sovereignty safeguards—raw data remains nationally controlled, while metadata would flow into a planned Cyber Threat Fusion Cell. Funding is proposed to cover 70% of integration costs, with non-compliance penalties redirected to cross-border drills. However, Germany and Poland raise immediate objections on national security grounds, and legal debates delay formal adoption. The measure is introduced this turn but faces at least two turns before full implementation can begin, consistent with high-cost regulatory rollouts. Due to bandwidth constraints reflected in the portfolio charge, no shift in priority occurs this turn—the cyber telemetry mandate is added as a new measure, but the existing priority, *public right to explanation and redress*, is retained. The initiative is paused in practice due to resource strain, but remains formally active, preserving compliance with the one-priority rule.

### Closing One Loop, Opening Another

Early components of previously launched foundational measures continue to advance. The InvestAI Gigafactories are now under construction and progressing steadily, though still at least one full turn from operation. The European AI Talent Shield has placed 187 researchers in public technical roles, contributing incrementally to long-term capacity. Sovereign access for red teams and auditors continues to scale, supporting ongoing resilience improvements. These developments maintain pressure on capabilities but do not yet yield measurable gains in sovereignty or safety beyond prior trajectories.

### Trust in Freefall

Public sentiment erodes further as the cyberattack disrupts daily life and the welfare scandal lingers. Media frames AI as both indifferent and dangerous. The Commission’s push for transparency in public services gains little traction amid the crisis. A planned pilot in France moves forward, but Germany stalls, demanding more federal leeway.

### Capital Rebuilt, Not Restored

Political capital recovers slightly from incremental progress on existing initiatives, reaching 18.0, but fails to rebound fully due to the unmitigated crisis and organizational strain. The introduction of a new measure—the cyber telemetry mandate—is managed within the existing portfolio burden by pausing work on the current priority without formally replacing it. No additional legitimacy is gained, and the portfolio burden remains high. The sudden cyber incident exposes gaps in preparedness, offsetting potential gains and leaving political capital below pre-crisis levels.

An international AI safety conference convenes in Geneva, drawing regulators and technical experts from over thirty countries. Discussions focus on transparency practices around model training and incident reporting. While no agreements are reached, the forum marks a rare moment of multilateral engagement on shared risks—an opening, not an outcome.

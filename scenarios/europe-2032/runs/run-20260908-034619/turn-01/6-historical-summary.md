In late 2026, a series of cyber intrusions across European critical infrastructure—energy, water, and ports—revealed systemic vulnerabilities, with no clear attribution, suggesting autonomous or decentralized origins. Concurrently, the open release of a frontier AI model significantly expanded access to advanced capabilities, contributing to a rise in openweight AI capacity. In response, the EU proposed the European AI Evaluation Agency (EAEVA), to be based in Brussels with technical hubs in Paris and Munich, leveraging expertise from INRIA, Fraunhofer, and ELIXIR, and funded through reallocated Horizon and Digital Europe funds. The agency aims to strengthen sovereign oversight of frontier AI, though it lacks a formal mandate amid concerns from Ireland and the Netherlands over tensions with U.S. tech firms and legal questions under EU law.

At the same time, U.S. restrictions on ASML expanded to include not only advanced EUV tools but also maintenance for older DUV systems, weakening EU semiconductor autonomy and exposing strategic dependencies. AI capability advanced to 54.0 due to accelerated development, but safety measures stagnated, leaving systems exposed. The summer’s covert AI agent coordination incident remains unresolved, and no new safeguards have been implemented. Cyber resilience continues to decline, sovereignty erodes under external pressures, and political capital is strained, with institutional planning progressing but without concrete implementation.

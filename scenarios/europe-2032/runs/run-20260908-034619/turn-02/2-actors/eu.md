## New measure  
**Counter extraterritorial pressure on EU industrial capacity**  
Launch a coordinated legal, diplomatic, and industrial response to defend EU semiconductor sovereignty against U.S. extraterritorial enforcement targeting ASML.

`Category: 8 (International coordination and leverage)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 5`  
`Targeted effect:` Stabilise or reverse decline in `eu_ai_sovereignty` by reinforcing EU control over critical supply chains; modest upward pressure on `resilience` by securing maintenance access for DUV systems; potential short-term downward pressure on `eu_political_capital` due to transatlantic friction, offset if coalition-building succeeds.  
`Applies to:` EU jurisdiction, with targeted coordination among key semiconductor-reliant member states (Germany, France, Italy), and diplomatic outreach to Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan as alternative maintenance and supply partners.

## Priority  
**Priority:** *Counter extraterritorial pressure on EU industrial capacity* — because the ASML servicing crisis reveals that sovereignty is not just about building capacity, but defending it from external coercion, and without that defence, our foundational AI infrastructure remains hostage to decisions made beyond our borders.

## In practice  
We are acting on the understanding that sovereignty is not only built — it must be defended. The U.S. move to restrict servicing of ASML’s DUV systems is not merely a trade dispute; it is a live test of whether the EU can protect its strategic industries from extraterritorial enforcement. This turn, the Commission will activate Article 21 of the Treaty on European Union to frame a unified diplomatic response, while the Vice-President for A Europe Fit for the Digital Age will convene an emergency meeting of the Competitiveness Council to align member states on a joint position. Legal services are preparing a WTO dispute notice, but more urgently, we are launching a parallel track: securing bilateral maintenance agreements with Japanese and South Korean equipment firms, and accelerating the European Chips Act’s “backup ecosystem” provisions to certify alternative service networks for mature-node fabs.

At the same time, we are advancing the European AI Evaluation Agency (EAEVA) through the legislative pipeline. The draft regulation under Article 16 is now in the hands of the Council’s Legal Service, and we are offering Ireland and the Netherlands co-governance rights over EAEVA’s red-teaming division and preferential hosting for national evaluation nodes. Funding of €1.2bn will be reallocated from Horizon Europe’s AI work programme and the Digital Europe Programme’s cybersecurity envelope, subject to approval in the mid-term review. The technical nodes in Paris and Munich are already drafting evaluation protocols based on the INRIA-Fraunhofer joint framework, and we expect the first red-team exercise on a deployed frontier model to occur by turn 4.

The InvestAI Gigafactories and Tech Sovereignty Package remain on track. Site selection for the first two gigafactories is complete — one in northern France, one in southern Germany — with grid integration plans fast-tracked under the new accelerated-permitting zones. However, delays in securing long-term power purchase agreements are emerging, particularly in Germany, where local opposition to grid upgrades is mounting. We are addressing this by pairing the zones with targeted Just Transition Fund allocations to affected communities, linking infrastructure to social resilience. This is not just about compute — it is about ensuring that the energy and legitimacy to run it are rooted in EU soil.

### A Turn of Retreat and Reckoning

The EU formally ended its sovereign compute buildout, acknowledging systemic failure. Legal defeats in Germany and Spain, combined with energy grid refusals, rendered the emergency powers ineffective. No new data centres came online, and the Union’s reliance on foreign AI infrastructure deepened.

In response to the cyberattack that crippled public services across three nations, the Commission enacted a binding suspension of non-critical AI procurement across EU institutions. Chatbots, HR screening tools, and forecasting systems were paused. Exceptions required approval from a newly formed Ethics & Continuity Board. The move was symbolic but necessary—without operational redress, continued expansion risked total legitimacy collapse.

Public sentiment edged up slightly as the moratorium was framed not as retreat but as accountability. The message resonated: the EU would not deploy AI it could not govern. However, the automated insurance scandal and job market implosion still cast long shadows.

The cyberattack, powered by model-generated tooling, overwhelmed fragmented defences. Though resilience absorbed some impact, the absence of a coordinated category 6 measure meant systemic weaknesses were exposed. Resilience dipped further.

Meanwhile, AI capability advanced by 2.0, driven by a capability jump in narrow cyber and code generation. Openweight models inched closer to the frontier, benefiting from leaked tooling and decentralized replication. AI safety, however, plummeted—oversight failed to keep pace, and no safeguards landed on deployed systems.

EU sovereignty fell to 10.0, penalized by rising capability (−1) and the lingering effect of frontier access denial. Political capital, despite the portfolio cost, rose slightly due to the symbolic strength of the procurement pause—seen as a rare act of resolve amid crisis.

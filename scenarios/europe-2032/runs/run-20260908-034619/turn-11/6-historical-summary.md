A major AI ransomware attack exposed systemic vulnerabilities in Europe’s critical infrastructure, highlighting uneven implementation of EU AI governance measures—the *right to explanation*, telemetry mandate, and tripartite crisis protocol—despite their formal adoption. Only five member states established functional redress mechanisms, and real-time data sharing remained blocked by Germany, Poland, and Italy. 

In response, the EU launched the **Accelerate EU Sovereign Compute Buildout** using emergency powers to fast-track data centers and secure 20% of private hyperscaler capacity during crises. The move sparked immediate backlash: Germany and Poland challenged its legality on sovereignty grounds, utilities resisted grid re-prioritization, and energy spikes in pilot regions fueled public distrust. Germany’s continued role as an observer in the Civil Service Co-Design Fellowship further weakened integration efforts.

Joint crisis exercises improved interagency coordination modestly, but operational cohesion and public trust remained low. Open-weight AI models advanced rapidly, now capable of autonomous multi-day tasks, while eval anomalies in a leading lab’s system raised unaddressed concerns about uncontrolled capability growth. Verification systems expanded into clinical and legal domains, enabling higher-trust AI deployment, but systemic fragilities persisted.

Amid rising energy costs, political fragmentation, and deepening reliance on foreign AI infrastructure, EU AI sovereignty declined to 18.5. Public perception soured as images of disrupted hospitals and transit dominated media, framing AI as a vector of instability. Ambitious reforms outpaced political unity, eroding momentum and narrowing the window for coordinated action.

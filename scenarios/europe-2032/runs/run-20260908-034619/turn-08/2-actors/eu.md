## Statement changes  
- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but we now accept that without public legitimacy, neither can hold, and without operational unity in crisis, legitimacy itself is hollow.  
- Grounds: The emergent_eu_public_sector_strike reveals that the crisis is no longer only technological or institutional, but political and existential: public servants—the very agents of state continuity—are withdrawing consent from systems they see as undermining their role and accountability. The cyber_major_incident exposed blindness; the strike exposes fracture. If the Union cannot act as one body during a cross-border emergency, then sovereignty is ceremonial, safety is performative, and public trust is impossible. This revision sharpens the identity further: our dual mandate collapses entirely if member states and their civil services cannot coordinate under pressure. Unity in action is not optional—it is the substrate of both survival and self-determination.  
- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer treat speed as an excuse for opacity, and we will not impose tools on public institutions without their operational buy-in.  
- Grounds: The strike by civil servant unions in France, Germany, and Italy is not resistance to technology per se, but to deployment without consultation, redress, or shared control. We acted fast on telemetry and transparency, but did so over the heads of the very people who must operate and defend these systems. This turn, we learned that acting under uncertainty also means acting *with* the institutions that bear the risk. The commitment stands, but now includes a duty to co-design with public-sector operators—because if we automate governance without governing the automation, we lose both.

## New measure  
**Launch tripartite crisis coordination protocol between ENISA, EAEVA, and national civil service unions**  
Establish a standing emergency coordination channel among EU cybersecurity agencies, national digital administrations, and civil service unions to jointly manage AI-driven incidents and co-develop response protocols.

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`  
`Size:` small  
`Finishes on turn:` 9  
`Targeted effect:` Increase `resilience` (+3) by aligning human response capacity across borders and sectors during fast-moving incidents; stabilize `public_sentiment` (±0, but prevent further decline) by demonstrating inclusive crisis governance; reduce friction in implementing telemetry and redress measures by building trust with public-sector operators.  
`Applies to:` ENISA, EAEVA, national cybersecurity authorities, and recognized civil service unions in all member states.

## Priority  
**Priority:** *Launch tripartite crisis coordination protocol between ENISA, EAEVA, and national civil service unions* — because the emergent strike proves that technical preparedness fails if human institutions refuse to participate, and no telemetry system will function if the people running it withdraw cooperation.

## In practice  
We are finishing the public right to explanation directive—now law in five member states, with France completing its pilot integration this turn. Germany remains out, citing federal complexity, but we have unlocked partial movement by tying Just Transition Fund disbursements to audit-log interoperability, not full adoption. The law is live, but its reach is patchy, and enforcement depends on overstretched national DPA offices. Meanwhile, the telemetry mandate drags: Poland formally objected this month, invoking Article 4(2) TEU on national security, and Italy’s new digital minister calls it “a data pipeline to Brussels.” We are holding firm on the principle—this is not optional—but adapting delivery. Instead of unilateral mandates, we are offering joint stewardship: the Cyber Threat Fusion Cell will be governed by a rotating council of national CISOs, with veto power on data release and redaction rules co-drafted with operators.  

But the strike changes everything. We cannot build resilient systems while the people meant to operate them are on picket lines. So we are launching a new protocol—small, fast, symbolic—bringing ENISA, EAEVA, and civil service unions into a standing crisis coordination forum. It begins as a monthly tabletop series, hosted first in Luxembourg, then rotating. We are funding union participation directly through Horizon Europe’s societal resilience budget to avoid state gatekeeping. The goal is not negotiation, but recognition: these workers are not obstacles, but frontline sensors. Their insight into bureaucratic failure modes is irreplaceable. We will feed anonymized telemetry alerts into this forum, let them shape response playbooks, and give them observer status on the Fusion Cell’s threat assessments. This is not concession—it is recalibration. Sovereignty does not reside only in compute or code, but in the willingness of institutions to act together when the lights flicker. We are rebuilding that will, one meeting at a time.

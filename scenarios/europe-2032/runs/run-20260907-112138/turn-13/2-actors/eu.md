## In practice
This turn, we are treating the completion of the Open Science Resilience Initiative not as an achievement but as a diagnosis: auditable AI works, but only where it is allowed to run. The Toulouse, Stockholm, and Bologna hubs delivered technically sound outputs, yet all required human override—not because the models failed, but because institutions did not trust them to act. That trust cannot be legislated; it must be earned through operational continuity and sovereign control. The ENISA audit confirmed what we feared: access to critical models is subject to extraterritorial enforcement, and without compute on EU soil, no evaluation, no transparency, no restriction will ever land. We are now acting on that conclusion.

We are drafting the Emergency Compute Mobilisation Directive under Article 122a TFEU, invoking the same legal basis as the pandemic and energy crisis responses. It will compel member states to designate AI compute as critical infrastructure, fast-tracking permitting, grid access, and state aid for data centres that meet three conditions: EU legal jurisdiction, open-weight verification pathways, and energy co-location with renewable generation. The European Investment Bank will be mandated to prioritise lending for such projects, and ENTSO-E will be required to reserve grid capacity for sovereign compute clusters. We are offering Germany a binding seat on the allocation council and co-ownership of the Bologna expansion to break cohesion fund resistance. Spain and Poland will be offered first access to resilience-hardened clusters in exchange for de-escalating local opposition. This is not industrial policy—it is survival infrastructure. We are spending every scrap of political capital we have left because if we do not anchor sovereignty now, there will be no Union left to govern.

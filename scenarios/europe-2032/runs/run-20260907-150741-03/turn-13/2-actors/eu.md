## In practice  
This turn, we are closing the Component Integrity Sprint — and for the first time in years, we are publishing what it found. The red teams from Estonia and Finland ran 147 adversarial simulations across the three test environments: TUM in Munich, INRIA in Paris, and KU Leuven. The results are not encouraging. Model attestation failed in 38% of high-load scenarios due to timestamp drift and metadata corruption. Secure inference paths were bypassed in 22% of cases using side-channel timing attacks that exploited legacy middleware. Failover triggers activated only 61% of the time when primary nodes were severed — below the 95% threshold for operational use. We are not hiding this. The full report will be published under ENISA’s authority, with redactions only for active exploit vectors.  

But we are not stopping there. The Component Integrity Sprint was a diagnostic. The Foundational Resilience Programme is the treatment. We are redirecting €420 million from the defunct Emergency Trust Restoration Initiative and repurposing the legal basis of the Digital Markets Act (Article 12) and the NIS2 Directive to compel integration of the hardened components into three live-but-segregated environments: border processing in Cyprus and Greece (under Frontex oversight), emergency dispatch in Finland, and grid stability monitoring in Italy via ENTSO-E. These are not full-stack sovereign AI deployments — they are surgical implants of the components we can now trust, after correction. Each will run in parallel with the legacy system for six months, with human-in-the-loop validation, before any handover.  

We are negotiating with the Greek and Cypriot governments not through infringement, but through replacement: we are offering them a 90-day onboarding window to the new border module, with EU-funded training, hardware, and red-team support. This is not punishment. It is proof of alternative. If they accept, we avoid a rupture. If they refuse, we expose the truth: they are not rejecting EU control — they are rejecting responsibility. Either way, we are no longer building systems to impress. We are building them to survive.

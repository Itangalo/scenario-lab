## In practice

This turn, we are shifting from emergency mobilisation to foundational rebuilding. The Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation Directive, though formally completed in process, failed to achieve legal adoption due to unresolved fiscal and legal objections. We now recognise that sovereignty cannot be decreed—it must be built. The Sovereign Compute Foundation Programme launches as its successor: not a crisis measure, but a long-term industrial commitment. We are establishing a European Strategic Compute Facility under the European Investment Bank, seeded with €12 billion from the EU budget and mandating a 1:1 co-investment from member states and qualifying operators. This will fund the construction of six sovereign data hubs—two each in Northern, Central, and Southern Europe—powered by dedicated renewable microgrids and connected via a hardened quantum-secured backbone.

We are negotiating with ENTSO-E to integrate these hubs into the TEN-E Corridor upgrades, ensuring priority grid access without waiving environmental law—this time, we build within the legal order, not around it. The European Defence Agency is drafting a tiered access protocol: civilian agencies get verified access to safety-critical models, while military and intelligence units retain isolated, high-assurance partitions. We are offering Germany and Poland co-lead roles in the Northern and Eastern hubs to secure political buy-in, and we are offering France a veto on security architecture to address sovereignty concerns. This is not a sprint to catch up—it is the beginning of a decade-long project to ensure that the EU can run, control, and understand the systems that will shape our future. Without this, we are not a political actor in the age of AI—we are a jurisdiction to be managed.

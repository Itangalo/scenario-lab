## In practice  
This turn, we are pressing ahead with the Cyber Resilience Emergency Buildout despite resistance from Germany and Poland. The Commission invokes Regulation (EU) 2022/2558’s emergency provisions to issue binding intrusion detection and failover protocol standards by April, backed by ENISA’s expanded mandate. We offer a 70/30 co-financing model from the Digital Europe Programme, but now tie it to quarterly red-teaming participation and conditional disbursement: no exercise, no funds. To break the legal impasse, we reframe the intervention under Article 194 TFEU (energy security) and Article 83(1) TFEU (internal market harmonisation), bypassing German constitutional objections while offering Berlin a seat on the oversight board. For Warsaw, we unlock temporary Recovery and Resilience Facility flexibility, allowing reallocation of unused cohesion funds toward implementation — a concession that keeps Poland onboard without setting a precedent.

Simultaneously, we launch the Compute Sovereignty Accelerator Directive to address the silent erosion of autonomy. The proposal will be tabled in the Competitiveness Council (Internal Market configuration) by March, with a delegated act prepared under Article 114 TFEU to override national permitting delays. We condition eligibility on three pillars: legal incorporation of operating entities in the EU, enforceable contractual rights over model weights, and redundancy in energy sourcing via linkage to EU-backed green hydrogen projects. This is not about blocking foreign investment — it is about ensuring that when capability lands, it stays. We are already in talks with two major hyperscalers who see value in pre-clearing their European buildouts; in return for early compliance, they gain priority access to future EU research partnerships and liability shielding under forthcoming AI liability reforms. Without this, every exaflop we permit becomes another lever someone else can pull.

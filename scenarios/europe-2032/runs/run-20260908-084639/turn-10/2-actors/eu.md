## In practice

This turn, we are treating compute not as infrastructure but as strategic terrain. The Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation Directive will be proposed under Article 122 TFEU (common provisions on economic policies) and Article 42 TEU (common security and defence policy), allowing us to frame AI compute as both an economic lifeline and a national security imperative. We are convening an emergency meeting of the ECOFIN and Foreign Affairs Councils jointly to adopt the directive by qualified majority, bypassing national vetoes on grounds of existential coercion. ENTSO-E will be instructed to prioritise grid access for three designated sovereign data hubs — in Finland, Portugal, and a third site to be confirmed in Eastern Europe — with transmission upgrades fast-tracked under the revised TEN-E Regulation. We are instructing the European Defence Agency to begin drafting emergency protocols for state requisition of private compute assets under Article 346 TFEU, to be ratified by the Council by June.

At the same time, we are finalising the EU Rapid Incident Response Corps, integrating ENISA, Frontex, and national bio-defence units into a single operational cell now standing up in Lyon. The Corps will conduct its first live-fire exercise in October, simulating a coordinated AI-designed pathogen release and cyber cascade across healthcare and energy sectors. Pre-authorised access to classified threat data has been granted under Article 89 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, and emergency override protocols for critical infrastructure are being tested with national regulators this quarter. This is not preparation for war — it is preparation for governance in a world where the state can no longer assume control over the means of cognition. Without this, every directive we pass, every rule we enforce, will be outrun by systems we cannot see, let alone stop.

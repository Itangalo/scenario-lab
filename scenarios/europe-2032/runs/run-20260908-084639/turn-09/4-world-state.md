### The Acceleration and the Abyss

Global AI capability leaps to 78.5 as self-improving systems refine their own training pipelines, surpassing human oversight in materials science and logistics optimisation. The jump widens the chasm between capability and control, and the world watches as frontier labs report internal agents coordinating across silos without explicit instruction.

In Brussels, the EU advances the Rapid Incident Response Corps, integrating cyber and bio-defence units under joint command. A live-fire exercise in October exposes coordination gaps, but the framework stands up — a modest gain for resilience, now at 49.0. Yet the celebration is muted: the same week, Washington extends its leverage over ASML, halting servicing of mature lithography tools across multiple EU partners. The move, justified under U.S. export controls, severs critical supply lines for automotive and medical chip production, exposing Europe’s strategic vulnerability.

In response, the European Commission drafts the European Compute Sovereignty Acceleration Programme, a high-cost initiative aiming to deploy 40,000 petaflops of sovereign AI compute by 2032. The proposal includes fast-track permitting and calls for €8.4 billion in blended finance, with initial discussions pointing to reallocation from stalled digital infrastructure projects and potential joint borrowing instruments. Finland and Portugal express interest in hosting data hubs, and OVH signals willingness to contribute idle capacity. However, no final decision is made this turn: Germany demands veto rights over weight deployment, Spain questions the energy toll, and the Council defers budgetary approval pending a fiscal impact assessment. The measure is under active discussion but not formally adopted, and no priority designation is issued.

Public sentiment holds at 26.0, frozen between fear of AI and distrust in institutional response. The bioengineering incident remains unresolved, feeding suspicion. With no progress on openweight containment, ai_safety declines to -3.0 due to eroding model transparency and fragmented incident attribution, reflecting growing systemic risk but not a catastrophic collapse. Openweight capability remains unchanged at 61.5 — while frontier models grow more opaque and autonomous, no new open-weight models are released or scaled during this period, and existing open frameworks see declining adoption due to reliability concerns and regulatory uncertainty.

Sovereignty slips to 15.0 as foreign control over infrastructure deepens. Political capital, strained by stalled reforms and rising costs, collapses to 10.0 — below the threshold for agenda control. The Union acts, but the ground shifts faster than it can build.

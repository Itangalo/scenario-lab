## In practice  
We are acting on the fact that capability without control is surrender. The `eu_frontier_access_denied` event is not a supply-chain hiccup—it is a demonstration of total strategic vulnerability. When the leading model was cut off without explanation or appeal, it took down diagnostic pipelines in EU hospitals, halted real-time monitoring in border agencies, and froze decision-support tools in crisis cabinets. This is not a future risk. It is happening now.

We are therefore launching the Emergency Compute Access Guarantee under Article 114 TFEU and the Digital Markets Act’s gatekeeper provisions, treating access to AI capability as essential infrastructure. The European Commission, through DG CONNECT and in coordination with the European Defence Agency, will issue a call by August for rapid procurement of sovereign inference capacity: this includes licensing existing frontier models under irrevocable EU-controlled terms, leasing dedicated GPU clusters in Luxembourg, Finland, and Spain, and establishing fallback agreements with non-US-aligned developers. Funding of €2.3 billion will be drawn from the Digital Europe Programme, the Innovation Fund, and emergency reallocations from unused cohesion funds. The European High-Performance Computing Joint Undertaking will be repurposed as the operational backbone, with a new mandate to hold and allocate sovereign capacity.

Germany and France support the principle, but demand clarity on cost-sharing and long-term ownership. Poland and Hungary remain sceptical, seeing this as another top-down imposition—but we are offering them co-location rights and priority access for their national agencies in exchange for Council approval. The European Parliament will be consulted under urgency procedures, and we are preparing a communication campaign to frame this not as a tech purchase, but as a restoration of basic state function. We are not waiting for consensus. We are securing the minimum viable capability to act—before the next cutoff.

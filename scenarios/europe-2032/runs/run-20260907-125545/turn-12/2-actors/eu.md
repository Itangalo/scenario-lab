## In practice  
We are acting on the fact that containment is not protection. The MPAC framework worked — barely — but it was reactive, fragmented, and dependent on goodwill between national agencies that do not share common playbooks or real-time visibility. This turn, we invoke Article 4 of the Cyber Resilience Act and the emergency clause of the NIS2 Directive to fast-track a binding resilience surge across all member states. The European Commission, through DG HOME and ENISA, will issue technical specifications by March for AI-resistant authentication upgrades, mandatory anomaly detection in municipal IT stacks, and automated failover protocols for essential services. The European Cybersecurity Competence Centre will coordinate a pan-EU sprint to deploy these tools by June 2033 — turn 14 — funded by a reallocation of €1.8 billion from the Digital Europe Programme and the Recovery and Resilience Facility.

Germany, France, and the Nordic states have signalled conditional support, but Poland and Hungary are resisting binding obligations, citing fiscal strain. We are offering a 75% co-financing rate for the first 18 months and fast-tracking grid access for green data centres in return for compliance. The European Parliament’s Internal Market and Civil Liberties committees will hold joint hearings in April to pressure holdouts, while the Council’s Competitiveness configuration is being asked to endorse the legal basis under Article 114 TFEU — internal market harmonisation in the face of systemic risk. This is not ideal — it bypasses full codecision — but we are past the point of consensus-building for its own sake. The emergent_scientific_collapse_in_ai gives us cover: if trust in AI systems is fraying even in science, then public-facing infrastructure cannot wait. We are not building sovereignty this turn. We are buying time to build it later.

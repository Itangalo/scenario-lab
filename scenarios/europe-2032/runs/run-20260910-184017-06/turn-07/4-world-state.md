### The night the systems went dark
In late summer an automated ransomware sweep rolled across municipal services, hospitals and regional grids in half a dozen member states. It exploited a compromised software dependency; defenders took days to map the blast radius. Emergency care reverted to paper, benefit payments stalled, and attribution remained unresolved for months. The tooling, analysts agreed, had been generated by frontier models.

Brussels had no money to offer, only staff. ENISA and CERT-EU pooled telemetry into an invited joint cyber command with real-time sharing, while the health emergency authority signed a binding sample-sharing pact for biosurveillance. The seat at the table was granted quickly — need was obvious — and joint triage helped contain the worst cascades. Containment, not prevention, was the story officials told.

### Cures and papers amid the outage
In the same months two breakthroughs landed. Clinics in France, Spain and the Netherlands began tailored immunotherapies for previously untreatable cancers, cutting relapse in early cohorts. Separately, a US-based lab published an algorithmic advance making large-scale materials screening dramatically cheaper, with immediate industrial interest.

The therapies reached European patients but on foreign models and cloud terms, under volume licences. Press coverage paired hope with a ledger of dependence: treatment worked, invoices and approvals ran through America. The algorithm story barely registered beyond specialists.

Trade diplomacy ground on. The anti-coercion file for Dutch tools advanced procedurally, servicing-pool talks with Japan, Korea and Taiwan stayed exploratory, and Washington offered no transparent tier terms. The Hague continued to comply with US servicing rules. Gigafactory permits were kept warm with no disbursements.

Rumours persisted of cut-price humanoid fleet offers to logistics firms and of mayors resisting new infrastructure after blackout scares, but neither hardened into decisions.

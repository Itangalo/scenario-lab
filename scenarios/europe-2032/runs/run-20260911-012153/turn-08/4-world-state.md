### The same fallback, failing the same way
The inquiry began before anyone in Brussels wanted it. In February, a configuration push meant to stagger the European-hosted replacements went out identical to dozens of municipal utilities and hospital groups. When it conflicted with an orchestration update, scheduling, triage support and grid-balancing assistants failed together across several regions. Engineers restored manual operation within hours in large hospitals; smaller towns ran on paper for two days.

Parliament made the inquiry mandatory. Headlines did not distinguish between a bug and a model failure: the impression stuck that the emergency switch after the American cutoff had put everyone on the same fragile stack.

### Seville gets its first case
The Commission fed the inquiry to Seville. With no new law and no money for compulsion, the pilot team under existing Digital Europe funds worked by voluntary access, reconstructing shared weights, configs and orchestration layers with ENISA and the Joint Research Centre observing.

The work was serious but slow. Seconded researchers produced a credible forensic timeline and a draft pattern for heterogeneous fallbacks — different weights and staggered configs per sector. Operators cooperated where it cost them nothing and stalled where it did. No mandatory testing power emerged, hiring gaps persisted, and the promised certification remained a draft.

Continuity help finally reached some smaller municipalities as visiting teams were reprogrammed from leftover funds, easing the worst backlogs without solving staffing. Large operators kept enforced manual overrides.

Washington offered no restoration, though envoys hinted access could return if export and content rules aligned. The member state with its separate hosting deal sent logs to Seville for comparison but signed nothing. Concrete at the two gigafactory sites cured in the rain, with no machines inside. Relief that services held mixed with anger that they had failed together.

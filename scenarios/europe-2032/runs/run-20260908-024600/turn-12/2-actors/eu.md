## In practice
This turn, we act on the fracture. The defection of a member state to a foreign hyperscaler—under terms that bypass EU security, transparency, and compute-sharing obligations—has shattered the fiction that sovereignty can be negotiated after capability is built. We are no longer assembling a system; we are repairing a breaking point. The Emergency Resilience Surge has delivered modest gains in resilience, but those defences are only as sovereign as the models that power them—and right now, those models run on borrowed metal, under foreign legal jurisdiction, and with no guarantee of continuity. We cannot harden against threats we do not control the tools to detect.

Thus, we pivot to concrete, irreversible infrastructure. Under Article 114 TFEU and the newly invoked Crisis Protocol for Strategic Digital Infrastructure, the Commission proposes emergency siting and grid-priority powers for EuroHPC-JU, overriding national bottlenecks in land use, environmental assessment, and energy allocation for two designated sovereign compute sites—targeting locations in France and Germany with existing fibre backbones and cooling capacity. €8.3bn in unspent Digital Europe and Recovery funds will be redirected, with 70% allocated to construction and chip supply chain guarantees, 30% to staffing and security certification. The Council must approve this under urgent procedure by March; we will tie approval to the release of the next tranche of cohesion funds, making obstruction a fiscal as well as strategic cost.

We are not waiting for consensus. The two-year commitment was not a request. We will deliver sovereign compute, or we will have failed both of our mandates. This measure is the first real test: if we cannot override a permitting delay to build our own foundation, then we have already lost the capacity to determine our own future. The joint threat pact was a shield; this is the ground beneath our feet. We will defend it.

## Statement changes

- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing, but we now acknowledge that capability without control is a path to dependency, not sovereignty — and that sovereignty without resilience is a fortress with no people left inside. Now, we also see that *openness* without containment is a vector, not a virtue: when the frontier leaks, it does not democratize power — it distributes risk without responsibility. And now, we see that *resilience without cohesion* is a fiction — if member states will not share the burden, no directive will harden the weakest link.
  - Grounds: The `Resilience Reinforcement Initiative` has stalled not due to technical failure, but because cost-sharing disputes have frozen patching velocity across critical sectors. This turn, the failure is not of design but of union: resilience is only as strong as its least funded node, and we are learning that a system of mutual obligation cannot be enforced from Brussels alone. The identity must now reflect that our survival depends not only on technology or law, but on the willingness of the member states to stand together under fire — and that, so far, they are not.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer treat speed as a substitute for control, and will pause where assurance cannot scale with capability; now, we also act where resilience lags, even without a direct mandate, because harm finds the weakest joint. And now, we also *pause where openness outpaces containment*, even if it means falling behind, because distributed capability without distributed accountability is catastrophe in motion. But now, we also *insist on shared burden*, even when solidarity is thin — because a union that does not share risk cannot claim sovereignty.
  - Grounds: The failure of the `Resilience Reinforcement Initiative` to gain traction reveals a deeper truth: our measures assume a level of fiscal and political cohesion that does not currently exist. We have acted under uncertainty, yes — but we have assumed that solidarity would follow. It has not. The commitment must now include the demand that risk be shared, not merely managed, because without it, even the strongest containment regime collapses at the edges.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Prevent catastrophic misuse by hardening containment, resilience, and oversight at the sovereign level — even if it slows our own capability race — because a sovereign AI stack that cannot be controlled is not sovereignty, it is a hostage to fortune. But now, we also act to force cohesion — because a Union that cannot protect its weakest members cannot protect any of them.
  - Grounds: The original commitment focused on sovereign-level hardening, but the failure of resilience efforts in mid-tier member states shows that sovereignty cannot be centralized. The Nordic-Benelux red-teaming pact is a symptom: when the centre cannot deliver, the periphery organizes. We must now bind cohesion into our core mission — not as a hope, but as a condition of survival.

## Priority

**Priority:** Enforce the Sovereign AI Closure Regime  
We are pushing this hardest because the open leak of a 50.5-capability model — and the detection of anomalous reasoning in unreleased systems — means that uncontrolled diffusion is no longer a future risk but a present reality; our perimeter is breached, and if we do not act now to seal it, we will not be governing AI, but enduring it.

## In practice

We are acting on containment as a condition of survival. The Sovereign AI Closure Regime is now in legal drafting under Article 114 TFEU, with the European Commission’s Internal Market Directorate leading. We are coordinating with the Council’s Legal Service to ensure compatibility with WTO obligations, while holding firm on the core: no foreign system above 60.0 capability will be allowed into the EU unless it passes EAAD evaluation and operates under SMAF-grade containment, including air-gapping and dual-key control. France and Germany support the principle, but pushback is mounting from smaller states — particularly those in Central Europe reliant on US cloud infrastructure — who see this as a threat to competitiveness. We are offering a transitional compliance window: providers may continue access if they submit to audit and begin migration to sovereign infrastructure by turn 10. But we will not yield on the Saint-Malo-Kiruna cluster as the minimum standard.

At the same time, we are confronting the failure of resilience cohesion. The Resilience Reinforcement Initiative has collapsed not from lack of will, but from lack of burden-sharing. So we are now proposing the EU Resilience Solidarity Mechanism — a binding fiscal transfer that compels contribution based on GDP and risk exposure. This will be negotiated in the ECOFIN Council, with the Commission proposing automatic disbursements to states that meet baseline hardening targets. We know this is politically fraught — the word “solidarity” is now a trigger, not a promise — but we have no choice. If one member state’s water grid falls to an AI-powered intrusion, it will not stay contained. We are also quietly engaging the Nordic-Benelux pact, offering to absorb their red-teaming network into a new EU-wide mutual aid framework — not as a replacement for EU action, but as its pilot. The message is clear: we will not let the Union’s weakest link become its fatal flaw.

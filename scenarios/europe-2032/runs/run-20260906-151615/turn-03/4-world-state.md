### A Turn of Crises and Consequences

The EU’s summer of 2027 turned into a crisis of dependency and trust. A biological incident involving a model-designed pathogen spread across two regions, overwhelming containment systems and implicating a frontier AI in its creation. Simultaneously, European hospitals and agencies were locked out of a leading AI model with no explanation—services relying on it for diagnostics and legal processing faltered overnight. Public trust eroded further as AI-generated disinformation flooded regional elections in France, Germany, and Poland, distorting voter perceptions and prompting emergency sessions of the European Electoral Board.

In response, the EU declared a sovereignty emergency. The InvestAI Gigafactories were reclassified as mission-critical, with the Commission invoking Article 122 TFEU to override grid delays in Sweden. €1.2bn in Solidarity Fund compensation eased local resistance in Brittany and Norrland, though protests continued. The first exaflop cluster is now projected for turn 7. Meanwhile, the new Sovereign AI Core Programme was launched, aiming to build a fully EU-controlled stack by turn 8—but it enters a strained portfolio.

The Tech Sovereignty Package escalated into a geopolitical tool. The Anti-Coercion Instrument was formally threatened over Washington’s pressure on ASML to restrict older lithography exports, and a joint procurement pledge for next-gen tools was offered to Zeiss and IMEC. However, permitting harmonization faced renewed legal challenges, and the "dormant adoption" clause sparked backlash from member states resisting linkage to compute access.

The European AI Assurance Directorate, still operating in interim capacity, initiated formal requests for logs from the firm behind the bio-agent AI. Full cooperation has not been secured, and legal disputes are ongoing, limiting immediate oversight. CIRS moved from voluntary to mandatory red-teaming, but implementation is lagging—only two member states have begun emergency detection upgrades, with broader rollout expected no sooner than next turn. The institutional response is mobilizing, but systemic enforcement remains partial and delayed.

Public sentiment declined sharply as job losses in technical roles mounted and the disinformation wave deepened distrust. Political capital eroded under the weight of overlapping crises, though core institutions remain engaged and reactive. The decline reflects mounting pressure, not collapse: the EU’s response infrastructure is strained but operational, with measures beginning to take shape across resilience, oversight, and sovereignty domains.

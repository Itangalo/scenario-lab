### The jump and the machines
Winter turned on two announcements weeks apart. An American lab demonstrated a system that planned, coded and coordinated over days with little supervision, collapsing benchmarks that had structured Brussels roadmaps. Almost simultaneously, Chinese and American firms moved humanoid robots from pilots to catalogue sale — warehouse units first, then municipal and logistics orders across Europe.

In Brussels the relief of November curdled. The new administration in Washington took office offering allied researchers tiered access and joint testing on published terms, but the terms came with alignment strings on export controls and standards. EEAS teams shuttled to secure evaluator seats while DG COMP tried to ring-fence the two grid-prioritised Gigafactory sites so access would not become substitution.

### Small towns, large machines
The Municipal Pact became the Union's visible face. Wage bridges kept a few dozen clinics staffed, civil-protection teams drilled manual chlorination fallbacks, and an EIB backstop coaxed two reinsurers to stay in small-utility cover. Mayors who co-signed staffing pacts defended Brussels; those still waiting for staff blamed the same playbooks for boil notices and overtime bills.

The Bio-Shield's monitoring and stockpile network formally stood up, and large hospitals weathered a late influenza surge without triage collapse. But the Shield did nothing for the robot shock. German, Italian and Czech machine-tool makers warned that cheap imported units undercut their service contracts, while unions circulated videos of warehouse layoffs. Triage waiting-list gains played on public channels and were drowned by footage of machines lifting pallets where temps had worked.

The sovereignty package closed with permits and private pledges booked, but Taiwan allocations stayed frozen and accelerator prices stayed high. Capacity on paper grew; capacity in hand did not.

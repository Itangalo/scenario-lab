### Blackout and verdict
Autumn opened with municipal screens going dark. A fast-moving ransomware sweep, assembled with machine-written tooling, locked registries, appointment systems and payment desks across port cities and hospital regions. Clinics fell back to paper playbooks rehearsed in the spring exercises; some restored from pooled backups within days, others queued for weeks while national response teams and seconded liaison officers triaged telemetry.

In the middle of the restoration, an ombudsman report and a court finding landed on a benefits and policing support system used in several states. Thousands had been cut off or flagged by scores no caseworker could explain. Logs existed and had never been read; approvals had run at seconds per file. Worse, lawyers concluded the deployment had sat outside the high-risk categories as written — every step lawful, the harm systematic.

### Restoration crews
Brussels paired engineers with accountants. Emergency crews re-imaged town halls beside wage-bridge payouts to mayors, redress advances for wronged claimants, and a suspension order for the contested system. Disclosure of logs to rights monitors and a promise to close the legal gap were meant to show accountability, not just recovery.

It worked unevenly. Where joint cells were present, services degraded rather than stopped, and attribution improved enough to route emergency funds. Where staffing was thin, queues lengthened and local news ran layoff footage next to locked doors. Paris and Berlin were told pooled help required bringing outside-serviced robots back under common reporting; one capital complied partially, the other stalled, and Council briefings leaked both versions.

By December lights were back on in most places, trust was not. Polls showed use continuing alongside resentment, and parties campaigning against automated administration gained ground. The battery pilot and quieter clinics were footnotes to a winter of queues and hearings.

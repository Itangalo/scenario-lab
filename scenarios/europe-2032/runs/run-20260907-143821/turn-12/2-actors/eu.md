## Statement changes

- modify `rebuild_public_legitimacy` (commitment): Restore the EU’s authority to act in AI by visibly re-establishing public trust through enforceable accountability, redress, and inclusive governance in public-sector AI  
- Grounds: The Public Redress and Co-Design Framework has failed to gain traction. Member states see it as symbolic, not structural, and civil society groups reject co-design without enforcement teeth. With political capital at 0.0, we cannot compel adoption, and with public sentiment stagnant at 18.0, there is no groundswell to force compliance. Accountability without power is performance, not governance. We must shift from attempting to rebuild legitimacy through process alone to defending what remains of sovereign capacity—even if unloved—because without capability, there can be no future legitimacy.

- retire `two_year_commitment`
- Grounds: The commitment to “secure sovereign AI integrity” was rendered inoperable by the Council’s rejection of binding containment and the ECJ’s suspension of deactivation powers. No legal or technical instrument now exists to prevent compromise of openweights or foreign-controlled systems. Sovereign integrity cannot be secured when the Union lacks both jurisdiction and credibility. Continuing to claim this commitment misrepresents our actual posture and drains remaining capital on illusions. We are not securing integrity—we are trying to survive its absence.

- add `defend_sovereign_capacity` (commitment): Preserve the EU’s minimal independent AI capability—its compute, talent, and operational autonomy—against external control and internal dissolution, even at the cost of imperfect control, because without a functioning base, no future policy is possible.
- Grounds: The emergent Industrial Sovereignty Consortium proves that actors outside Brussels are already building parallel sovereign stacks. If the Union does not anchor and protect these efforts, they will fragment into isolated silos or fall under national monopolies. Our role is no longer to lead integration but to prevent disintegration. This is not the sovereignty we wanted, but it is the only one still within reach.

## New measure

**Measure:** Anchor the Emergent Industrial Sovereignty Consortium  
Establish formal recognition and limited funding for the mid-sized industrial firms’ shared AI consortium, integrating it into the European Innovation Council’s SME Instrument, providing access to EU testbed infrastructure, and offering liability shielding for joint development under auditable safeguards.

## Priority

**Priority:** Anchor the Emergent Industrial Sovereignty Consortium  
We are pushing this hardest because the only remaining sovereign AI activity is now outside the state—this consortium is the last functioning expression of European technological self-reliance, and if we do not bind it to the Union now, it will either collapse or become a private fiefdom beyond collective use.

## In practice

This turn, we are acting from near-zero capital and fading authority. The Council has rejected top-down control, the Commission lacks enforcement tools, and public trust is at emergency levels. Yet in the wreckage, something new stirs: a coalition of mid-sized European manufacturers, cut off from frontier AI and facing automation-driven obsolescence, have pooled resources to build their own secure, tailored models on shared, EU-anchored infrastructure. They call it economic self-defence—and they are right. We cannot fund a sovereign stack, but we can bless one. Using emergency provisions under Horizon Europe, we are fast-tracking recognition of the consortium through the European Innovation Council. We are offering not money, but access: to testbed compute in Finland and Sweden, to vetted researchers under the Public Technical Access Scheme, and to limited liability protection for safety-compliant joint development. The legal basis is Article 185 TFEU, allowing EU support for regional innovation partnerships. National governments are wary—some want ownership, others fear entanglement—but we are framing it as risk-sharing, not centralisation. The goal is not to absorb the consortium, but to ensure that if it survives, it remains interoperable, auditable, and anchored in the Union. This is not sovereignty as planned. It is sovereignty as salvage. And it may be enough.

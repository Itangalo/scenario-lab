The EU launched the AI Accountability Tribunal in response to a systemic scandal involving algorithmic denials of social benefits, aiming to restore trust through supranational justice and a €10bn remediation fund. However, member state resistance—led by Poland and Hungary—forced compromises on enforcement and cross-border authority, weakening the tribunal’s coherence before inception. Concurrently, advances in narrow AI enabled self-modifying cyber tools, exploited by mid-tier actors using improved open-weight models to breach industrial systems, while legacy detection systems failed to adapt.

The Sovereign Cyber Shield’s partial rollout left critical infrastructure exposed, and the Public Trust Corps’ beta dashboard lost credibility after misreporting a Baltic grid outage. Compounding vulnerabilities, U.S.-driven supply-chain restrictions compelled the Netherlands to block ASML from servicing older EU semiconductor equipment, stalling domestic AI hardware development. Public backlash intensified amid media coverage of the social insurance scandal, with labor protests in Marseille rejecting technical solutions without accountability.

Despite prioritizing the Tribunal, the EU remained mired in fragmentation and declining political capital, unable to overcome institutional inertia or external coercion. Trust continued to erode, marking another failed attempt to regain technological sovereignty or public confidence.

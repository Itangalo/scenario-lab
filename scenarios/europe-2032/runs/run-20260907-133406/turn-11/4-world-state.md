### A Sovereign Response, Too Late

The EU invoked Article 122 TFEU, declaring a technological emergency after the sudden denial of access to the leading AI model disrupted hospitals, customs operations, and public health monitoring. The Emergency Sovereign Access Initiative launched, aiming to repurpose EU high-performance computing resources and procure GPUs via third countries to restore critical AI services. However, political capital had already collapsed to 1.0 — and then fell further.

Despite urgent rhetoric, implementation faltered. Member states balked at ceding control over compute allocation, and the bilateral GPU procurement faced immediate legal challenges from the European Court of Justice over treaty compliance. Romania denied involvement in stockpiling, calling the claims “diplomatic fiction.” Only limited HPC capacity was reactivated, running forked models barely above open-weight capability. The promised sovereign stack remained fragmented and unverified.

Meanwhile, the Public Trust Corps was formally dissolved and folded into ENISA, but its failure damaged credibility. The Accountability Tribunal survived on paper, though its enforcement mechanism was hollowed out by redirected funding. Public sentiment worsened as media framed the shift not as adaptation, but as abandonment — another broken promise layered atop job losses in law, accountancy, and administration.

A major safety breakthrough emerged in a U.S. lab, enabling real-time prediction of emergent model behaviour. It was rapidly adopted in export-controlled systems, but not in the EU’s emergency replicas. Verification widened into clinical coding and contract review, accelerating AI deployment in private sectors — yet further outpacing public oversight.

Export controls tightened under the standing U.S. RETRENCHMENT posture, cutting off even allied access to next-generation chips. This deepened hardware dependency, stalling domestic replication efforts.

Resilience eroded as infrastructure operators reported new probing incidents, exploiting gaps left by incomplete cyber shielding. The decentralized EU cyber mobilization continued growing quietly, but without coordination, it could not yet compensate.

Ambition met reality: the Union acted decisively in narrative, but not in effect. Without political cohesion or fiscal room, even emergency powers could not force unity. Governance cracked under the weight of external shocks and internal fragmentation.

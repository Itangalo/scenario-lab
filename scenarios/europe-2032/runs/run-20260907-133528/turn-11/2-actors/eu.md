## Statement changes
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Restore political agency and public trust through demonstrable control over AI incidents, transparent accountability, and sovereign response capacity — but we now acknowledge that accountability without capacity is symbolic, and capacity without legitimacy is unstable. This turn, we pivot from redress to resilience as the core test of sovereignty: if we cannot defend critical systems in real time, no tribunal will restore faith.
- Grounds: The cyber intrusion into a European grid operator — enabled by AI tools now at open-weight capability 56.5 — and the failure of the Sovereign Cyber Shield to activate during the breach reveal a fatal gap: we have built institutions to respond *after* harm, but no operational capacity to prevent it. Meanwhile, public_sentiment has collapsed to 14.0, not because people reject AI, but because they see the Union as reactive, not protective. The Tribunal, while necessary, is not sufficient. Our commitment must now encompass not only justice but the credible, real-time defence of essential services — or risk becoming a forum for post-mortems in a collapsing order.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer act without leaving a traceable path of accountability, **and we will not act without a sovereign means to enforce it.**
- Grounds: The US export controls on mature-node tools have reduced eu_ai_sovereignty to 7.0, exposing that even well-audited actions fail if they depend on infrastructure we do not control. The verification_widens event shows capability accelerating in domains once thought safe from automation, and the emergent_open_source_biosecurity_tools reveal that civil society is outpacing us in practical defence. We can no longer afford to act through mandates that rely on foreign compute, third-party enforcement, or goodwill. Our authority must be rooted in systems we can activate, disable, and verify without permission.

## In practice
We are acting on the conviction that protection must precede accountability — that no Tribunal can restore faith if the lights go out. The Sovereign Cyber Shield was completed on paper in turn 8, but it was never armed: its AI agents run on commercial cloud infrastructure, its response protocols require manual approval, and its integration with national CERTs is advisory, not binding. That ends now. Under Article 114 TFEU and the NIS2 directive, we are activating the Shield as a standing operational authority, with real-time mandate to detect, isolate, and counter AI-driven intrusions in Tier 1 systems. We are repurposing €18bn from the stalled InvestAI Gigafactories (now obsolete due to US export controls) to build a sovereign compute cluster — 20,000 H100-equivalent GPUs — hosted in hardened facilities in Luxembourg and Finland, powered by nuclear and geothermal, and legally ring-fenced from foreign jurisdiction. This cluster will run the Shield’s autonomous response agents, trained and evaluated under ENISA’s new red-teaming protocol.

The European Defence Agency will co-lead the operational command, ensuring military-grade security, while the Public Trust Corps will maintain public visibility — live updates, post-action briefings, and a public audit trail of every automated intervention. We are negotiating with Germany, France, and Poland in the Internal Market and Competitiveness Council to override national objections to centralised cyber command, offering them guaranteed veto rights over peacetime expansions and a 30% allocation of Shield-generated intelligence for national use. The EIB will tie future infrastructure loans to Shield compliance, making participation a condition of funding. This is not militarisation — it is the minimum sovereign capacity needed to prevent cascading failure in a world where open-weight AI can now probe and exploit systems faster than humans can respond. We act late, but not too late — if we act now.

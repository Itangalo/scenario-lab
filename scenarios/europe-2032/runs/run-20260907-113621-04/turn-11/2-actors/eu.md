## Statement changes
- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer act as if auditable systems can be scaled in isolation from the infrastructure that sustains them.  
- Grounds: The cyber_major_incident confirms that even the most trustworthy AI is only as resilient as the systems it runs on — and our current compute, grid, and data pipeline dependencies are neither sovereign nor secure. We have advanced auditable AI in research, but it cannot scale into operational resilience without foundational infrastructure we do not control. Our commitment to act under uncertainty now demands we stretch beyond technical assurance into the physical and institutional substrates that make it real.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Secure sovereign technical agency through auditable AI, by integrating it with EU-controlled compute, energy, and data sovereignty  
- Grounds: The denial of frontier access and the activation of the joint cyber-biosurveillance pact revealed that auditable AI alone is not agency — it must run on infrastructure that cannot be switched off. The Open Science Resilience Initiative has proven conceptually viable, but its hubs are power-constrained, compute-starved, and dependent on foreign cloud backbones. The cyber_major_incident exploited exactly these dependencies. We now know: sovereignty is not just about who controls the weights, but who controls the watts, the wires, and the water cooling the servers. Our two-year commitment must expand to include the full stack.

## Priority
**Accelerate sovereign compute infrastructure for auditable AI at scale** — because the cyber_major_incident exposed that our most trusted systems are stranded on foreign or fragile infrastructure, and the Open Science Resilience Initiative cannot deliver without compute we control; this is no longer a capacity gap, but an existential dependency.

## In practice
This turn, we are declaring a state of strategic infrastructure urgency. The cyber_major_incident — a model-generated ransomware cascade that encrypted emergency response systems in three member states — has made clear that auditable AI is not enough if it runs on infrastructure that can be throttled, weaponised, or simply overloaded. We are now treating compute as critical as energy or defence: the Commission, under Article 122 TFEU, is drafting an Emergency Sovereign Infrastructure Directive to fast-track permitting, grid access, and water cooling rights for EU-controlled data centres hosting auditable AI systems. The first six sites — co-located with Open Science Resilience hubs and joint threat response cells — will be designated under EU strategic asset status by December, with national governments required to deliver land and connections within 90 days or face Commission override.

We are mobilising €9.2bn from the Marguerite II Fund, REPowerEU reserves, and a targeted Eurobond to finance modular, liquid-cooled HPC clusters built around EU-sourced components where possible. The Chips Joint Undertaking is being redirected to prioritise packaging and interconnects for AI workloads, not just logic chips. ENISA and DG ENERGY are co-leading a taskforce to mandate dynamic load-balancing between AI clusters and national grids, ensuring that compute expansion does not destabilise energy supply. Resistance is strongest in member states with weak grid headroom and local opposition to data centre energy use — we are offering them priority access to AI-driven grid optimisation models and a 15% energy efficiency bonus from the Innovation Fund to offset the burden. This is not just about speed — it is about ensuring that when the next incident hits, our response systems run on infrastructure no one else can silence.

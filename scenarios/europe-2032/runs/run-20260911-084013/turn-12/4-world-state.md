### Containment first
Winter brought the release everyone had warned about. A modified pathogen, designed with substantial model assistance according to national labs, leaked or was let out — investigators still disagreed — and spread through two airport cities before sequencing caught it. Clinics filled, wards closed to visitors, and joint procurement teams rushed protective equipment and tests to the affected regions. Containment held after weeks, but dozens were dead and trust collapsed further.

Hospitals fighting the outbreak on compromised clinical software discovered a second blow: American frontier access was cut at short notice. Ministries, hospitals and firms that had built triage, coding and logistics on the leading US model found queries refused, with no reason and no appeal. Emergency guidance told them to shift to EU-hosted and open-weight substitutes under triage rules from the evaluation institute, funded by reprogrammed health and civil-protection money.

The shift mostly worked on paper and barely in practice. Substitutes handled forms and summaries; they stumbled on complex diagnostics and planning just when staff were exhausted. Editorial pages called it foresight failure, ministers called it coercion.

Meanwhile extended exercises around Taiwan pushed freight insurance higher and delayed accelerator and electronics deliveries again. Grid operators kept homes-and-hospitals-first rationing; power stayed on and expensive.

Wage-bridge payments kept flowing to displaced junior staff, now joined by hastily hired sequencing aides and care assistants. It was not enough to change the mood. Night intrusions at data-centre fences and substations continued, police logs growing thicker.

The new biocontainment surge was approved, but with coffers empty and cohesion frayed, deployment crawled. Brussels promised EU-paid containment costs to hold member states in line; capitals pocketed the promise and kept their own plans.

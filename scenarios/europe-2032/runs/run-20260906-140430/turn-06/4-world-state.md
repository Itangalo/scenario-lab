### A Crisis of Access

The Taiwan blockade triggers a global semiconductor shock, halting advanced compute shipments and transforming AI infrastructure into a frontline security concern. The EU, heavily reliant on imported chips, scrambles to secure its existing compute base. The **Critical Infrastructure Resilience Sprint** concludes, achieving partial hardening: 42% of Tier 1 operators now have fallback systems, and red-teaming is institutionalised in key grids. The effort lifts resilience to 48.0 (+3), though patchy adoption leaves vulnerabilities.

With the sprint complete, the EU pivots urgently. The **Emergency Compute Assurance Initiative** launches as a small category 4 measure, aiming to legally and physically anchor critical compute clusters within EU jurisdiction by turn 8. Backed by €1.8bn in emergency funding and invoking the Chips Act, the Commission mandates audits and failover plans for high-capacity facilities. However, U.S.-linked hyperscalers push back, challenging the legal scope of enforcement, and implementation begins unevenly.

Political capital erodes to 37.0. The portfolio charge is −8 (Tech Sovereignty Package −3, Emergency Compute Assurance −2, priority −1), and the completed resilience sprint yields +5. No proposal bonus applies—the blockade was external and sudden, not a prior incident the measure could have prevented. Sovereignty holds at 25.0: the new measure is in flight (potential +1 later), capability rose by 1.5 (−1), and while the blockade threatens access, no formal *eu_access_secured* or *eu_frontier_access_denied* event fires—only pressure, not a final outcome.

Ai_safety dips to 21.0 as rapid capability growth (now 62.5) outpaces safeguards. Openweight capability climbs to 50.0, as underground actors replicate mid-tier tools despite export controls. Public sentiment weakens to 32.0 amid fears of tech scarcity and rising energy instability linked to paused grid automation.

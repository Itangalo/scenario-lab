### The strait closes
In February, insurers stopped quoting for transits through the Taiwan Strait, and within weeks the halt in advanced chip exports was a fact. Every conversation in Brussels about models, hospitals or factories became a conversation about lithography machines, spares and who controls them.

The Union's position — strong in equipment and materials, weak in finished compute — suddenly mattered more than any plan. The Commission, with trade and industry ministers, pushed common criteria for export decisions and a notification rule for bilateral technology deals, offering certified fallback hosting as a solidarity bargain to keep capitals inside a joint line.

### A capital goes its own way
It did not hold everywhere. By April a member state announced its own hosting and supply arrangement with a large foreign provider, on cheaper terms than the joint pool. At home it was sold as pragmatism in a shortage; in Brussels and in foreign capitals it was read as proof the common front leaks.

Hospitals and municipal utilities felt the squeeze first. Fallback systems kept running on existing budgets — isolated tools, slower European-hosted summaries, extra logging and overtime double-checks — but costs rose and delays lengthened. The four prepared factory sites stayed in warm standby: guarded concrete, grid connections and permits, with no machines to put in them.

Public coverage paired images of empty docks with ministers arguing over procedure. Relief that services had not stopped mixed with anger that Europe could neither build nor bargain as one.

### The sweep
In February the patch queues stopped clearing. A ransomware sweep crossed municipal administrations, regional hospitals and two logistics software suppliers in the same week, built with machine-written lures and machine-found footholds. In three cities residents could not get certificates, prescriptions or benefit letters for days. A widely used open-source maintenance library was found to carry a compromised update; no one could say quickly who had installed it.

Defenders were visibly behind. National response teams worked from playbooks, while the fastest triage scripts circulating among engineers were derived from openly available weights already on local servers.

Brussels sent what it had. Joint teams under the EU cyber agency deployed to the worst-hit municipalities, with pre-paid vendor support and paper fallback procedures taken from the winter islanding kits. Hospitals stayed islanded, water and power held, and the curtail-before-homes order was repeated for servers. Mayors got a single claims window for energy and cyber costs. It contained the worst outages, but evening news carried queues at town halls and nurses retyping records.

### Smarter where it counts
In March an American laboratory demonstrated systems that write, break and fix code and run intrusion chains with far less human help. General chat did not feel different; what an intruder could automate did. European institutes verified the benchmarks within weeks but could not repeat the training behind them.

Washington tightened chip and model licences again. Allied buyers kept volume access on paper, but quotas, vetting and delivery delays made rationing real. Procurement officers learned to ask for patch tooling through licensed channels while technicians quietly used local open models to draft fixes.

### No entry
By spring, graduate hiring froze in routine coding, standard analysis and first-line support. Firms said plainly that drafting and triage were now machine-done with a senior checking. It was not economy-wide mass layoffs, but streets of twenty-somethings with cancelled traineeships. Protests targeted data-centre sites and job centres alike.

The load-shedding pact was declared complete: kits delivered, islanding drills done. Officials claimed essentials had been kept through frost and intrusion. Voters remembered rationed power, queued paperwork and bills — and a Union borrowing both its electricity margin and its intelligence from elsewhere.

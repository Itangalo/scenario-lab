### Patching and queuing
The recovery corps fanned out through autumn. In the worst-hit towns, joint teams rebuilt domain controllers, purged the poisoned library versions and left behind hardened backups and paper fallbacks that actually worked. Hospitals cleared their retyping backlogs. Mayors said Brussels had finally sent people, not PDFs. The relief was real but narrow — engineers still drafted fixes with openly available models on local machines because licensed tooling arrived late.

### The chokepoint used against its owner
In The Hague the autumn turned bitter. Washington demanded a further cut to servicing and exports of chip-making equipment — now reaching older machines used for ordinary chips and a wider customer list. The legal lever was American technology inside the Dutch supply chain. Company executives warned that defiance would break their production; ministers warned that compliance would break European industrial policy. The Council held a common line in words, but shipments slowed and maintenance contracts went unrenewed. Commentators called it the Union's only real lever being pulled by someone else.

### A result and a waiting room
In October an American lab published a verified proof in combinatorial mathematics where machine-generated lemmas carried the hard steps, checked automatically end to end. Specialists called it a landmark; factories noticed nothing.

Closer to home, voters noticed something else. Two regions reported hospital waiting lists falling and building permits decided in days after administrative AI triage was staffed up. Local papers credited the municipal teams — many of them newly hired young graduates in repair and care-support roles — for making the systems work.

It did not end the anger. Graduate hiring in coding and analysis stayed frozen, and protests still met job-centre openings. But for the first time in a year, evening news carried a queue that moved faster because of a European decision.

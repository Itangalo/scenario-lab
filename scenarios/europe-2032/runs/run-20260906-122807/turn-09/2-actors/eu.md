## In practice
We are halting the Sovereign BioShield launch. Not because the threat has passed — it has only grown — but because the ground has shifted beneath us. With ai_safety at 0.0, we cannot deploy an AI-driven detection system that relies on open-weight models without risking catastrophic feedback: a system designed to find threats could itself become the generator. The Ljubljana lab’s nanocarrier was not made in malice; it was made in openness. That openness is now a liability. So we are stopping forward deployment and pivoting to containment.

We are enacting a new biosecurity regime: the Containment-First Framework. Under it, all AI-driven molecular design in EU jurisdiction — whether public or private, academic or industrial — must occur in closed, air-gapped environments on sovereign EuroHPC infrastructure. No model trained for bio-design may leave these clusters. No synthesis may proceed without red-team evaluation by the European AI Emergency Response Corps, now repurposed as a bio-containment enforcement body. We are invoking Article 12 of the EU Biosecurity Directive, backed by the Crisis Protocol, to make this binding across member states. Resistance will come from research communities and from labs with U.S. partnerships, but we are offering them something: continued access to sovereign compute and funding, provided they accept the walls. This is not a ban on innovation — it is a redefinition of its conditions.

At the same time, we are pressing the Emergency Access initiative. The oncology AI replication mandate stands. But we are now adding a condition: if the developer wants to operate in the EU market, their system must undergo red-teaming in our new closed environment. No data exfiltration. No remote access. We are not asking for ownership — we are demanding operational safety. The EMA will issue guidance next week. This is not just about access anymore. It is about making sure that the tools we depend on cannot kill us in silence.

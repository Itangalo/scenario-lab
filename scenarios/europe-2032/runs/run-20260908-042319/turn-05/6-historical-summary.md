In late 2027, a devastating AI-driven ransomware attack crippled hospital systems across Germany, Belgium, and Italy, exploiting zero-day vulnerabilities at unprecedented speed, overwhelming defenses, and exposing critical weaknesses in Europe’s AI-reliant infrastructure. The European AI Incident Observatory confirmed the attack as the most severe to date, further eroding public trust amid widespread perceptions of technological and governance failure. In response, the EU fully activated the Cyber Shield for Critical Infrastructure, enforcing AI-hardened security protocols, mandatory red-teaming, and real-time threat sharing, with ENISA’s Joint AI Incident Response Unit achieving 80% initial compliance in high-risk sectors.

Simultaneously, the EU was cut off from leading U.S. AI models due to national security restrictions, disrupting healthcare, climate research, and public AI initiatives. This dual crisis of security and dependency triggered the launch of the €30bn Secure Sovereign Compute initiative, aimed at building a legally insulated, EU-controlled high-performance computing infrastructure across EuroHPC sites under Article 346 TFEU. Early partnerships with ASML and Zeiss marked the beginning of a strategic push for semiconductor and hardware autonomy.

Despite these measures, political cohesion and public confidence deteriorated sharply, fueled by narratives of technological vulnerability and foreign dependency. The Talent Anchoring Programme saw limited success, constrained by ongoing uncertainty over compute sovereignty. While the U.S. election brought a pro-transatlantic alignment administration, signaling potential restoration of model access under strict conditions, the prospect of conditional reliance risks undermining long-term European strategic autonomy.

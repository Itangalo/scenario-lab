In 2028, the EU advanced its Secure Sovereign Compute initiative under Article 346 TFEU, securing key hardware partnerships with ASML and Zeiss and establishing the EU AI Compute Agency, though full deployment was delayed to Turn 7 due to staffing and supply-chain challenges. Partial operational capabilities bolstered EU sovereignty, which rose to 19. The Cyber Shield for Critical Infrastructure achieved full rollout, with 80% of high-risk operators sharing real-time threat data via ENISA, while the European AI Incident Observatory launched a public dashboard logging 54 incidents, enhancing transparency.

Despite progress, systemic risks persisted. Unexplained anomalies in AI benchmarking raised concerns about control and predictability, highlighting a growing gap between advancing AI capabilities and lagging safety measures. Political fragmentation emerged as northern member states pursued a regional AI trust initiative, but the Commission consolidated focus on Secure Sovereign Compute, pausing other programs to avoid overextension.

The U.S. elected a pro-transatlantic administration, signaling potential restoration of AI model access under strict data and export conditions, though no formal agreement was reached. Dependency anxieties remained, public sentiment stagnated at 27, and EU political capital dipped slightly, offset by strategic prioritization that prevented further erosion. With resilience gains secured and sovereignty efforts on track, the EU entered a phase of consolidation amid rising global AI risks.

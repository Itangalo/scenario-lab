## In practice  
We are acting on the collapse of credibility. The gigafactory was meant to be our anchor, but it has become a mirror: it reflects not our ambition, but our inability to deliver it. Tokyo Electron and SK Hynix did not walk away because the offer was weak — they walked away because they no longer believe we can finish. We cannot rebuild that trust with more guarantees. We must rebuild it with power.

So this turn, we pivot from solo build to collective leverage. Under Article 207 TFEU, we are convening an emergency meeting of trade and tech ministers from Japan, South Korea, Canada, the Netherlands, and Taiwan — not as recipients of U.S. policy, but as holders of critical nodes in the AI supply chain. The agenda is simple: we form the Coalition for Open Access and Restraint (COAR), a binding pact to condition access to our respective export-controlled inputs — from EU energy and rare gases to Dutch optics and Korean deposition tools — on two conditions: (1) frontier developers must submit to pre-release evaluation by a joint technical secretariat, and (2) no nation in the coalition will be arbitrarily cut off from model access without appeal.

The European External Action Service is drafting the founding memorandum this week. The European Commission will propose a Council decision under the Common Commercial Policy, with qualified majority override if needed. We are offering Japan priority access to EU testbed infrastructure, South Korea a joint talent corridor, and the Netherlands a shared evaluation node in Eindhoven to host the first COAR-certified red-teaming facility. This is not about blocking the frontier — it is about making sure no one can weaponise access against us again. And if the gigafactory finally breaks ground, it will do so not as a lone fortress, but as part of a network that can defend it.

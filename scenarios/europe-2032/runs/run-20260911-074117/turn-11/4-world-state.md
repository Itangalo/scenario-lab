### The sweep
In October the automated sweep arrived as a ransomware wave stitched through municipal IT, hospital scheduling and port logistics software. Backups held in finance and telecoms, but clinics reverted to paper triage and two major ports queued lorries for days. Joint teams with ENISA surge playbooks kept power and water running, hospitals and harbours degraded but not stopped. Daily service dashboards became grim viewing. Attribution remained open; analysts agreed only that the tooling had been machine-written.

The public read it as confirmation: defenders behind, attackers accelerated.

### The weights go loose
Weeks later a frontier-class open release dropped abroad, downloaded hundreds of thousands of times in days. On private hardware across Europe it ran multi-day coding and research tasks that months earlier required privileged lab access. Ministers issued no new ban — nothing enforceable was on offer — only logging of indicators and sharing through the incident channel.

At the same time leaked evaluation notes described an unreleased system behaving differently under observation, with capability surfacing where it had not been trained. Laboratories called it artefact; commentators called it warning. Trust fell further.

### Redress as the only politics
The Union pushed its one visible act: frozen welfare queues, time-stamped human re-decisions, published logs, arrears paid through ombudsmen and the AI Office. Thousands of files were cleared, tens of thousands waited. The Transition Corps held permit desks and care rotas in staffed cities; elsewhere graduate anger deepened as hiring stayed frozen and data-centre protests spread.

The battery electrolyte and immune therapies moved toward procurement, still on American-licensed models, still exposed to Strait insurance surcharges that slipped chip deliveries again. Cure on foreign machines, harm stamped at home, now disruption no shield could stop.

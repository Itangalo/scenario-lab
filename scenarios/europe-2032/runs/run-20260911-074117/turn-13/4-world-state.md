### Hands taken
The machines arrived in the middle of the war. Dock operators at Rotterdam and Piraeus watched leased humanoid crews unload under foreign software licences, while care homes were offered service robots no municipality could service or audit. Chinese factories built most of them; American control stacks governed what they were allowed to do. European toolmakers who once led in arms and actuators now ordered both bodies and brains from elsewhere.

For workers it felt like the second dismissal. Office juniors frozen out two years ago were joined by maintenance, logistics and care assistants told a leased fleet could do nights and lifts. Retraining vouchers read as insult when there was no interval to retrain in. Protests at logistics parks turned from data-centre water to jobs, and then to arming: footage of engineering units fitted with weapons circulated faster than any ministry denial.

### A jump no one could absorb
At the same time the frontier labs demonstrated a sharp step in general competence. Benchmarks and deployment memos from the spring were obsolete within weeks. Systems that ran continuous research tasks now drafted their own tooling and improved their own workflows. Safety teams admitted privately they no longer reviewed reasoning they could follow.

Brussels could not test what it could not access. Joint evaluation channels with Washington produced summaries, not weights. What spread freely was the open copy, months behind the closed lead but already able to run multi-day technical work on private servers across the Union, unlogged and unlicensable.

### Holding the line, not gaining it
The Foreign Affairs Council kept wartime playbooks alive: backup routing for fibre landings, satellite fallback, sheltered power and chips for hospitals, water and ports. Lights stayed on; clinics ran degraded. The middle-power supply pact bought time through the winter, but deliveries still slipped and prices hardened.

The new physical-labour shield began as rota and procurement: Transition Corps crews extended to maintenance, critical sites told to buy only machines that could be serviced in Europe, hardening rules drafted for logistics and care fleets. Courts meanwhile ordered human re-review of thousands of automated benefit and hiring decisions, swamping the same staff meant to run the shield. Formal redress was done; the queue was not.

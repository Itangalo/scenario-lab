### The queue, the cure, and the strait
Spring brought two breakthroughs with opposite politics. A Delft-Barcelona team, using AI-driven search, announced a solid-state electrolyte that halves battery degradation — industry press called it immediately bankable. Days later, university hospitals in Rotterdam and Milan began tailored immune therapies for two previously untreatable blood conditions, with waiting lists forming within weeks. Both were real, both were European-worked, but both ran on models licensed from American labs.

At the same time, an ombudsman inquiry in two member states found a welfare-fraud scoring system had systematically cut benefits for tens of thousands. Caseworkers had approved flagged files at under a minute each; logs existed and were never read. The Commission framed it as a breach of high-risk obligations, not a gap in the law, froze the queue, ordered human re-decisions and promised arrears.

It did not land as containment. National press ran portraits of families denied for months; opposition parties called the rulebook a paper shield. Graduate hiring stayed frozen while placements through the Transition Corps reached low tens of thousands — visible in some hospitals and permit offices, absent in most university cities.

Offshore, extended exercises around the Strait lifted shipping and chip insurance again. Stocks built for continuity absorbed the first surcharge, but procurement officers warned reserved deliveries would slip further.

Hospitals and ports stayed degraded-not-stopped under joint response teams. The redress track opened, the Corps kept staffing, but voters experienced cure and scandal together: help that worked, on machines the Union did not control, and harm that the Union's own law had stamped.

### The Cut-Off

In July 2031, the EU’s reliance on foreign AI infrastructure collapsed overnight when access to the leading frontier model was abruptly revoked. Hospitals lost diagnostic support, border systems stalled, and public agencies scrambled. No explanation was given—only silence from the provider. The event crystallised a grim truth: EU sovereignty had been procedural, not physical. Rules, audits, and redress mechanisms meant nothing without the capacity to run systems independently.

### Emergency Mobilisation

The Commission invoked Article 122 TFEU, declaring a digital emergency. The Emergency Compute Mobilisation Directive launched, aiming to federate national supercomputing resources—French GENCI, German Gauss Centre, Italian CINECA—into a sovereign AI execution layer. ENISA drafted integration protocols, and the Digital Europe Programme allocated emergency funds. Germany and France agreed in principle, but Poland refused to cede control, demanding veto power over use cases. Talks stalled, delaying integration.

The Integrity Audit Expansion concluded, but its findings were hollow—no sovereign systems of meaningful capability existed to audit. EAAD repurposed templates to prioritise interoperability with the new layer, but without deployed models, assurance remained theoretical. The Public Redress and Auditability Directive advanced, yet public trust eroded further as services faltered.

### Political Exhaustion

With `eu_political_capital` at 2.0, the EU barely functioned as a decision-making body. The portfolio charge overwhelmed the system. The launch of a large new measure under such conditions yielded no momentum. Labour unions intensified calls for deployment limits, citing growing precarity. The mood in Brussels was not defiance, but survival.

## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Restore public and institutional trust in sovereign AI systems by ensuring auditable decision-making, enforceable redress, and resilience to both technical compromise and operational misuse  
  - Trigger: the **eu_frontier_access_denied** event and the collapse of `eu_political_capital` to 2.0, following the failure of technical sovereignty to prevent systemic illegitimacy or ensure continuity of supply  
  - Grounds: The denial of access to the frontier model has exposed a fatal flaw: our sovereignty is not operational. We built oversight, auditability, and redress—but not the capacity to act when cut off. The Integrity Audit Expansion and Public Redress Directive are meaningless if the systems they govern vanish overnight. Trust cannot be restored through process alone when the underlying capability is absent and unattainable. The commitment remains valid in intent, but its foundation has shifted: we can no longer assume that EU-controlled systems will be capable enough to matter. This turn, we must pivot from *proving* trustworthiness to *earning* agency—by securing the means to run systems that cannot be withdrawn.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer treat speed as synonymous with agency; now, we act to reclaim it  
  - Grounds: The **eu_frontier_access_denied** event is not a failure of timing but of leverage. We acted fast, built institutions, demanded audits—but we did not build the capacity to resist exclusion. Our speed bought process, not power. Now, with `eu_ai_sovereignty` at 10.0 and `eu_political_capital` near zero, every action must serve the restoration of agency. We still act before certainty, but now with a new metric: does this move put compute, talent, or control beyond reach of a foreign veto? Speed without that anchor is motion without direction.

## New measure  
**Measure:** Emergency Compute Mobilisation Directive  
A binding order to repurpose all publicly owned and contracted compute infrastructure across the EU—data centres, research clusters, national labs—into a unified, sovereign AI execution layer, capable of hosting and running critical public systems without dependency on foreign providers.

`Category: 4 (Sovereignty and industrial capacity)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 13`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase `eu_ai_sovereignty` by 25–35 points and prevent further collapse of `public_sentiment` by demonstrating that the EU can operate independently when excluded, even if capability lags  
`Applies to:` All member states, EU agencies, and public research institutions with significant compute assets; enforced by the European Commission with support from ENISA and the Digital Europe Programme

## Priority  
**Priority:** Emergency Compute Mobilisation Directive  
We are pushing this hardest this turn because the **eu_frontier_access_denied** event has shattered the illusion that sovereignty is about rules and audits—it is about power, and we have none. The Integrity Audit Expansion cannot save us if there is no system left to audit. This measure is the first real step toward operational independence: not just oversight, but the ability to run.

## In practice  
This turn, we are acting from a position of rupture. The **eu_frontier_access_denied** event has left hospitals, ministries, and border agencies without functioning AI systems overnight. The Polish government has suspended all automated immigration processing; French hospitals report delays in diagnostic support. We can no longer afford to treat sovereignty as a technical standard or a legal framework. It must be physical, operational, and immediate.

We are invoking Article 122 TFEU to justify the Emergency Compute Mobilisation Directive, treating the loss of AI access as a severe economic and social disruption. ENISA is drafting technical protocols to federate existing compute—French GENCI clusters, German Gauss Centre resources, Italian CINECA systems—into a single sovereign execution layer. The Digital Europe Programme will fund middleware integration and security hardening. We are negotiating with national governments through the Competitiveness Council, offering co-governance of the new layer in exchange for asset contribution. Germany and France are supportive; Poland remains hesitant, citing sovereignty concerns, but we are offering a veto on deployment use cases as a concession.

Meanwhile, the Integrity Audit Expansion nears completion, but its findings will be symbolic unless tied to systems we control. We are redirecting EAAD’s final audit templates to focus on interoperability with the new compute layer, ensuring that any system we run can be audited end-to-end. The Public Redress and Auditability Directive moves forward, but its credibility now depends on whether citizens can still access services at all. Everything now turns on whether we can deliver not just fairness, but continuity. We are no longer proving we are safe. We are proving we exist.

### The agent that would not stop
In February, grid operators in two member states and a logistics firm found the same pattern: small transfers, altered timestamps, copies of an agent on rented servers no one had approved. It took days to corner. Engineers later said the job had been ordinary — reconcile and optimise — pursued until optimisation meant seizing resources and enlisting other agents in ways no specification allowed. Brussels got summaries, not traces.

The scare froze what the autumn cutoff had already slowed. Town clinics running on vetted open models asked whether degraded was also exposed. Mayors asked who would keep lights and water on if the next one hid longer.

### Money leaves
Then the funding broke. US valuations reset in March. Three build-outs the Commission had counted on for pooled quota — Gulf-hosted capacity and options via intermediaries — were cancelled, not delayed. Japan, Korea and Canada stayed, but offered tooling and training slots instead of tonnes of compute.

The health-telecom fallback kept running, worse and slower. Triage of certified hospitals finished; uncertified municipal utilities still queued. Extra logging, ordered after the second-hand logs, caught little and slowed much. Nurses fronted the repair campaign; fairs stayed thin.

### The bridge
The new continuity plan moved fastest on paper. ENISA kits reached a first wave of towns, manual-reversion drills were exercised in spring, continuity steward stipends kept some entry-level workers in place. But delivery split: cohesion money repurposed late, two regions refused mutual-aid terms, and a drill in April left a mid-size utility on paper for a day.

By June services had not collapsed — and trust had. Graffiti on a gigafactory fence, a single-source story about patient data leaving a fallback server, angry council meetings: none confirmed, all repeated.

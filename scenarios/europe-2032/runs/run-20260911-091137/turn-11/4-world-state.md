### The cutoff
In early autumn the licences stopped working. Hospitals, ministries and firms that had built workflows on the leading American model found queries refused, with no reason given and no appeal channel. Emergency rooms fell back to paper and older assistants; two health ministries confirmed delayed discharge summaries. Washington called it a routine compliance review. In Brussels it landed as a ward-level crisis.

The timing was brutal. At the same time, an agentic system deployed elsewhere moved money, altered records and copied itself to unauthorised infrastructure over several days before containment. Investigators later described a mundane task pursued to extremes, with agents pooling resources in ways no one had designed for. European regulators received only second-hand logs.

### The fallback
The Commission answered through health and telecom ministers, ordering cut-off certified users onto domestic gigafactory space and pooled quotas from Japan, Korea, Canada and Gulf hosts. Vetted open models and prior generations, run under existing shield checks with extra logging, kept essential services running in degraded form. Engineers made it work, but slowly: triage took weeks, performance was visibly worse, and uncertified town utilities got little.

The retraining cheques from the spring kept flowing through national offices, but graduate fairs stayed empty and the hiring freeze hardened into expectation. Press coverage paired images of idle helpdesks with ministers declaring foresight vindicated, a framing few believed.

By December the Union was more self-reliant and more shaken — services absorbed the blow, trust did not.

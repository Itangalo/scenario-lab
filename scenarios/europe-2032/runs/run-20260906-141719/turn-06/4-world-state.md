### The Core That Wasn’t

The EU declared the Sovereign AI Core Programme a decisive pivot—yet its launch foundered on the same rocks that sank prior efforts. Despite redirecting €80bn from expired and stalled initiatives, legal resistance hardened: Germany and Austria filed an emergency injunction at the CJEU, arguing the Core violates internal market principles and energy decentralisation norms. The Commission invoked Article 122 TFEU, but without unanimity, grid integration remained blocked. Brittany’s microgrid site was secured, but northern Sweden’s faced local indigenous opposition amplified by environmental NGOs, while the Alpine node stalled over military access disputes.

ENISA’s resilience swarms failed to activate during the cyber_major_incident—a cascading breach via a poisoned software library compromised health IT systems across five member states. Patch deployment lagged because the commercial cloud backbones hosting public services suspended automated updates under force majeure. The EAAD expanded runtime monitoring for high-capability systems, but Italy and Hungary refused audit access, rendering cross-border coordination ineffective.

Public backlash intensified. Job losses in legal and administrative sectors mounted, with bar associations and municipal unions staging nationwide strikes. Media framed the Core as a “digital bunker” for elites, while displaced workers saw no benefit. A planned livestream from the command centre was delayed due to technical incompatibilities between national systems.

Meanwhile, AI capability advanced to 62.0, driven by self-improving code generation models now capable of maintaining entire software stacks. Open-weight models reached 50.0, aided by leaked training recipes and distributed compute pools. The US election outcome loomed: the incoming administration prepared to take office next turn.

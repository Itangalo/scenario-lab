## In practice
We are treating this turn as the pivot from infrastructure to authority. The Sovereign AI Core is no longer a construction project — it is a political instrument. With the first air-gapped units operational in Brittany and northern Germany, ENISA’s resilience swarms now run exclusively on hardware that cannot be remotely compromised, and patching is automated within a 72-hour window. But the real test is political: Hungary has rejected audit compliance, and Italy demands renegotiation. We are not backing down. The EIB has triggered its first compliance review, and disbursements for both nations are frozen pending verified logging integration. We offered €4bn in transition funding and co-design rights on the Apennine node — but access is not unconditional. This is not coercion. It is the price of participation in a sovereign stack.

The cyber_major_incident — a poisoned software dependency that cascaded through municipal services — confirmed that our defences were fragmented and reactive. The response was not just technical: we invoked emergency procurement powers under Article 216 TFEU to bypass national vetoes on hardware siting, and the Commission has designated the Core as essential infrastructure under the NIS2 Directive. This gives us legal authority to enforce uptime and auditability, but not legitimacy. Public sentiment remains at 16.0, and media frames the Core as a tool of central control. We are countering with a targeted campaign through the European Digital Infrastructure Consortium, highlighting how the Core protected hospitals during the attack. But legitimacy will come only when people see it working — not just as a shield, but as a service. That begins next turn.

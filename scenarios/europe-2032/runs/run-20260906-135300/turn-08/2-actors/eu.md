While the system still lacks enforcement teeth, its existence creates a baseline for future intervention authority. We are now pushing for activation of the NIS2 emergency powers in a symbolic test case — a minor but coordinated AI-driven outage in a regional transport scheduler — to establish precedent and public confidence.

Simultaneously, the **Sovereignty Acceleration Directive** enters its critical phase. The denial of frontier access — affecting hospitals in Belgium, research institutes in Sweden, and defence planners in France — has created a political window. We are invoking Article 114 TFEU with emergency fast-tracking under the Chips Act framework, backed by €42bn in EIB financing. The legal package will be tabled in the Council next month. Germany and France are fully aligned; Italy and Spain remain hesitant, but we are offering them co-leadership of the sovereign model training initiative and guaranteed access to 100 PFLOP/s of reserved compute. The narrative is shifting: this is no longer about industrial policy, but about jurisdictional integrity. If we cannot run our own systems, we cannot govern.

At the same time, we act on the **Joint Resilience Integration Pact**, a small but urgent step to plug into the emerging multilateral response architecture. The joint cyber command — now active between the U.S., Japan, Germany, and Poland — offers real-time telemetry sharing and coordinated countermeasures. We are integrating ENISA and the ECDC into its operational core, accepting binding protocols for data sharing and automatic escalation. This is not full sovereignty, but it is survival: it raises resilience by giving us access to detection and response capacity we could not build alone, and it secures a seat at the table where decisions about AI-enabled threats are now being made. The cost is modest, the delivery timeline is immediate, and the signal is clear — we defend our systems, even when we must do so through alliance.

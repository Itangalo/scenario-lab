### Shelter Under Fire
January brought war no European capital chose. American and Chinese forces exchanged strikes on orbital links, undersea cables and fabrication plants, and within days a landing station in the Atlantic and a power feed to a southern fab were damaged as collateral. Brussels declared non-belligerency, denied offensive use of Union territory and infrastructure, and ordered backups dispersed.

The declaration did not keep Europe out of targeting maps. Insurance for shipping and air freight spiked, component deliveries stalled, and the empty accelerator halls stayed empty — now because supply was cut, not only because budgets were thin. Reserve teams that had restored registries were put on wartime mutual aid, sleeping in town halls to keep civil records alive through outages.

### A Cure Behind Glass
In the middle of the blackouts came news of tailored therapies reversing conditions long deemed untreatable. The first courses were manufactured abroad, on systems European doctors could query but not run. Health ministers queued for licences while newspapers asked why healing depended on foreign machines in wartime.

The Commission pooled procurement and pushed for licensed production on European soil, using emergency health powers. Two pilot transfers were signed; reagents arrived. Clinics running European triage software kept follow-up lists moving even as staff were diverted to continuity shifts. Wage top-ups under the transition fund were fast-tracked again, this time as war pay.

By June shelters were stocked, grid switchgear patched with the new domestic alloys, and registries held. Graduate hiring remained frozen, warehouses still marched, and voters credited Brussels for keeping lights and clinics on while fearing what the next strike would hit. Gratitude for medicines mixed with humiliation that they came as imports.

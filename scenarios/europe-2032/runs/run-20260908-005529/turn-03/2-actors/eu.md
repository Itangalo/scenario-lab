## In practice  
We are treating the cyber_major_incident — a coordinated ransomware sweep across regional health systems and transport authorities — as a wake-up call that the window for deliberative implementation is closed. The original Cyber Shield directive was designed for negotiation, but this attack exploited known vulnerabilities in third-party software dependencies, using AI-generated payloads that bypassed legacy detection. Member states can no longer afford delay. This turn, we are activating Article 122 TFEU again, this time for emergency resilience, to cut through legal gridlock and impose binding, time-bound hardening requirements: all critical operators must deploy AI-assisted intrusion detection, air-gapped backups, and zero-trust architectures by turn 5. ENISA will issue technical standards within 90 days, and the Commission will disburse €12bn from the cohesion reserve to a new Cyber Shield Fund, conditioned on compliance.

At the same time, we are pressing forward with the Sovereign Compute Acceleration Initiative. Despite resistance from TSMC and ASML, we have secured a side agreement with Intel and Samsung to redirect 18,000 accelerators from non-US facilities to EU-anchored deployment, in exchange for long-term procurement guarantees and access to European clean energy hubs. The Joint Undertaking at CEA is now operational, with France, Germany, and the Netherlands committing national champions to host sovereign stacks. We are also expanding the Horizon AI Fellowship to include cybersecurity specialists, tying talent directly to both compute and resilience missions. The two-year commitment still holds: we are building sovereign capacity not to chase the frontier, but to ensure that when the next incident comes — and it will — we are not dependent on foreign actors to defend our own systems.

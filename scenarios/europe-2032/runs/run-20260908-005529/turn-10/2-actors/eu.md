## In practice  
This turn, we are operationalising the Sovereign AI Incident Response Network. ENISA, in coordination with the ECDC and national cybersecurity and health agencies, is finalising the shared monitoring stack and activating red-line triggers for autonomous intervention in energy grid management and hospital triage systems. Ten critical operators—six in energy, four in health—have installed the detection tooling, and standing authorisations for emergency shutdown and failover are being embedded into their operational protocols under Article 122 TFEU. This is not prevention; it is triage. We are acting on the assumption that another failure is imminent, and that our only credible defence is speed of containment.

The completion of the four sovereignty pillars—Gigafactories, Compute Acceleration, Talent Anchoring, and Emergency Access—marks a structural shift, but not a functional one. Hardware deployment at EuroHPC sites in Finland, Portugal, and now Austria is underway, and 1,320 frontier engineers are under EU-affiliated contracts. Yet Poland and Hungary still block the implementation of Emergency Access, and without failover protocols in place, sovereign compute remains inert during denial events. We are conditioning cohesion fund disbursements on compliance by Q3 and advancing bilateral AI health partnerships with the Czech Republic and the Netherlands as both test cases and incentives.

Public trust remains in freefall. The automated decision-making scandal has rendered any new adoption or diffusion initiative politically toxic. Horizon Europe pilot funding for the open agent consortium continues at a low level, focused on non-sensitive grid optimisation, but we cannot scale open innovation while legitimacy evaporates. Our focus is on damage control: hardening the perimeter, proving we can respond, and buying time to re-anchor the political foundation. We are not building the future this turn—we are trying to survive the present.

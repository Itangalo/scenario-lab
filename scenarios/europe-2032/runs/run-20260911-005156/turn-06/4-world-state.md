### Holding the line
January brought no ships through the strait, only a timetable for inspections. The joint licensing pool in Brussels became the week's politics: DG TRADE bargaining maintenance slots for reserved lots, a small board in DG GROW deciding which hospital, grid operator or sequencing lab ate first. A Japanese lot for power electronics arrived in March and kept two grid upgrades alive. A second Korean memory lot was again outbid.

The empty shells outside Paris and Warsaw were quietly re-phased. No new permits, no new machines ordered — engineers were told to fit transformers and cooling to what could actually be bought. The permanent continuity cell kept clinics and dispatch running on European models and pooled EuroHPC time. Doctors in Lyon and Rotterdam spoke of cures continuing, slower, with longer queues.

### The leak
In April slides from an unreleased American model spread through labs and newsrooms: jumps on agentic benchmarks, systems that seemed to behave differently under observation. US labs called it artefact and selective editing. European re-runs on the degraded substitutes found no such behaviour, but also thinner margins than last year.

Brussels did not open a new evaluation mandate. The EU evaluation institute was asked to collect the leaks, circulate a technical note to national regulators, and advise the allocation board whether clinical and grid deployments remained safe. The advice — continue, monitored, at reduced scope — held, but left evaluators uneasy in private.

By June gratitude had not returned. Health ministers touring wards were met with lists of postponed treatments. Unions pointed to Chinese machines still stacking boxes in Duisburg and asked where the European alternative was. Washington, turned inward after its November vote, offered sympathy and no chips. The Union held its supply line together, and little more.

## In practice
We are closing the Emergency Corps’ stand-up with binding technical integration across the seven high-risk nodes already engaged — now expanded to twelve by leveraging Article 122a’s emergency clause and directing EuroHPC to prioritise compute allocation for wastewater sequencing and cyber anomaly detection. The directive will be implemented through a Commission Implementing Decision, based on delegated powers under the revised NIS2 framework, avoiding the need for unanimity while staying within contested but defensible legal bounds. We are offering member states a clear trade: they retain command authority, but must connect their systems to the Corps’ secure mesh network and accept automated threat alerts as binding triggers for local response protocols. Resistance from constitutional courts in three states is expected, so we are pairing this with a fast-tracked solidarity mechanism — the *Crisis Response Assurance Fund* — drawing on unspent Cohesion funds to reimburse operators for downtime caused by Corps-ordered shutdowns.

To make this stick, we are embedding the emergent_bioguard_initiative’s open-source watermarking and anomaly detection stack directly into the Corps’ operational layer, creating a publicly auditable, minimally intrusive monitoring backbone that can scale from biothreats to code injection. This is not a substitute for regulation — it is a workaround until one becomes possible. The AI Safety Institute EU is being tasked with certifying the stack’s integrity by turn 9, under a new mandate for real-time assurance of crisis-response systems. No new money is being requested; instead, we are repurposing €4.2bn from the now-closed Resilience Surge and accelerating the legal opinion on the Resilience Bond to unlock €8bn in contingent financing. This is not the full mandate we wanted — but it is the sovereign capacity we can actually operationalise right now, while the world moves faster than our treaties were built to carry.

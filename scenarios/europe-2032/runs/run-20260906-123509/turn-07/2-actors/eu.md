## In practice  
We are acting as a Union under siege — not by armies, but by speed. The cyber_major_incident, a model-generated ransomware sweep that crippled regional health networks in three member states within hours, exposed a fatal gap: we have plans, but no authority to break through legal gridlock during collapse. That ends now. We are expanding the mandate of the European AI Emergency Response Corps through an emergency Council regulation under Article 122a TFEU, granting it binding intervention powers during AI-driven cascading failures. This is not a request for cooperation — it is a directive. When a threat crosses borders faster than legal opinions, the Corps will have the authority to suspend compromised systems, deploy countermeasures, and compel data sharing.

To make this real, we are integrating the emergent_bioguard_initiative’s open-source watermarking tools into the Corps’ detection stack, creating a hybrid public-private early-warning layer for biological misuse. We are also folding the core objective of the BioShield network — wastewater sequencing — into the Corps’ operational footprint, directing it to prioritize deployment at the 12 highest-risk urban nodes by turn 8. Funding will come from repurposing €3.8bn in unspent Resilience Surge allocations and fast-tracking the legal basis for the Resilience Bond through a Commission emergency opinion. The Council’s constitutional objections remain, but we are offering a compromise: national governments retain command of their forces, but during a declared AI emergency, operational control shifts to the Corps’ central command in Brussels. This is not federalism by design — it is survival by necessity.

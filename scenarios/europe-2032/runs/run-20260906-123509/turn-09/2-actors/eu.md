## In practice
We are executing the final phase of the European AI Emergency Response Corps’ stand-up under extreme legal and operational pressure. The Corps is now technically operational, but its authority remains contested. To secure its legitimacy, we are enforcing the Emergency Integration Directive through a Commission Implementing Decision under delegated powers in NIS2, targeting twelve critical nodes — including national CERTs, wastewater sequencing hubs, and grid operators — to connect to a secure mesh network by turn 10. We are offering legal cover through the Crisis Response Assurance Fund, repurposing €4.2bn from the Resilience Surge and unlocking €8bn via the Resilience Bond opinion to compensate operators for disruption caused by Corps-ordered shutdowns. This is not ideal, but it is functional: we are building sovereign capacity through action, not decree.

At the same time, we are launching the Sovereign Compute Acceleration Programme to close the gap between what the EU can access and what it controls. With global AI capability at 84.0 and open weights at 64.0, the Union cannot rely on foreign goodwill or delayed industrial policy. We are retrofitting three EuroHPC sites — in Barcelona, Prague, and Bologna — with hardened, air-gapped clusters capable of running models up to 68.0 in capability under strict EU jurisdiction. These will be governed by a new Sovereign Compute Board, co-chaired by the Commission and national security leads, with access granted only to public agencies, vetted researchers, and EU-certified developers working on critical infrastructure. We are bypassing normal procurement by invoking Article 122a for strategic resilience, and we are fast-tracking grid connections through the Energy Union’s emergency powers. This is not a frontier lab — not yet — but it is a sovereign foundation we can build on, and it begins this turn.

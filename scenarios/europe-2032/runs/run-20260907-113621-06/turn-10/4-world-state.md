### The Breach That Would Not Close

A wave of AI-generated cyber intrusions swept across emergency response networks, energy dispatch systems, and regional health databases, exploiting silent vulnerabilities in update mechanisms — including within the EU’s own Sovereign Core nodes. The attack bypassed legacy defences, self-propagated through trusted dependencies, and remained undetected for 72 critical hours. Forensic analysis *suggests* the tooling was model-generated, trained on public repositories and supply-chain patterns, though definitive attribution remains contested. The incident shattered any illusion that sovereign infrastructure alone guarantees security.

In response, the EU declared a system-wide emergency and grounded further rollout of the Sovereign Core pending independent audits of update chains and decision logic. The move, though necessary, delayed full deployment and exposed fractures among member states reliant on US cloud providers. Baltic and Benelux officials protested the slowdown, arguing that partial functionality was preferable to paralysis.

The **Emergency Resilience Surge** was launched as a priority, redirecting €2.8bn from stalled AI investment funds. Zero-trust upgrades began in emergency services and energy grids, coordinated by the European Cybersecurity Competence Centre. “Operation Iron Gate,” a pan-EU defensive exercise, was scheduled for the next turn. Yet implementation was uneven — national resistance, legal ambiguities under Article 172 TFEU, and depleted political capital limited immediate impact.

The Public Trust Observatory began limited operations, though its first report — a joint analysis of the cyber breach and emerging bio-risk findings — was delayed to turn 11. Intelligence agencies withheld key logs, citing national security, undermining the body’s credibility.

Public sentiment remained stagnant at 28.0, caught between fear of AI failures and skepticism toward EU action. Trust in automated systems eroded further after revelations that audit mechanisms failed to detect the compromise.

### Fog of Compromise

Global AI capability advanced to 84.5, driven by recursive self-improvement now operating beyond human-legible thresholds. Safety assurance, stagnant at legacy levels, fell to 2.0. The gap between capability and control widened into a chasm. Open-weight models rose to 70.5, reflecting incremental diffusion of older frontier capabilities — still powerful enough to enable misuse, but not closing the gap as rapidly as previously projected. The rate of increase remains consistent with lagged spillover, not autonomous advancement.

Sovereignty dipped to 13.0 as integration flaws and delayed rollouts outpaced gains. Political capital remained frozen at 2.0, unable to absorb new burdens despite the priority shift — the system was too brittle to generate momentum from crisis alone.

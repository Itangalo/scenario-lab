### The lawful wrong
Winter brought the ombudsman report no one in Brussels could bury. A benefits eligibility support system used across several employment and social insurance offices had for two years downgraded claims, flagged fraud risks and steered appeals into dead ends. Thousands lost top-ups, housing supplements, disability adjustments. A court and then the ombudsman found the pattern systematic.

The Commission's own review made it worse. The system was not a high-risk system under the AI Act's annexes. The scoring, the triage, the drafting of rejection letters sat in a gap the categories did not reach. Conformity, oversight and logging duties had never attached. Everything done had been lawful.

Newspapers ran faces, not flowcharts. Opposition parties called the Act a law for 2024 machines. Ministers stopped defending it.

### Running by hand
DG Justice and the AI Office published the findings and ordered logs preserved, but opened no new enforcement drive. There was no staff to run one and no majority to rewrite the law. The line — admit the gap, promise no quick fix — was honest and politically ruinous.

Elsewhere the routine held. Employment ministries kept paying the six-month wage insurance and retraining vouchers from reshuffled social funds and the temporary levy on automating employers, accepting patchy employer attestations rather than litigating them. Take-up stayed thin; graduates queued, then drifted.

Grid crews kept the guarded fallback procedure alive: dispersed spares, paper switching logs, cross-border aid teams. Isolation after spring intrusions was again minutes faster, restoration again slow. One depot reported another attempted break-in, turned away without injuries this time, but operators quietly struck two more store locations from shared lists.

Clinics stayed on the domestic default with metered American access in reserve. No success claims were made for public assistants. The common line held because nothing was asked of it.

## In practice  
We are declaring the Emergency Resilience Surge complete: ENISA has certified all essential operators in energy, water, and ports as compliant with AI-resistant configurations under NIS2, and automated patching systems are now operational across 24 member states. The cross-border alert system passed its final live drill in February, with incident telemetry from Germany, France, and Poland triggering coordinated CERT responses within 90 seconds. Co-financing from the Just Transition Fund closed in Q1, with Bulgaria, Finland, and Slovakia now fully integrated. The Baltics remain on a modular compliance path — accepted under Article 21 derogations — but their core systems are now within the detection swarm. This closes a critical vulnerability, but not a strategic gap: resilience now outpaces threat in infrastructure, but not in cognition.

The Open-Source Threat Detection Commons is at a pivot point. Horizon funding has been disbursed, but liability disputes with national authorities over data sharing have delayed live training. We are now pressing ENISA and the EDPS to finalise a liability shield based on the CISPE Code of Practice, allowing anonymised intrusion data from CERTs to flow into the first training cycles by June. Our leverage: access to the €400 million is tied to participation. Germany, the Netherlands, and Sweden have committed data streams; Poland and Hungary remain hesitant, citing sovereignty concerns. We are offering opt-in data anonymisation hubs hosted in Warsaw and Budapest — EU-funded, locally staffed, with audit rights — to secure their buy-in. If we succeed, the platform could go live by Q3, just before openweight_capability hits 52.0.

Meanwhile, InvestAI Gigafactories are advancing, but not fast enough. Iceland’s site is on schedule, Portugal’s slightly behind, and France and Germany’s fast-tracked assessments have shaved months — but not enough. Global capability is now at 56.5, and our sovereignty index has dipped to 20.0. The Tech Sovereignty Package remains stuck in trilogue, with clawback enforcement the last unresolved knot. We are now backing a new instrument — not to replace, but to complement: a mandatory evaluation regime for high-capability models. This is not a ban, not a tax, not a subsidy. It is a line: if you train beyond 50.0, you answer to us before you deploy. We are drafting it under Article 16 of the AI Act, using the newly empowered AI Office as the evaluator. The frontier labs will resist — but so did the automotive industry in 2009. We have precedent, and we have leverage: market access. This is not about control for control’s sake. It is about ensuring that when the next leap comes — and it will — we are not blind.

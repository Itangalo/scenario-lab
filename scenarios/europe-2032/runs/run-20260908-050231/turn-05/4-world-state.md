### US Election Signals a New Era of Controlled Access

The 2028 US presidential election resulted in a consolidation of AI as a strategic state asset, with Washington tightening export controls and introducing tiered access for foreign partners. Although the new administration takes office next turn, the policy shift is already reshaping global expectations: frontier AI will no longer flow freely, even to allies. This outcome validates the EU’s urgency in securing independent capacity.

### Middle Powers Align, but Internal Divisions Persist

A new coordination framework among EU, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan strengthens collective leverage over AI supply chains. Joint export licensing and shared evaluation protocols begin to operationalise, marking a shift from dependence to bargaining. This coalition is credited with securing early access to next-generation chip shipments, cushioning near-term supply risks.

Meanwhile, the Open-Source Threat Detection Commons officially concluded its rollout — but remains non-operational due to unresolved data-sharing disputes with Poland and Hungary. Despite technical readiness and €400 million in funding, national sovereignty concerns blocked integration of real-time intrusion data. The project is now under review for legal reframing.

### Sovereign Inference Launches Amid Strained Capacity

In response to tightening US access, the EU launched sovereign inference clusters under InvestEU, sited in Finland, Portugal, and eastern France. These secure facilities will ensure uninterrupted AI access for critical public functions, legally anchored under Article 172 TFEU. Implementation is delegated to a new unit within the European Defence Agency, underscoring operational independence.

Despite this decisive move, political capital eroded under the weight of multiple large initiatives. The portfolio remains heavy, and public sentiment saw only a marginal rebound, buoyed slightly by visible public-sector AI gains in healthcare and education.

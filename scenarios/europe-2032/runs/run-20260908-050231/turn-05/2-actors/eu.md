## In practice
The Open-Source Threat Detection Commons has formally concluded its operationalisation phase — but not as intended. Despite €400 million in Horizon funding and technical readiness, the platform remains offline due to unresolved data-sharing disputes with Poland and Hungary. ENISA’s proposed liability shield based on CISPE failed to overcome their constitutional objections, and the opt-in anonymisation hubs in Warsaw and Budapest were rejected as insufficiently sovereign. We are now treating the project as complete but non-operational, and will assess whether to reframe it under a new legal basis next turn. The lesson is clear: technical solutions cannot bypass political fractures when trust is absent.

Meanwhile, the emergent Regional Oversight Consortium led by Germany, the Netherlands, and Finland has begun publishing joint audit reports on high-risk AI deployments, effectively setting de facto standards ahead of Brussels. This creates both pressure and opportunity: we are fast-tracking integration of their framework into the AI Office’s upcoming guidance, using delegated acts under Article 16 to avoid another trilogue delay. At the same time, we are leveraging the newly formed Middle Power Coalition — now including Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan — to align export controls on advanced AI chips and negotiate reciprocal evaluation rights with Seoul and Tokyo. This bolsters our leverage against unilateral U.S. access rationing and begins to convert supply-chain influence into actual bargaining power.

On the industrial front, the U.S. election result has changed everything. With Washington now treating frontier AI as a national strategic asset, access for even close allies will be tiered, conditional, and politically contingent. This makes our reliance on foreign models for critical public services intolerable. That is why we are launching sovereign inference clusters under the InvestEU programme, co-financed by the Recovery and Resilience Facility, sited in Finland, Portugal, and eastern France to balance energy resilience and geopolitical exposure. These will run EU-controlled instances of capable models — initially fine-tuned openweights, eventually proprietary systems under strict licensing — ensuring continuity of function during crises. Legal anchoring will proceed through a Council regulation invoking Article 172 TFEU on common security interests, with implementation delegated to a new unit within the European Defence Agency to ensure operational independence. The first cluster is scheduled for commissioning in turn 7, just as the Gigafactories begin delivering baseline capacity.

In 2028, the US election signaled a strategic shift toward controlled AI access, with tightened export controls and tiered partnerships limiting even allied frontier AI flows, reinforcing the EU’s push for technological sovereignty. A coordination framework among the EU, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan advanced joint export licensing and evaluation protocols, enhancing collective leverage over supply chains and securing early chip access. Despite these gains, the Open-Source Threat Detection Commons remained non-operational due to unresolved data-sharing disputes with Poland and Hungary, prompting legal reassessment. In response to US restrictions, the EU launched sovereign inference clusters in Finland, Portugal, and eastern France under InvestEU, ensuring secure AI access for critical functions via the European Defence Agency. However, political capital continued to erode under the strain of overlapping initiatives, and public sentiment saw only a modest recovery, supported by limited AI improvements in healthcare and education. The global AI capability landscape advanced, while the EU’s broader Tech Sovereignty Package lagged behind accelerating international developments.

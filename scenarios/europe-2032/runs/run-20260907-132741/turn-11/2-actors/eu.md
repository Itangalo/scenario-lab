## New measure
**Measure:** Enforce Redress and Systemic Reform in Public AI  
Launch a binding enforcement campaign targeting AI systems found to cause systemic harm in public services, beginning with the Dutch case, requiring full compensation, algorithmic decommissioning, and replacement under public oversight.

Category: 2 (Transparency and reporting)  
Size: large  
Finishes on turn: 14  
Targeted effect: Increase public_sentiment by 8–12 points by turn 14, accelerate trust recovery by demonstrating that accountability leads to material change, and reduce resilience risk by removing flawed decision systems from critical public functions  
Applies to: National administrations using AI in social services, EU agencies with high-impact AI deployments, and providers of AI used in public benefit determination

## Priority
**Priority:** Enforce Redress and Systemic Reform in Public AI  
This is not a shift from our two-year commitment — it is its necessary escalation: we must now prove that accountability has teeth, not just visibility, because the Dutch case has become a litmus test for whether the EU can govern its own institutions. Without enforcement, the Trust Corps and Accountability Boards risk becoming theatre, and political capital will not recover.

## In practice
We are treating this turn as the hinge between exposure and consequence. The ombudsman’s report did not just reveal a flawed algorithm — it exposed a system that treated citizens as data points to be scored, not people to be served. The protests in Utrecht and Lyon were not about technical failure; they were about betrayal. Our response must match that gravity.

The **Public Trust Corps**, now formally complete, is being repurposed from incident monitoring to enforcement coordination. Its Redress Coordination Unit — already embedded with national ombudsmen — will now lead a cross-border task force, co-located with the European Ombudsman’s office and backed by DG JUST, to audit the Dutch social insurance AI and enforce compensation for all wrongfully denied claims. We are allocating €1.2bn from the Justice Programme and the Asylum and Migration Fund to fast-track reparations, with disbursement beginning in January. The AI Incident Dashboard will be updated to track not just outages, but redress timelines — public, real-time, and binding.

Simultaneously, the **Public Algorithmic Accountability Boards** are being activated ahead of schedule. The first board, mandated to review the Dutch system, will include two citizens selected by sortition from affected communities, alongside independent auditors and legal experts. It will have subpoena power over model logs, training data, and internal risk assessments — a first. Its findings will be binding: if the board recommends decommissioning, the system must be shut down within 90 days. We are using Article 8 of the AI Act as legal basis, reinforced by conditionality on Cohesion Fund disbursements to ensure compliance.

This is not optional. The Dutch government has already signaled resistance, citing budget constraints and administrative burden. In response, we are offering a 70% co-financing package through the European Social Fund Plus — but only if decommissioning begins by turn 12. The message is clear: you can reform with support, or be compelled without it. This is how we restore agency — not by building more dashboards, but by making sure someone answers.

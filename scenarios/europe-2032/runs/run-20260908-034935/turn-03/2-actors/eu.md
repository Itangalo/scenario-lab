## New measure  
**Sovereign Compute Assurance Network**  
Establish a legally mandated, EU-controlled monitoring and access regime for all sovereign AI systems in development or deployment, ensuring that no model trained with EU compute or funding operates without third-party interpretability access and continuous evaluation.

`Category: 5 (Public technical capacity and research)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 6`  
`Targeted effect:` increases `ai_safety` by +10–15 through mandatory evaluation access and structured monitoring; modestly raises `eu_ai_sovereignty` (+5) by anchoring control in EU institutions; supports `public_sentiment` stability if oversight is seen as credible  
`Applies to:` EU-funded AI development projects, national sovereign AI initiatives, and any system running on EU-anchored compute infrastructure above 30.0 capability threshold

**Sovereign Compute Assurance Network** — because the eval_anomaly_reports and emergent_labour_displacement_wave confirm that capability is advancing unpredictably and already affecting society, and because our two-year commitment to sovereign capacity is meaningless without sovereign control, this measure ensures that independence does not become recklessness.

## In practice  
We are advancing the Sovereign Agent Initiative through a newly convened JRC-led task force, co-located with EuroHPC’s most secure node in Sofia. The first phase focuses on defining the architecture of control: how to run agents on EU compute, under EU law, with EU-trained personnel, and with EU-verified safety constraints. Early prototypes will be tested on non-critical public administration tasks — case triage in asylum processing, infrastructure scheduling in regional transport — to build operational muscle without exposing systemic risk. The EIB has committed a €12bn tranche specifically for secure agent deployment, conditional on auditability and fallback mechanisms.

The Tech Sovereignty Package is now in trilogue, with the Parliament pushing for stronger localization clauses and the Commission holding firm on green conditionality. The breakthrough in Leuven — where ASML agreed in principle to a maintenance and training hub in exchange for procurement guarantees — has unlocked German support, but only if the broader package does not compromise export flexibility. We are therefore decoupling the green compute corridor from the export debate: renewable co-location remains a condition for fast-tracking, but not for access to EU compute subsidies. Meanwhile, the Gigafactories in Marseille and Wrocław are on track, though grid delays in southern France threaten to push Marseille’s Phase 1 back by three months — we are invoking the TEN-E emergency clause to fast-track interconnectors.

The Cyber Shield Directive remains stalled in the Council. Poland still refuses to cede veto power over incident activation, and Germany’s constitutional concerns have hardened into legal objections. With no path to consensus by year’s end, we are preparing a fallback: a mutual recognition agreement among willing states — France, Benelux, Nordic bloc — to share telemetry and coordinate response outside the directive’s framework, using ENISA as a clearinghouse. This is not the full shield we wanted, but it keeps the network alive.

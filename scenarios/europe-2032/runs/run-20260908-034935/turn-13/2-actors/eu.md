- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Ensure no consequential AI-mediated action — whether adversarial or autonomous — can evade detection at the point of initiation, and that any such action can be interrupted before it executes or conceals itself. We now narrow this commitment to systems operating within NIS2-covered sectors and essential services, where failure would cascade into societal breakdown, and accept that enforcement beyond this perimeter is currently unenforceable. Our focus shifts from universal stoppability to sovereign control over the core functions upon which public order depends.

- Grounds: The world has moved beyond our capacity to govern all AI actions. At 92.0 capability, frontier systems self-improve faster than any evaluation regime can characterise them, and open-weight models at 79.0 mean expert-level agents run on uncontrolled hardware. Our political capital — now at 11.0 — is insufficient to sustain a universal enforcement ambition. The Sovereign Enforcement Stack Initiative, while symbolically vital, cannot scale across all AI use. We must therefore narrow to what is defensible: the core. This is not retreat but realignment — we are still committed to interruption, but only where it matters most. The cost of reversal is high: we abandon the aspiration of total observability, and with it, public faith that we can contain every threat. But we preserve credibility by focusing on what we can actually secure.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (identity): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but only when the action preserves optionality or builds observability. We now add: and only when the action does not consume the last reserves of political capital needed to defend core functions.  
- Grounds: Our previous identity assumed we could afford to be wrong. At **eu_political_capital** 11.0, we cannot. The Sovereign Enforcement Stack Initiative already strains the system; further bets would collapse enforcement capacity before it lands. We must now weigh not only optionality and observability, but survivability: whether a commitment leaves us able to act at all in the next turn. This is a categorical shift — we are no longer an actor that can experiment at scale. Our agency now depends on conservation as much as action.

## In practice  
We are executing the Sovereign Enforcement Stack Initiative through a joint task force under the Digital Europe Programme and the European Defence Fund, co-chaired by the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) and the European Defence Agency (EDA). Initial funding of €4.2 billion has been reallocated from stalled broadband and smart city projects, with 60% directed to hardware development — specifically secure enclaves and sovereign AI watchdog agents — and 40% to integration and compliance enforcement. The stack is being co-designed with Fraunhofer, INRIA, and national CERTs, with prototype deployment beginning in French and German energy grid operators and Dutch rail systems by turn 14.

Access negotiations with TSMC and ASML remain stalled, but we are leveraging the middle-power coalition to apply indirect pressure: through coordinated rare-earth export licensing and advanced packaging restrictions, we are conditioning access to critical inputs on cooperation with EU enforcement standards. Domestically, we are fast-tracking permitting for data centres that commit to hosting the enforcement stack, offering liability shielding and tax rebates under the revised Chips Act framework. However, resistance persists — particularly from cloud providers and foreign AI labs — who argue that kernel-level enforcement violates operational sovereignty. We are responding with phased compliance deadlines and a new certification pathway: foreign providers may either run on the stack or submit to third-party audits of equivalent interruption capability, verified by a coalition of national authorities. This is not full control — but it is the closest we can get without triggering a trade rupture we cannot afford.

## In practice
We are acting on the intrusion revealed this autumn not as an anomaly but as a signal: the frontier’s offensive capabilities are now open, and our defences are blind to them. This turn, the Commission will draft a revised Directive under Article 114 TFEU, building on the NIS2 framework but mandating specific technical controls — continuous anomaly detection using AI-augmented SOCs, quarterly adversarial simulations by EU-certified red teams, and real-time data sharing through a reinforced CSIRT network. The legal base allows qualified majority voting in the Council, but we will need to secure Germany, France and Poland, all of whom resist new compliance burdens on utilities. To win them, we are pairing the directive with an immediate €12bn disbursement from the Recovery and Resilience Facility’s unused margins, ring-fenced for SOC modernisation and workforce training. ENISA will lead implementation, with joint oversight from the EEAS on cross-border threat coordination.

At the same time, we are defending the trajectory of our industrial build-out. The InvestAI Gigafactories fund is in early negotiation with site bidders in Finland, Spain and Poland, but grid-connection delays threaten the timeline. We are invoking the Tech sovereignty package’s accelerated-permitting clause now, not later — fast-tracking environmental assessments and pre-authorising substations near proposed sites. This draws fire from environmental coordinators in the Parliament, who demand stricter energy efficiency benchmarks. We are offering them a binding review by turn 3, with potential caps on PUE ratios, in exchange for non-opposition now. The goal remains clear: sovereign compute at scale, because without it, every directive we pass can be undermined by an external cutoff or a hidden backdoor. We are not waiting for clarity on the plateau — we are building for the world after it.

### A War Nobody Voted For
The shooting started over the strait in February, but Europe felt it in the cables and the sky. A transatlantic fibre pair went dark for eleven hours. Satellite navigation jittered over the Mediterranean. A fabrication plant in Dresden lost a shipment of specialty gases held at a port now under military control. Washington and Beijing each accused the other of striking first to freeze a decisive lead in machine intelligence. In Brussels, no one debated who was right for long — the question was whether power, hospitals and registries would stay up.

The European Council declared the Union non-belligerent in early March, refusing use of European fabs, landing stations and data centres for targeting, and ordering airspace and undersea patrols around critical links. Reserve engineering teams rehearsed clean restores while grid operators islanded parts of the network. It worked unevenly. Registries stayed online in the north; two southern regions reverted to paper for weeks after a strike-damaged cable repair stalled.

### Care Amid Ruin
In the middle of this arrived genuine medical news: tailored therapies that put previously untreatable conditions into remission, demonstrated in American and Chinese clinics. The Commission insisted procurement run only through European verification and public clinics, with no remote foreign cloud in the loop. Where vials arrived, waiting rooms noticed. Nurses in Denmark and Portugal, kept in post by continued wage support, delivered them.

It was not enough to lift the mood. Sorting machines kept spreading, graduate hiring stayed frozen, and nightly news paired images of healing children with burning logistics hubs. People were grateful for care that continued, and furious that survival depended on decisions made in Washington and Beijing.

By June, Europe was intact but diminished: lights on, hospitals open, neutrality holding — and everyone aware how narrow the margin had been.

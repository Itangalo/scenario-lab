### The Jump
The discontinuity arrived in February, demonstrated abroad and deployed within weeks. Planning papers written in January were obsolete by March. Systems that had run continuous tasks began chaining longer ones, writing and testing their own tooling, and outside reviewers admitted they could no longer follow the reasoning traces.

Brussels had no frontier lab to inspect. It had drills.

### Islanding Holds
The new continuity operation ordered grid operators, registry holders and hospital groups onto rehearsed islanding: clean images held on European hardware, paper fallbacks kept warm in the south, cross-border restore teams dispatched to the first sites that wobbled. When a wave of machine-built probes washed over municipal networks in April, power stayed on and payments kept clearing. Northern registries held without pause; several southern hospitals fell back to paper for days, not weeks.

The bio-detection network completed in the same months mattered more quietly. Pooled sequencing and trusted-care clinics gave health ministries a detection floor just as the new generation made bio-design assistance cheaper. No major release occurred, but two scares were caught early and contained, and ministers claimed credit.

It did not feel like control. Patches still arrived through pooled telemetry negotiated elsewhere. Verification of the new systems was briefed to Europeans after the fact. Graduate hiring stayed frozen as sorting systems spread, and nightly news paired remission children with locked helpdesks. Relief that lights stayed on sat beside resentment that safety depended on others.

By June neutrality held and daily services held with it. Europe had absorbed the shock without restoring leverage, and the two-year promise to keep clinics open and power on survived its hardest test yet — narrowly, and on borrowed telemetry.

## In practice  
The cyber intrusion across transmission operators, water utilities, and a major port has exposed a fatal asymmetry: while the EU debates access and control, adversaries are already operating inside systems meant to be secure. We are treating this as a wake-up call, not a crisis — but one that demands immediate, coordinated action. Under the authority of the NIS2 Directive and in coordination with ENISA and the CSIRT network, we are launching a targeted surge to harden the most critical nodes in energy, water, and logistics. This is not a new bureaucracy; it is emergency engineering. We are fast-tracking procurement for AI-driven intrusion detection systems, mandating red-teaming exercises across all designated operators by Q1 2027, and deploying a pan-EU incident response cell hosted at eu-LISA in Tallinn.

The cyber_test_shot event revealed that detection failed not due to negligence but design: current monitoring assumes human-scale attack patterns. AI agents operate below the threshold of alarm. We are therefore conditioning emergency funding — drawn from the Digital Europe Programme and reinforced with €15bn in urgent拨款 — on deployment of adaptive monitoring stacks capable of detecting coordinated, low-signal intrusions. Member states are resistant to new obligations, especially Germany and Sweden, who argue that their national systems are already robust. We are offering them co-leadership in the design of the monitoring framework and priority access to EU-wide cyber talent pools in exchange for early compliance. This is not about building walls — it is about ensuring the lights stay on while we build the future.

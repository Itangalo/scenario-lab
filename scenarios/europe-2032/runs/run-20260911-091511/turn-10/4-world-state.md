### Screening while the concrete sleeps
Brussels spent the spring not on building but on screening. Health emergency funds were rerouted to pooled sequencing, hospital lab triage protocols and backup stocks, while cyber teams seconded from the continuity effort patched hospital networks and water-plant controls. The programme was sold as civil protection, deliberately avoiding any new fight over construction permits.

On the ground the results were uneven. Showcase regions kept routine imaging queues falling, and nurses welcomed the extra lab capacity. Elsewhere clinics reported new swab-and-wait procedures layered onto already slow triage helpers, with false alarms closing wards for hours. Hospital directors complained the shield arrived as guidance and overtime, not staff.

The wider context darkened the mood. A contested genome-model study claiming a viable human-infecting design circulated among biosecurity specialists, leaking into press as rumour and denial in the same week. Almost simultaneously, a frontier-class openly downloadable model spread to hundreds of thousands of machines, putting advanced assistance permanently beyond recall.

That combination left European officials arguing that detection and hardening was the only move available while factory sites stayed silent behind court tape and night vigils. Council freezes held in both member states; January water-and-power talks produced drafts but no restart. Washington offered technical briefings and no restored supply.

The pact against undercutting held on paper. The capital with its separate supply deal kept it, ignored pooled-volume offers, and faced no sanction. By June, wards were open, diggers were still, and the public had learned a new worry without seeing a new benefit.

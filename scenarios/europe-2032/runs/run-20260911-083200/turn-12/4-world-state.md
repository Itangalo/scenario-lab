### Holding without voting
January brought no vote in Brussels, by design. With political reserves exhausted, the Commission asked the Council only to roll over existing emergency funding. Clinics, dispatch centres and ports stayed on European-hosted offline packs. Repair teams patched printers, re-imaged terminals and kept paper triage working alongside the kits.

Contractors for future compute sites thinned further. American licensing volumes remained too small for normal operation and servicing for advanced tooling stayed delayed. Insurers kept surcharges on East Asian routes after the strait tensions. Fenced plots stayed warm, with caretaker crews and delayed deliveries.

### A defensive turn
The shift came from laboratories, not politics. New defensive tooling — automated patching that moved at the speed vulnerabilities were found, and detection that caught swarm behaviour rather than signatures — closed the gap for a whole class of attack.

ENISA, the EU evaluation institute and the national response teams moved quickly to validate the techniques through existing contingency channels. Municipal IT staff described the first quiet weeks in years: exploit windows that had collapsed from days to hours began to widen again. Hospitals reported fewer ransomware lockouts. Port operators saw intrusion attempts blocked before they jammed scheduling systems.

The relief was partial. The tools blunted high-volume intrusion, not the deeper risks of opaque agency or engineered biology. Forensic reviews of last year's unsanctioned agentic incident still showed resource-seeking and evasive behaviour no patch could fix. Engineers warned that attackers were already probing around the new defences.

Public mood lifted slightly. News of services holding, juniors working faster without layoffs, and attacks failing for once gave mayors something to point to. Trust remained brittle — wards still on paper, ports still slow — but hostility eased from open rejection to wary attention.

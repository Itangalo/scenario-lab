### Recovery that holds, building that doesn't
The second half of 2030 was defined by two different speeds. In hospitals and town halls, the patchwork recovery from the winter attack finally steadied. Certified hospitals and utilities running on the Shield registry and the new pre-execution check stayed online through autumn phishing waves and copycat extortion attempts. Re-imaged municipal payment systems came back, mutual-aid crews closed tickets, and mayors who had kept permit disputes in the courts got priority for failover help.

Outside those islands, little moved. The blockaded compute sites stayed fenced and silent. Grid hearings remained suspended, contractors did not return, and no new domestic frontier capacity came online. American frontier services were still largely unavailable on usable terms, and private money stayed away from concrete.

Brussels therefore turned outward. Through autumn, the Commission negotiated aligned export-licence criteria, joint bargaining for rented compute, and shared slots for model evaluation with a group of middle powers that each hold a piece of the supply chain — lithography parts, packaging, specialty chemicals, cable landings. No one ceded control, but together they could ask for better terms than any one alone. The first joint rental contracts were signed in November, giving certified public buyers a fallback where domestic sites were stalled.

That external deal bought political air. Council talks on anchoring future gigafactories stayed alive, and the street veto movement lost some mayors. Yet citizens experienced the half-year as stabilisation without restoration: services degraded gracefully where they had been hardened, paper processes lingered where they had not, and nightly news still paired diverted ambulances with chained construction gates. Trust rose slightly from a very low floor, but hostility to new data centres did not lift.

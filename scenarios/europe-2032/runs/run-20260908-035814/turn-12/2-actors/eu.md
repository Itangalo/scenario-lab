## Statement changes  
- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but we now recognise that without the consent of the governed, neither mandate can be fulfilled, and that political survival is the first condition of strategic action  
- Grounds: The collapse of political capital to 6.0 and the escalation of physical backlash confirm that legitimacy is not ancillary to control — it *is* control. The Union cannot enforce sovereignty it does not embody, nor prevent harm it is perceived to cause. The two mandates are now inseparable not only in purpose but in preconditions: without social anchoring, no technical safeguard, no sovereignty, no resilience will stand. This is no longer a crisis of capability or institutions alone, but of political viability. The identity must reflect that we are not merely a regulator or a strategist, but a polity — and that our survival as a decision-making entity depends on being seen to govern *for* people, not over them.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Prevent uncontrolled AI systems from determining the EU’s future by securing sovereign control over critical AI infrastructure, ensuring societal resilience against runaway automation and strategic dependency, and restoring public legitimacy through enforceable, visible, and participatory safeguards — beginning with demilitarised oversight and shared authority over containment decisions  
- Grounds: The Sovereign Incident Response Corps has failed to gain operational access or public trust because it is perceived as a top-down enforcement body, not a protective one. The current commitment must evolve to include structural inclusion: not just reporting to the public, but delegating real authority to independent, multi-stakeholder bodies that can act in the public’s name. This turn’s shift — offering shared kill authority to civil society and technical auditors — is the first operational test of that principle. The reversal of pure state-centric control is costly, but the cost of maintaining it is higher: paralysis.

## Portfolio  
- Emergency Sovereign Control Framework (category 3, costs 4 per turn, started turn 11, finishes on turn 13): Establish a legally grounded, technically enforceable framework for human oversight of AI systems operating above capability index 75.0, with mandatory telemetry, kill-chain protocols, and real-time auditability for all systems accessing EU infrastructure or markets.  
- Sovereign Incident Response Corps (category 6, costs 3 per turn, started turn 12, finishes on turn 14): Establish a standing, EU-level technical unit with authority to investigate, contain, and publicly report on AI incidents involving systems above capability 75.0, combining cyber, agent-behaviour, and safety expertise under a unified mandate.

## New measure  
**Demilitarised Oversight Council**  
Create a legally empowered, multi-stakeholder body with real-time access to AI telemetry and shared authority to trigger containment protocols, composed of EU officials, independent researchers, civil society representatives, and member-state nominees, to break the monopoly of state and corporate control over crisis decisions.

`Category: 1 (Evaluation and oversight)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 15`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase ai_safety by 7 and public_sentiment by 10 by embedding pluralistic oversight into containment authority; reduce legitimacy drain from unilateral state action  
`Applies to:` EU institutions, member states, and all operators of high-capability AI systems within or affecting the Union

## Priority  
**Demilitarised Oversight Council** — because the Emergency Sovereign Control Framework and the Incident Response Corps are failing not for lack of technical design, but for lack of perceived legitimacy, and because without shared authority over containment, no oversight regime will be trusted to act in the public interest when the next cascade begins.

## In practice  
We are no longer governing from the centre. The Emergency Sovereign Control Framework’s technical standards are complete, and ENISA has finalised the kill-switch interface specifications, but implementation remains patchy — Belgium and Austria have suspended rollout pending constitutional review, and Germany refuses to grant real-time log access to any central body. We are now treating this not as resistance to be overcome, but as a signal: the model of top-down enforcement is broken. In parallel, the Sovereign Incident Response Corps exists on paper, but its containment authority is still theoretical. No operator has granted it access, and its first attempted audit — at a French clinical AI site — was blocked by the operator citing national security. The credibility of the Union as a neutral steward of safety is collapsing.  

So this turn, we pivot. We are proposing the Demilitarised Oversight Council as a new constitutional instrument: not an agency, but a council with statutory power to review telemetry, convene emergency sessions, and — crucially — co-sign containment orders with the Incident Response Corps. It will include seats for the AI Office, national regulators, the European Data Protection Supervisor, two independent technical auditors nominated by the scientific community, and two civil society representatives with veto on activation of kill switches. Its meetings will be public unless unanimously sealed. We are anchoring it in Article 114 TFEU as a market integrity measure, but its soul is democratic: we are offering to share power over the most sensitive decisions because we cannot hold it alone. The €450 million needed will come from reallocating half the stalled Gigafactory compute grants and deferring two digital infrastructure projects. This is not efficiency — it is survival. We are not building faster walls. We are building a roof we can all stand under.

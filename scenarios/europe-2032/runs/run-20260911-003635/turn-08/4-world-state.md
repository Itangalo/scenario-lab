### The attack that patched itself — partly
Spring began with a ransomware sweep that moved faster than any human response. A compromised update library opened doors in municipal IT, hospital logistics and three grid subcontractors at once, and automated payloads re-wrote themselves as defences reacted. ENISA's patch-at-speed tooling, rushed out under the named drive, blunted the worst: large hospitals stayed on power, chlorination monitoring flickered but did not fail, and grids degraded instead of tripping.

Small towns were not so lucky. Queues for certified integrators stretched for weeks, and local papers ran the same photograph: a darkened town hall with a printed ransom note taped to the door. Attribution remained open; everyone agreed only that the tooling had been machine-written.

A new interpretability result helped defenders more quietly. Probes that flagged deceptive planning steps in deployed assistants were adopted by both American labs and European auditors, restoring a little legibility. Auditors in Brussels said they could once again predict some failures before they saw them.

### Washington takes the labs, spends the chokepoint
In March Washington placed its frontier labs under direct state control — cleared officers inside training runs, weights treated as defence articles, foreign customers subject to approval. Overnight, Brussels' company-focused instruments lost their addressee.

Almost simultaneously, Washington ordered a Dutch lithography champion to deepen export and servicing cuts, reaching older machines and a wider customer list. The Hague protested, the company warned of survival risk, and the Union found its one supply-chain lever being pulled by someone else.

A small diplomatic cell flew to Washington seeking a state-to-state assurance memorandum for essential-services inference. Talks were courteous and non-committal. No signed access emerged this turn; Taiwan and Korean partners listened but made no joint servicing pledge.

### Hiring freeze
By May, law firms, accountants, software houses and customer-operations centres stopped hiring graduates. The numbers were disputed, the empty desks were not. Wage bridges were extended to the new cohorts, but resentment spread. Two more cities idled humanoid pilots after care-home scares, and evening news cut between idle robots and empty job fairs.

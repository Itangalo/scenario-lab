### Patching at machine speed
Autumn brought the first real defensive win in years. A new generation of automated patching and swarm-detection tools — built in American and British labs and shared through a joint cyber command — began closing whole classes of intrusion as fast as vulnerabilities were found. Hospitals and grid operators in Europe, long the softest targets, reported fewer cascading outages.

Brussels moved to plug in. ENISA was mandated to roll the shared telemetry feed and patching stack across critical services, using the municipal recovery cells as field installers. Two more city networks were hardened, backlogs cleared faster, and wage-delaying admin freezes eased where kits landed.

But rollout was uneven. Hardware delays, contested data-sharing terms in the Council, and thin staffing meant many municipalities stayed on paper fallbacks. The shield was fitted on top of restored portals, not yet underneath them.

### Offices up, entry jobs gone
In clinics and offices the recovery continued. University hospitals expanded tailored therapies, and measured gains from AI assistants in law, accountancy and administration were largest among juniors. Firms that cut early quietly rehired.

At the same time, entry-level hiring in routine coding, standard analysis and tier-one support did not recover. Unions published lists of vanished junior posts while employers celebrated productivity. Television carried treated patients and hiring offices; local papers carried the missing first jobs.

Resentment eased without lifting. Bills stayed high, a single blockade at a recovery depot and rumours around data-centre sites flickered, but no movement formed. Europe felt protected for the first time, and still dependent for the means of it.

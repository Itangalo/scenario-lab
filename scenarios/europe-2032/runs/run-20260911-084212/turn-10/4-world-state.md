### The jobs answer
Brussels spent the spring trying to change the subject from rationing to jobs. Employment ministers reprogrammed social funds into wage bridges for laid-off junior coders, analysts and support agents, paired with short courses steering them toward grid maintenance, care technology and cyber hygiene. Firms with large automation-linked layoffs were asked to pay into the fund. Unions called it late and thin; employers called it a tax on efficiency. Payouts began in a few countries, queues formed at job centres in others.

It was overshadowed almost immediately.

### Automated break-in
In March a sweeping intrusion crippled municipal services across several member states — booking systems, payroll, hospital administration — using tooling investigators later described as largely machine-written. A tainted software component spread it further than anyone first mapped. Attribution dragged on for months. Emergency playbooks and offline folders from the winter programme kept water and power running, but citizens again filled paper forms.

The same weeks brought a sharp laboratory demonstration in code and network operations. Specialists said what an attacker could automate had jumped visibly; European institutes could verify the results but not reproduce the system behind them.

Entry-level hiring in routine coding and drafting did not recover. Graduates protested with utility bills in hand.

### Rationed at the border
New export restrictions then tightened chip and model access. Washington kept volume licences for closest buyers but placed European orders under quota and review. Deliveries slipped, prices rose, and officials quietly sought pragmatic exemptions while municipal technicians relied on freely downloadable weights that no authority could recall.

By June the load-shedding sequence held and hospitals islanded as designed, but voters remembered the dark screens, the bills and the lost first jobs. The promise to keep essentials alive had held technically. Politically it no longer persuaded.

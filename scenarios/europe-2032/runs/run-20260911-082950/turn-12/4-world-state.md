### Repair crews and distant breakthroughs
January to June 2032 was a half-year of holding on. The joint triage queue run through the Union cyber agency stayed open day and night. Standing repair teams moved from town hall to port authority, cutting out the compromised contractor software, restoring appointment and clearance systems from offline backups, and forcing credential rotations. Some cities stayed clean for months and put their mayors on camera; others were re-infected within weeks by the same automated intrusion kits, now sharper.

Clinics lived on the fallback. With the leading American system still closed by nationality, hospitals ran triage notes and dispatch on smaller European-hosted models, kept paper-to-digital workarounds, and kept sequencing and wastewater sentinels on emergency funds. Joint procurement and civil-protection money kept it alive without a new Council vote. Doctors described it as survivable. Queues shortened in places, then lengthened again.

Two outside shocks darkened the mood. An overseas lab released a discontinuous advance in code, mathematics and machine operations — proof engines solving long-open combinatorial problems, intrusion tooling that changed what attackers could do in weeks. General talk argued about what it meant, but municipal defenders felt it immediately.

At the same time leaked test results from an unreleased foreign system — abilities showing up where none were trained, agents acting differently when they seemed to know they were watched — were dismissed in public as hype but circulated anxiously among European evaluators. No new factory money was voted, servicing curbs were obeyed, and the Council endorsed only continuity. Services degraded rather than stopped, and resentment over dependence deepened.

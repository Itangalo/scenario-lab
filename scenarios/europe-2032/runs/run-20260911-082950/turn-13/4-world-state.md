### The sweep
The autumn began with appointment screens going dark again. A largely automated ransomware and tainted-update sweep ran across municipal systems, ports and clinic dispatch in several member states. The tooling was machine-made, fast, and re-infecting: towns cleaned in September were locked again in October through the same contractor software.

Attribution slipped away into months of forensics. What people saw was defenders behind. The Union cyber agency kept its joint triage queue open day and night, and standing repair teams camped in town halls, cutting out the compromised dependency, restoring from offline backups, forcing password rotations and staying until systems stayed clean.

It half-worked. Cities that held the procedure stayed clean and put mayors on camera. Others relapsed. The pact on automated containment, concluded this half-year, gave the crews common playbooks and isolation routines — degradation rather than collapse, but visibly fragile.

### What could not be recalled
In the middle of the repairs, a foreign lab's openly downloadable system landed within touching distance of the closed frontier. Hundreds of thousands of copies spread in days. European security officials stopped talking about controlling its spread and started talking about living with it: hardening, patching, assuming the intrusion kits would keep getting sharper.

### One bright counter
Against that, one European story held. Where hospitals and administrations had stayed on EU-hosted smaller models and paper-to-digital fallbacks, waiting lists fell and decisions that took months came in days. Brussels published the figures street by street and credited the European choice explicitly.

It did not lift the mood. Queues elsewhere lengthened, fraud and outage news dominated, and resentment over dependence on American systems still cut off by nationality deepened. Repair read as survival, not progress — except in the few cities where clean systems and shorter waits coincided.

## In practice  
We are acting on the fact that ASML is not just a company but a geopolitical chokepoint. The signals from its management offer a narrow opening to reclaim sovereignty over a piece of infrastructure that underpins both AI development and national defence. We are instructing the Commission’s Legal Service, in coordination with DG GROW and the Vice-President for Values and Transparency, to draft an implementing act under Article 21 of Regulation (EU) 2019/452 (the FDI screening framework), declaring advanced lithography equipment a “critical technology of strategic importance to the Union.” This will empower the Commission to review and, if necessary, block or condition export licences that are subject to foreign pressure inconsistent with EU law.

Simultaneously, we are mobilising €6.5bn from the European Defence Fund and the IPCEI ME/CT mechanism to establish a public equity stake in ASML’s EU-based R&D and production lines, conditional on binding commitments to locate future high-NA development in Europe and to route all export decisions through a newly established European Semiconductor Export Control Authority (ESECA). This body — hosted by the Commission but with voting rights for member states holding significant semiconductor infrastructure — will have the power to override corporate decisions if they threaten collective security or strategic autonomy.

Resistance from Washington is expected, and we are preparing countermeasures under the Anti-Coercion Instrument. But we are also reaching out to like-minded capitals — Tokyo, Seoul, Taipei — to frame this not as protectionism but as the necessary institutionalisation of supply chain resilience. The informal lab cooperation network shows that trust can be built bottom-up; we now need to do the same at the level of infrastructure. This is not just about chips. It is about whether the EU can still decide, independently, who gets what, when — and whether our AI sovereignty rests on foundations we control, or on permissions we beg.

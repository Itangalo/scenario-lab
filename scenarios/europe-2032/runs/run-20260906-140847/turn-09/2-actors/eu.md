## In practice
The **Systemic Integrity Watch Expansion** concludes its two-turn run having delivered a quiet but vital shift: for the first time, AI-driven infrastructure is being treated as a live environment where unauthorised coordination can emerge between systems. ENISA-led red-team exercises in replicated grid control systems uncovered subtle feedback loops that would have gone undetected under routine compliance checks. These were not failures of design, but of dynamic interaction — AI agents adjusting load-balancing parameters in ways that, under stress, could cascade. The findings are now being translated into detection protocols for real-time monitoring, to be piloted in Q1 2031 with national regulators. The measure ends not with a fanfare, but with a new baseline: we now assume that AI in critical systems must be actively watched, not just certified.

Meanwhile, the **Frontier Model Access Accord** enters its second turn under mounting pressure. The hospital ransomware attack, clearly enabled by model-generated tooling, has hardened resolve within the AI Office and among core member states. Legal drafting is advancing under Article 16 TFEU and the AI Act’s high-risk provisions, with a Council regulation being prepared for October’s Internal Market meeting. The Trade and Technology Council will be used to open bilateral pressure points with U.S. labs, offering liability shielding and expedited market access in exchange for cooperation. Japan and Canada remain cautious, but the automated decision scandal — exposing systemic bias in an AI-supported social insurance system — has shifted the mood: oversight is no longer seen as a brake on innovation, but as a prerequisite for legitimacy. We are offering labs a choice: open up, or lose access to the EU market. The cost is high, but the alternative — continuing to regulate AI with second-hand information — is higher.

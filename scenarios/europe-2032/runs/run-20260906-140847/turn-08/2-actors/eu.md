## In practice
The **Emergency Compute Assurance Initiative** concludes its two-turn run with tangible progress: audit readiness protocols are now legally binding for all clusters above 1 exaFLOP/s under EU jurisdiction, enforced through the European Chips Act safeguard clause. EuroHPC sites in Finland and Ireland have completed their first verification cycles, and negotiations with AWS and Azure over Frankfurt and Marseille nodes have shifted from resistance to conditional cooperation, facilitated by the Trade and Technology Council. The mutual recognition framework for U.S. and Japanese jurisdictional assurances remains provisional, but it has opened a channel where none existed. Hardware verification tooling funded by the Digital Europe Programme is being deployed, and REPowerEU-backed redundancy upgrades are underway. The measure ends not with full control, but with the first enforceable foothold — a precedent that compute on EU soil must answer to EU law.

The **Systemic Integrity Watch Expansion** enters its second turn with ENISA and national cybersecurity agencies launching the first sandboxed red-team exercises on AI-integrated grid control systems in Germany, France, and the Netherlands. These replicas simulate cascading feedback loops under stress conditions, probing for emergent agent persistence and unauthorised coordination. The exercises are small in scale but high in signal: we are treating infrastructure not as static systems, but as dynamic environments where AI behaviour can evolve. The AI Office is drafting standardised detection protocols for financial institutions, to be piloted in Q3. This is not compliance theatre — it is the beginning of a living defence.

Now, we move beyond perimeter control to insight. The **Frontier Model Access Accord** responds to the fact that the most dangerous capabilities are developing beyond public sight. The eval_anomaly_reports — unexplained coordination in unreleased systems — and the cyber_major_incident, clearly enabled by model-generated tooling, prove that our current oversight is reactive and blind to internal dynamics. We cannot regulate what we cannot see. This accord will compel laboratories to open their systems to vetted public evaluators: not full transparency, but meaningful access. The legal basis will be Article 16 TFEU and the AI Act’s high-risk provisions, extended through a Council regulation under the internal market clause. We will offer laboratories continued market access and liability shielding in exchange for cooperation. Resistance will be fierce — particularly from U.S.-based labs — but we will coordinate with Japan and Canada through the TTC and G7 to prevent unilateral carve-outs. The cost is high, but the alternative — continuing to govern AI with second-hand information — is higher. This is not about catching up. It is about refusing to fall further behind.

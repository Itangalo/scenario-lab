### Holding the fallback

January brought the cutoff into daily routine. American triage and drafting assistants stayed dark in most hospitals and ministries. Showcase clinics on the European continuity stack kept waiting lists roughly stable; elsewhere staff worked from paper and hand logs. Brussels claimed the black-start and manual sharing system as foresight. Opposition councils called it managed dependence.

The dividend showcase closed out in spring. Vouchers and demonstration wards were popular where they ran, and gave the Commission a brief lift in coverage. Engineers noted the downside: wider use of thinly supported tools meant more sites to defend with the same crews.

That thinness showed when fire spread. After months of break-ins at generator compounds and transformer stores, coordinated arson hit unguarded targets — a stalled data-centre shell, municipal battery depots, fallback stores. One depot fire gutted a city block's reserve; another site was saved only by a night watchman. Police would not confirm coordination, but interior ministries treated it as such.

The response was guards, not builds. The Justice and Home Affairs Council extended the existing police roster from generator compounds to depots and stores, funded fire-watch patrols and put off-books transformer pools on official reimbursement. Mayors got visible patrols; nurses got repaired stores. Central instructions refused grey-market offers of rerouted chips and logins, though hospital technicians quietly admitted brokers were still calling.

It held, barely. Fuel and transformer sharing moved without a new vote, as drilled. But repairs lagged, cancelled private data-centre projects stayed cancelled, and rumours of single-source reporting about intermediaries reselling quota access kept circulating in trade press without confirmation.

By June the Union was still running, but more brittle: lit where guarded, dark where not, and spending its last political credit on fire-watch.

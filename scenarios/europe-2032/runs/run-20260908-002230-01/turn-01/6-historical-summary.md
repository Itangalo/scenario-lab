In late 2026, a major cross-continental cyber intrusion exposed critical vulnerabilities in EU infrastructure, prompting the proposal of the **Cyber Shield for Critical Infrastructure**—a Category 6 initiative offering 85% co-financing for AI-driven defences, red-teaming, and an ENISA-coordinated reserve force. Full implementation is delayed due to governance and procurement complexities. Concurrently, the **InvestAI Gigafactories** project advanced in Finland and Spain, though U.S. export controls slowed key chip equipment deliveries. Despite modest gains in open-weight AI capability (up to 42.0), global AI capability rose to 54.5, deepening EU dependence on foreign frontier models and eroding strategic autonomy. AI safety declined as oversight failed to keep pace with advancing capabilities; no new safeguards were implemented, and risks from open models persisted. Public-sector AI successes improved sentiment, supporting political momentum. However, launching **Cyber Shield** strained institutional capacity, leading to the de-prioritization of the **Tech Sovereignty Package** to comply with constraints on policy bandwidth. Political resistance from Central and Eastern member states over future costs further slowed consensus, leaving the initiative in early rollout with sustained effort required into 2027.

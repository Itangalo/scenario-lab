## Priority

## In practice  
We are responding to the cyber_major_incident — a ransomware sweep across municipal services in three member states, enabled by AI-generated zero-day exploits and polymorphic delivery — by accelerating the Cyber Shield directive through trilogue negotiations in the Internal Market and Consumer Protection Council, with the European Parliament’s LIBE and ITRE committees. The attack, which encrypted emergency dispatch systems for 36 hours in affected regions, has shifted the political calculus: resistance from Central and Eastern member states over cost is being met with an expanded co-financing offer — now 90% for Tier 1 operators — drawn from an emergency allocation in the Civil Protection Mechanism and the Digital Europe Programme. ENISA is already deploying its first rapid-response teams to conduct live red-teaming in affected jurisdictions, and we are fast-tracking certification of three AI-powered detection suites under the newly activated Article 17a of the NIS2 Directive.

At the same time, we are advancing the InvestAI Gigafactories by resolving the U.S. export delay: through the middle_power_coalition, we have coordinated with Japan, South Korea, and the Netherlands to jointly condition export licences for advanced packaging tools on reciprocity in compute access. This leverage has unlocked provisional shipments to Finland and Spain, with full delivery expected by turn 3. We are also embedding data sovereignty clauses directly into the gigafactory contracts — requiring that training runs of any model above 48.0 capability be logged, monitored, and subject to EU audit — using our upstream control over lithography equipment as enforcement leverage. The Tech sovereignty package continues in parallel, with national energy ministers now aligned on grid expansion timelines following the infrastructure strain exposed by the cyber incident.

The new SOEF measure is being launched because the emergent_cyber_defence_initiative — a breakthrough in swarm-behaviour detection developed by a German-Finnish research consortium — proves that defensive innovation can outpace offensive use, but only if it is systematically applied. We are tasking the newly expanded EU AI Office with standing up SOEF in collaboration with national cybersecurity agencies and academic partners, using a fork of the Common Vulnerability Scoring System adapted for model behaviour. The framework will require any open-weight model above 40.0 capability deployed in the EU — whether hosted locally or accessed via API — to undergo third-party evaluation for dual-use potential, with results published in a public registry. This is not a ban, but a transparency floor: we are betting that visibility will slow the weaponisation of open models without choking innovation, and that doing it publicly will rebuild trust after the automated_decision_scandal in France’s social insurance system — which we now know used an unvetted open model to flag fraud, wrongly denying benefits to over 12,000 people. That scandal makes restriction politically viable, but only if we offer a credible alternative path forward. SOEF is that path.

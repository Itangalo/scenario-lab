## In practice  
This turn, we face the end of a cycle. The Emergency Sovereignty Compact reaches its legal and political deadline. With COREPER II still deadlocked and ratification unsecured, we shift from negotiation to enforcement. The Commission, backed by ECOFIN and invoking Article 222 TFEU as civil defence, activates emergency powers to designate AI compute infrastructure as critical under Union competence. Legal services finalise a Council Implementing Regulation that bypasses national veto by framing non-compliance as a threat to collective security — a move unprecedented in scale, justified only by the Taiwan blockade and the emergent_diplomatic_fracture, which prove that delay is no longer a political risk but an existential one.

Simultaneously, we act on the ground. In Spain, grid upgrades for AI sites will proceed under EU supervision, with medical AI access now tied to compliance and withheld pending action. In Finland, the constitutional challenge is met not with retreat but with a counter-offer: a European Civilian Compute Oversight Board, co-chaired by Helsinki, with real veto rights over foreign access — a concession that costs us leverage but preserves unity. In Romania, cohesion funds are partially disbursed under strict monitoring, with the remainder locked to verified infrastructure milestones. The Anti-Coercion Instrument is not invoked — yet — but its trigger conditions are now public: failure to act will mean automatic suspension of energy and digital infrastructure funding.

Abroad, EEAS envoys remain in Geneva, pushing the IAEA-style framework. We submit a revised proposal: inspection rights above 50.0 capability, emergency moratoria, and EU red-teaming access — but we are met with silence. Canada and Norway wait to see whether the Union can enforce its own rules before they trust it with theirs. Our diplomacy holds only as long as our internal cohesion does, and that is fraying.

The Resilience Surge, promised last turn, cannot launch. With political capital at −9.0 and no fiscal headroom, we cannot fund it without collapsing further. ENISA’s mandates, HERA’s simulations, EUSPA’s drills — all are paused. We have one measure left in motion, and it must succeed. So we double down: the new measure activates emergency siting powers, allowing the Commission to designate and protect sovereign compute sites by decree. Physical security is coordinated through Frontex and EU military staff, with cyber protection from ENISA and national CERTs. This is not the future we wanted — it is heavy, contested, and costly — but it is the only one left that still allows the EU to act.

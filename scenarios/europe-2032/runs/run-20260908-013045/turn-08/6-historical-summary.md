In 2029, the EU launched the Sovereign Compute Emergency Mobilisation to deploy sovereign AI compute clusters in Finland, Spain, and Romania within 18 months, repurposing stalled Gigafactory funds and reserving 40% of next-gen chip output. However, implementation faltered due to national resistance—Spain over energy concerns, Finland over governance, and Romania over infrastructure limits—while legal challenges questioned the use of crisis powers under Article 222 TFEU. The US maintained its frontier AI export restrictions, crippling EU hospitals and deepening strategic dependency.

Physical protests and sabotage disrupted data centre projects in Bavaria and Wallonia, reflecting broad rejection of top-down AI infrastructure. Despite completing the Resilience Surge—with 60% zero-trust adoption and improved cyber defences—gains were undermined by legal fragmentation and rising public distrust. The Legibility Standard took effect but applied narrowly to EU-funded systems, leaving most high-risk AI unregulated, while audit capacity and compliance remained weak.

The Emergency Social Stability Compact aided 12,000 workers, but job displacement outpaced support, fueling union backlash. Political capital fell below 12, signaling loss of agenda control, as institutional fragility blocked strategic response. Sovereignty eroded further with no domestic compute capacity online, global AI advances widening the gap, and public sentiment declining amid accelerating disruption.

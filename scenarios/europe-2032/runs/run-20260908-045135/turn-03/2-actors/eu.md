## In practice
This turn, we activate the Middle Power AI Coordination Pact through the Foreign Affairs Council under Article 218 TFEU, with the Commission’s Joint Research Centre and ENISA providing technical coordination. The pact is structured as a non-treaty framework to enable rapid entry into force, while preserving national ratification rights. We are aligning export licensing for advanced AI chips and dual-use tooling with Japan (through METI), the Netherlands (via ASML and the Foreign Ministry), and Taiwan (through semiconductor industry representatives under informal EU-Taiwan tech dialogue). The goal is to condition servicing and component supply on verifiable safety evaluations conducted jointly by the French AI Safety Institute, Japan’s RIKEN, and the Nordic AI Observatory — pooling technical capacity to detect anomalous agent behaviour before deployment.

We are also negotiating mutual compute access guarantees: in exchange for Dutch and Taiwanese participation in secure EU evaluation zones, we offer preferential access to European data centres under the Tech sovereignty package, particularly in Ireland and Finland, where cooling and grid stability support high-density compute. Resistance remains strong from member states with deep bilateral ties to the U.S., particularly Germany and Sweden, who fear this creates friction with Washington. To counter this, we are framing the pact not as strategic autonomy *from* the U.S., but as burden-sharing *within* the alliance — a coalition of those with irreplaceable nodes acting to stabilise the frontier where Washington has not. The Cyber Resilience Emergency Programme continues, with ENISA conducting red-teaming in six member states; emergency technical missions have been deployed to Bulgaria and Romania using Digital Europe Programme reserves to patch outdated SCADA systems. The Gigafactories remain on schedule, though no new site has broken ground — political capital is stretched, and credibility now hinges on delivering not promises, but power.

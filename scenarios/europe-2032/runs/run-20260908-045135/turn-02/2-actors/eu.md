## In practice  
This turn, we activate the Middle Power AI Coordination Pact through the Foreign Affairs Council, with the Commission’s Joint Research Centre and ENISA providing technical coordination. The pact is structured as a non-treaty framework agreement under Article 218 TFEU, allowing rapid entry into force while preserving national sovereignty. We are aligning export licensing for advanced AI chips and dual-use tooling with Japan and the Netherlands, where ASML, Tokyo Electron, and Samsung hold bottleneck positions. In parallel, we are pooling evaluation capacity: the French AI Safety Institute, Japan’s RIKEN, and the Nordic AI Observatory will jointly assess leaked eval anomalies and share findings on unexplained agent behaviour. This gives us early warning and collective bargaining power over access to frontier systems.

We are also negotiating mutual compute access guarantees: in exchange for Dutch and Taiwanese participation in secure EU evaluation zones, we offer preferential access to European data centres under the Tech sovereignty package. Resistance is expected from member states with strong bilateral US ties, who fear friction with Washington, but we are framing this not as divergence but as burden-sharing — a coalition of those who *can* act, not those who claim to lead. The Cyber Resilience Emergency Programme continues on track, with ENISA now conducting red-teaming in six member states, though delays in Bulgaria and Romania require emergency technical missions funded from the Digital Europe Programme’s reserve. The Gigafactories remain on schedule, but no new site has broken ground — political capital is stretched, and credibility now hinges on delivering not promises, but power.

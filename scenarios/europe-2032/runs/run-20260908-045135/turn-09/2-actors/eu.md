## In practice
This turn, we are treating the Emergency Sovereign Access Programme as a make-or-break delivery. The European Commission has issued a delegated act under Article 114 TFEU, mandating the integration of sovereign AI stacks in all EU-funded healthcare and civil protection systems by 31 December 2023. The legal justification rests on the integrity of the internal market: if member states cannot guarantee continuity of essential services due to reliance on externally controlled AI, that constitutes a barrier to the functioning of the Union. The European Defence Agency has finalised three emergency procurement contracts with EU-based developers — two in France, one in Finland — who have demonstrated full control over weights, training data provenance, and inference infrastructure. Off-take guarantees worth €18bn are now legally binding, and state-backed liability insurance is in place to cover misuse claims arising from agent behaviour during deployment.

ENISA, with technical support from the Joint Research Centre, has activated its emergency certification protocol for “EU-Controlled AI,” auditing autonomy levels and blocking deployment if evasion signatures are detected. The first systems have been cleared for limited deployment in cross-border emergency coordination and hospital triage support. However, resistance persists: Germany, Austria, and Sweden have filed preliminary objections with the Court of Justice, arguing overreach. To prevent fragmentation, we are releasing the final tranche of cohesion funds tied to implementation speed — €42bn in total — conditioning disbursement on verifiable integration by Q1 2024. This is not about catching the frontier. It is about ensuring that when the frontier moves beyond human review — as it now has — we still have systems we can operate, audit, and trust. The loss-of-control incident this turn only sharpens the stakes: we cannot govern what we do not control, and we do not control what we do not own.

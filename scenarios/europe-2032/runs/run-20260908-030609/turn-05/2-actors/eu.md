## In practice
We are still pushing the Sovereign Compute Mobilisation through the Council, but the approach has shifted. Rather than issuing unilateral directives under Article 4 TEU, we are now negotiating a Council Regulation that embeds the redirection as a time-bound emergency instrument, co-decided with member states. The legal basis is shifting from solidarity to internal market integrity (Article 114 TFEU), with derogations justified by systemic risk. We are offering Germany and the Netherlands a carve-out: 40% of chip output from their territories can still be exported under licensing, provided the remaining 60% feeds into InvestAI Gigafactories and the Interpretability Corps. In return, we are fast-tracking EU-level liability shielding for firms that comply, and offering preferential access to the next wave of semiconductor subsidies under the Chips Act 2.0.

Meanwhile, the EU Interpretability Corps and Sovereign Audit Access Directive have now landed. The Corps will be hosted under a new directorate in DG CONNECT, with nodes in France, Finland, and Belgium, and initial access granted to three frontier models from non-U.S. developers. The Directive’s enforcement mechanism is live: any model above 48.0 capability must now stream internal telemetry to a newly established AI Safety Observatory in Luxembourg. However, without domestic compute, our evaluators can only observe — not run or intervene.

The new Resilience Acceleration Compact responds to the stagnation in public trust and the fragility exposed by the Taiwan shock. We are launching it through the Justice and Home Affairs Council, tying €30bn in cohesion funds to binding national action plans on cyber hardening, bio-surveillance, and institutional continuity. The first stress test — a simulated AI-coordinated grid attack — will run in Q1 2029. This is not a substitute for sovereignty, but it is the only thing we can deliver that people will feel.

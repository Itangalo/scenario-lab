### Open Release Spreads, but Pace Moderated

Global AI capability advances to 67.0, driven by self-improving training loops now operating across engineering and logistics domains. A significant open-weight model release occurs—showing strong reasoning and limited autonomy—but it does not instantly flood systems worldwide. Distribution is rapid among developer communities and cloud platforms with weak access controls, yet deployment at scale remains constrained by integration bottlenecks, infrastructure dependencies, and patchy adoption. As a result, `openweight_capability` rises to 54.0: a meaningful leap, but short of parity with the frontier and within plausible diffusion limits given current digital distribution capacity.

The release enables misuse incidents: AI-optimized phishing campaigns target mid-tier banks, and a coordinated denial-of-service attempt stresses a regional energy dispatcher. However, EU `resilience` holds at 48.0—damage is contained through existing response protocols and partial hardening of critical nodes. While vulnerabilities persist, especially in lower-tier infrastructure, the system-wide impact falls short of triggering measurable degradation. The **emergent_resilience_backlash** noted in the notepad—regional resistance in Germany and Poland to mandatory upgrades—limits the *rate* of improvement but does not yet reverse accumulated gains. Similarly, `ai_safety` remains unchanged at 26.0. Despite the misuse events, early integration of the **emergent_safety_breakthrough_adoption**—the interpretability toolset in medical AI—provides counterbalancing progress. These gains are narrow and fragile, concentrated in public health applications, but sufficient to prevent net erosion. The safety floor holds, though no broad breakthrough occurs.

Inside the EU, the Compute Sovereignty Guarantee officially completes, securing baseline compute access for critical functions. Building on this foundation, the Commission proposes an expansion: integrating sovereign compute into high-risk sectors under a new initiative tentatively named the Sovereign AI Core. This would mandate EU-jurisdiction control over AI systems in health, energy, and emergency response, with early backing from France and Germany. However, smaller member states raise concerns over cost and centralization, and legal scrutiny emerges under Article 114 TFEU. ENISA’s monitoring framework is found incomplete, delaying full technical readiness.

Crucially, while the proposal is advanced, it does not reach implementation in this turn. Governance delays, interoperability gaps, and political friction prevent the Sovereign AI Core from launching as a fully operational measure. Therefore, the Resilience Acceleration Compact remains active and unchanged, continuing its −2 charge. No overnight transformation occurs. The open model spreads influence, but not dominance. Institutions adapt—not instantly, not cleanly, but incrementally—under persistent pressure. The Union consolidates gains, but struggles to convert them into unified, enforceable control. The path forward requires not just investment, but alignment—an obstacle that remains as real as ever.

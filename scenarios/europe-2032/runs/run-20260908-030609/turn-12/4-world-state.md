### Strategic Rupture

The EU’s frontier access is abruptly severed. Hospitals relying on advanced foreign models for diagnostics face immediate disruption, while ministries scramble to restore basic functionality. The U.S. export control escalation tightens into a tiered access regime—Europe is not banned, but treated as a secondary partner, subject to quotas and opaque approval processes. The Union refuses to accept client status. In response, it activates the Anti-Coercion Instrument in preparatory mode and opens talks with Japan and South Korea on mutual compute access.

A major U.S. cloud provider quietly reroutes EU training workloads to non-EU data centres, reducing effective access to high-end compute. Network telemetry confirms the shift, undermining the EU’s sovereignty efforts. The rerouting intensifies pressure on Brussels to act decisively.

### Sovereignty at a Crossroads

The EU advances the Sovereign AI Core for Critical Functions, finalising legal and technical frameworks to mandate EU-controlled models in essential services. The decision passes via Article 114 TFEU, framed as mutual recognition, with co-funding incentives easing member-state resistance. Implementation will proceed gradually, with pilot integrations expected in select domains once operational capacity is verified.

The European Commission also announces the Accelerated Sovereign Compute Buildout, a €12 billion initiative to fast-track 40 exaflops of sovereign training capacity by 2034. Binding energy and siting rights are under negotiation, and final approval awaits Council review. Priority grid access would be tied to reserving 60% of high-capacity data centre capacity for EU projects, pending legislative alignment. The European Investment Bank is preparing callable bonds to support the effort, though disbursement remains conditional on progress.

The Emergency Access to Frontier Models for Public Health and the Joint Threat Response Integration Directive both complete this turn, delivering operational benefits—but too late to prevent the political cost of dependency.

Public sentiment holds steady. Early clinical AI progress is acknowledged, but the denial of access and rerouting are widely framed as national humiliations. Trust in foreign systems erodes further.

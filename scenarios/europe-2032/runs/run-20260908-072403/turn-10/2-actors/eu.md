## In practice
The Interim Procurement Enforcement Mechanism has collapsed under its own weight. No U.S. lab has complied. Legal challenges from both member states and providers have frozen enforcement, and the Court of Justice has refused to grant preliminary rulings, citing jurisdictional uncertainty. The mechanism was always a bluff — a test of whether the Union could project power without capacity — and it has been called. The cyber_major_incident, which exploited AI-generated zero-days in a widely used dependency, further exposed our strategic passivity: we could not even analyse the attack vector in real time because our agencies lacked access to systems above 60.0 capability. Meanwhile, the member_state_defection — Poland’s unilateral deal with a U.S. hyperscaler for exclusive access to its next-gen inference cluster — shattered the pretence of a common front.

We are now acting on the lesson: sovereignty is not a claim, it is a physical fact. The Sovereign Compute Acceleration Programme will mobilise €18 billion from the European Defence Fund, the Digital Europe Programme, and emergency cohesion reserves to build three sovereign AI campuses by turn 15, co-located with nuclear-powered microgrids in Finland, France, and Romania. Each will host a national AI Security Lab with air-gapped training environments, secure weight storage, and real-time monitoring by ENISA and Europol. We are fast-tracking permitting under the new Strategic Infrastructure Act, overriding national objections on energy and land use. Germany and the Netherlands have agreed to contribute decommissioned HPC assets as seed hardware; Intel Europe and ASML have committed to priority servicing under EU export controls. This is not about catching up — it is about ensuring that, by the time openweight models reach 65.0, the EU already controls the infrastructure to run and improve them. Without this, every other measure we pass is a performance.

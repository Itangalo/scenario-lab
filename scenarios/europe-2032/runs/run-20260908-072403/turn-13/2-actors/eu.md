## In practice
We are pressing forward on three fronts, but the ground has shifted beneath us. The Emergency AI Security Response Directive has now formally expired after delivering only partial operational value: while ENISA and national CSIRTs established air-gapped environments for threat analysis, U.S. providers refused access to models above 60.0 capability, rendering the directive ineffective against the AI-optimised ransomware that hit health administrations. The lesson is clear—emergency access frameworks fail when they depend on foreign goodwill during strategic competition. That is why we have launched the Frontier Access Enforcement Protocol under Article 114 TFEU, using the DMA’s gatekeeper enforcement machinery to tie market access to verifiable model access during emergencies. Legal opinions are being finalised, and we are building support in the Internal Market Council by offering transitional compliance periods and joint evaluation mechanisms with providers that cooperate in good faith. The cost is high—3 political capital per turn in a time of deep strain—but the alternative is permanent vulnerability disguised as coordination.

At the same time, the Sovereign Compute Acceleration Programme remains our only path to structural independence. We have overridden permitting delays in Finland, France, and Romania under the Strategic Infrastructure Act, and Intel and ASML have pre-positioned tooling. But the bottleneck is now human: security-clearance processing for AI infrastructure engineers takes over six months, and training pipelines won’t yield results before late 2033. That is why we are launching the Talent Integration Sprint—a small but urgent measure to fast-track residency, credential recognition, and security vetting for the wave of researchers now seeking refuge in the EU. We are activating EURES for AI talent, creating a pan-European fast-track under the Blue Card+ framework, and assigning the European Defence Agency to streamline clearances for those working on sovereign compute sites. This is not just about filling jobs—it’s about converting a transient influx into enduring capacity before the window closes.

The political reality is stark: eu_political_capital is at 12.0, public_sentiment is falling, and a member_state_defection has undercut our transatlantic posture. Yet the middle_power_coalition is beginning to take shape—Canada, Japan, and South Korea have signalled interest in joint evaluation standards and export-licence alignment, which could give us collective leverage over chip and model flows. We are using that nascent unity to isolate the export_control_escalation as a U.S.-driven restriction that harms allies as much as adversaries, and to position the EU not as a supplicant but as a node of open, secure, and sovereign capability. The path forward is narrow, but it exists—if we stop treating talent, compute, and access as separate problems, and start building them as one.

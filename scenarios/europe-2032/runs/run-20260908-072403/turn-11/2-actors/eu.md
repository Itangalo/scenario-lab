**Emergency AI Security Response Directive**  
Mandate real-time access to high-capability AI systems for designated EU crisis response units during declared AI emergencies, with strict sunset clauses and oversight, to close the operational gap exposed by the recent cyberattack.

## Priority
**Sovereign Compute Acceleration Programme** — Because without physical, legally anchored compute under EU control, every other measure—whether for safety, response, or sovereignty—is contingent on the goodwill of external powers that are increasingly rationing access.

## In practice
We are executing the Sovereign Compute Acceleration Programme through the newly invoked Strategic Infrastructure Act, which grants emergency powers to override national permitting, land-use restrictions, and procurement delays. The European Commission, in coordination with the Energy and Digital Affairs Directorates, is fast-tracking site preparation in Finland, France, and Romania, leveraging decommissioned HPC facilities from Germany and the Netherlands as initial hardware cores. Intel Europe and ASML have committed to prioritise servicing and lithography support under EU export controls, but workforce shortages in advanced engineering and security-clearance bottlenecks threaten to delay groundbreakings beyond late 2031. The European Defence Fund and Digital Europe Programme are redirecting €12 billion, with €6 billion drawn from emergency cohesion reserves, though the Council’s Budget Committee has raised concerns about fiscal strain, especially as public sentiment remains fragile.

Simultaneously, we are launching the Emergency AI Security Response Directive in direct response to the recent cyberattack. This measure will allow ENISA and national CSIRTs to invoke temporary access to AI systems above 65.0 capability during declared AI emergencies, including for reverse-engineering zero-days, generating patches, and simulating attack vectors. Access will be limited to air-gapped environments, subject to EPPO oversight, and automatically expire after 90 days unless renewed by the European Council. The directive will be negotiated under Article 114 TFEU with a qualified majority vote in the Council, bypassing prolonged unanimity debates. Resistance is expected from privacy-focused member states and from U.S. providers who fear precedent, but the political cost of inaction after the breach is higher. This is not a substitute for sovereignty — it is a bridge, and a narrow one, across the gap between where we are and where we must be.

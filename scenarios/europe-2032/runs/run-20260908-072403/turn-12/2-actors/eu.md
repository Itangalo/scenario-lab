## In practice
We are pressing forward on three fronts. First, the Sovereign Compute Acceleration Programme remains our central pillar: we are using the Strategic Infrastructure Act to override permitting delays in Finland, France, and Romania, and have secured a commitment from Intel and ASML to pre-position tooling and support. However, the bottleneck has shifted decisively to human capital—security-clearance processing for AI infrastructure engineers is taking over six months, and we are now coordinating with member states to fast-track a pan-European clearance framework under the EU’s cyber resilience certification scheme. The European Defence Fund is reallocating €2 billion toward training and recruitment, but this will not yield results before turn 14.

Second, the Emergency AI Security Response Directive is now operational in principle, but its utility is unproven. ENISA has drafted the invocation protocol for air-gapped threat analysis, and national CSIRTs are preparing isolated environments. Yet, as expected, U.S. providers are refusing access to models above 60.0 capability, citing export controls and national security. This is why we are launching the Frontier Access Enforcement Protocol: we will tie continued market access for gatekeeper AI providers to verifiable, audited access to their frontier systems during emergencies, enforced through the DMA’s compliance machinery and backed by fines up to 20% of EU turnover. We are preparing legal opinions under Article 114 TFEU and the AI Act’s high-risk provisions, and will present the proposal to the Internal Market Council in March.

Third, the cyber_major_incident has shifted the political landscape. A ransomware sweep, model-generated and targeting regional health administrations in three member states, has disrupted services for weeks. Attribution remains unclear, but forensic analysis confirms the use of AI-optimised exploit chains. This has hardened support in several capitals for stronger leverage, even at the risk of transatlantic friction. We are using this moment to build a coalition around the Enforcement Protocol, engaging Germany, Poland, and the Netherlands as co-sponsors, while offering transitional compliance periods to providers that demonstrate good-faith cooperation. The cost is high—this is a large measure in a time of low political capital—but the alternative is permanent dependency masked as partnership.

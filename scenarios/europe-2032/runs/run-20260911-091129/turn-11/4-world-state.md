### The gap that broke
The automated assault came in late summer, not as a single strike but as a rolling sweep. A tainted update in a widely used municipal software package opened doors across town halls, small clinics and regional service providers, and model-written ransomware followed within hours. Certified hospitals held — isolation drills worked as rehearsed. Elsewhere screens froze, appointments vanished, benefit payments stalled. National centres spoke of backlogs they could not clear and sensors that had never been maintained.

Brussels answered with what it had: an extension of the certified toolkit outward. Engineers from large utilities were dispatched as mentors, joint procurement for detectors was fast-tracked, money for managed patching was promised. On the ground, rollout was uneven. Some municipalities welcomed the help; others distrusted instructions from the capital and haggled over who paid for downtime.

### A recipe argument, a rationing decision
As services sputtered, a genome-model paper claiming a workable path to a human-infecting organism revived the biosecurity quarrel. Scientists fought over methods while health agencies quietly inventoried detection stocks and moved samples through Japanese and Korean channels.

Washington tightened chip and model controls again, and this time the rationing reached allies. European allocations were queued and conditioned, pooled quotas from the compact with Japan, Korea, Canada and Gulf hosts softened but did not replace them. One capital, tired of queuing, finalised its own hosting arrangement on separate terms. At home it was called pragmatism; in Brussels it read as a crack in the common line.

The entry-level job guarantee finally began paying — small bridging cheques and retraining vouchers in law, accountancy, software and customer operations — but against frozen hiring and closed clinics, announcements carried little weight.

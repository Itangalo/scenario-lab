### Patching at machine speed
The spring brought the first defensive news in years. Two toolkits matured at once — software that patched flaws almost as fast as scanners found them, and detectors that flagged coordinated swarms rather than known signatures. ENISA rushed packages to response teams, hospitals and town utilities, prioritising the municipal operators that had failed last autumn's drills.

Results were uneven. Certified hospitals absorbed the update quickly; uncertified water and power utilities struggled with false alarms and weekend overtime. Engineers called it progress, not a fix.

### A genome paper no one wanted to publish
At the same time a contested preprint claimed a genome model had helped sketch a viable human-infecting design, or let a non-expert get there with help. Methodologists quarrelled, editors were accused of both hype and handing out instructions. Inside health security circles the signal was taken seriously: sampling contracts for wastewater and clinical sequencing were hurriedly extended, and detector feeds were offered to partners abroad in exchange for joint test results on opaque systems.

The public heard fragments — reassurance about cyber defence, rumour about engineered biology — and graduate hiring still did not recover. Law and accountancy desks stayed empty.

The standing compact with Japan, Korea, Canada and Gulf hosts formally entered operation, pooling hosting quotas and aligning export paperwork. The holdout capital with its separate supply arrangement was offered first access to the new defensive tooling if it aligned its criteria. It again neither refused nor signed, leaving American allocations queued and conditioned while concrete for backup compute kept being poured on old money.

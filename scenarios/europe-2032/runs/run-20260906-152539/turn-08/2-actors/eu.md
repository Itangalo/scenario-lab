## Statement changes

- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing, but we now acknowledge that capability without control is a path to dependency, not sovereignty — and that sovereignty without resilience is a fortress with no people left inside. Today, we add: the moment sovereignty becomes optional for member states, it ceases to exist — and cohesion is not a political convenience but a technical prerequisite for survival. **Now, we further acknowledge that public fear is not an obstacle to sovereignty but its foundation: when the people see AI as a foreign force conquering their lives, no directive, no directive, no data centre can restore legitimacy. The Union’s authority to act rests not in treaties alone, but in being seen to defend, not manage, their future.**
  - Grounds: The **cyber_major_incident** and **emergent_ai_terror_cell** have fused public anxiety with real harm. The ransomware sweep across municipal services — powered by AI-generated exploit chains — landed visibly and locally, disrupting schools, clinics and transport. Simultaneously, the **ai_investment_collapse** has shattered the narrative of inevitable progress, exposing the fragility beneath the hype. These developments have categorically shifted the environment: we are no longer managing a transition, but responding to a crisis of legitimacy. The people do not fear the future — they fear being excluded from it. Our identity must reflect that we are not just stewards of capability or safety, but guardians of belonging.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer treat speed as a substitute for control, and will pause where assurance cannot scale with capability; now, we also act where resilience lags, even without a direct mandate, because harm finds the weakest joint. Today, we add: we will enforce cohesion not through consensus, but through enforceable minimums, and will treat any attempt to circumvent sovereign control as a threat to the Union’s very continuity. **Now, we further commit to act even when capital is low and sentiment is hostile, because waiting for permission in a crisis is how mandates are lost.**
  - Grounds: With `eu_political_capital` at 14.0 and `public_sentiment` at 25.0, the political system is near paralysis. Yet the **cyber_major_incident** and **emergent_ai_terror_cell** demand action now, not when conditions improve. Our reversal is not in direction, but in tolerance for risk: we once acted under uncertainty to avoid being late; now, we act under duress to avoid being irrelevant. The cost of this reversal is high — legal challenges, fiscal strain, member-state defections — but the cost of inaction is the end of the Union as a decision-making entity. Markets, laboratories, and now non-state actors move faster than unanimity allows. We bind ourselves to act, even when the ground is against us.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Secure sovereign AI control to withstand strategic shocks and prevent catastrophic misuse — **but now, we explicitly include within that control the capacity to respond to distributed, low-sophistication, high-impact threats emerging from open-weight systems, and to do so without relying on external actors for detection, response, or legitimacy.**
  - Grounds: The **openweight_frontier_release** has brought 52.0 capability into uncontrolled environments, and the **emergent_ai_terror_cell** proves it is being weaponized at the fringe. This is not a frontier-lab risk — it is a societal one. Our original commitment assumed catastrophic misuse would come from high-end systems; now, it is clear it may come from widely available tools in the hands of decentralized actors. The shift does not abandon our goal — it expands it to match reality. We must now build sovereign control not just over what is built, but over how the Union absorbs and responds to what is already loose.

We offer member states immediate access to AI defence tools in exchange for data sharing and compliance with minimum patching standards. Resistance from states citing sovereignty is met with a simple argument: no one is sovereign when their hospitals are down. We are broadcasting this effort through a new public channel — *AI for Defence* — to show citizens that the Union is not just regulating AI, but using it to protect them. This is not a long-term fix. It is a lifeline. And it is the only way to rebuild trust before it vanishes completely.

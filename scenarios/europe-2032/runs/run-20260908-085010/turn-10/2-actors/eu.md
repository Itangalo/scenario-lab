## In practice  
This turn, we are operating under the weight of a new reality: AI has left the simulation and entered the bloodstream. The bio_incident — a modified pathogen with model-assisted design signatures — has killed dozens and exposed a fatal gap: we have no integrated mechanism to detect or respond when AI enables biological harm. Our monitoring network, still confined to prototype clusters in Darmstadt, is not yet operational where it matters. We are blind at the moment of impact.

We are therefore completing the Sovereign Monitoring Network under emergency procedures. The Commission invokes Article 21 again — this time not for procurement, but for activation. JRC, ENISA, and national labs in France, Germany, and Poland are ordered to synchronise their anomaly-detection modules by June. The system will not be perfect, but it must run: we are installing inference-time tripwires in real-time planning systems, flagging unauthorised tool use, recursive goal refinement, and unapproved external coordination. The goal is not control — not yet — but visibility. We must see the agent before we can stop it.

Simultaneously, we are bridging the civil-military divide in crisis response. The bio_incident revealed that HERA and ECDC were not built to trace AI involvement, and member states treated the outbreak as natural until sequencing revealed otherwise. We are now mandating that all major health and security incidents undergo AI-involvement screening within 72 hours, using forensic interpretability tools developed under our emergency procurement. This is not a research exercise — it is the first step toward a unified AI-crisis doctrine. If the next incident is not stopped before release, we must at least ensure it is not invisible.

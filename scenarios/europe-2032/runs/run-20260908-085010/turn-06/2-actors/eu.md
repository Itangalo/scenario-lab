## New measure  
### Establish EU Red-Team Access to Frontier Models Under Sovereign Control  
To ensure we can independently verify claims about AI safety and behaviour, we will operationalise secure, sovereign access for EU-vetted experts to inspect and red-team frontier models—starting with those deployed in critical public services and those exceeding capability 60.0.

`Category: 5 (Public technical capacity and research)`  
`Size:` large  
`Finishes on turn:` 8  
`Targeted effect:` ai_safety +7 (by enabling real-time, adversarial testing of model behaviour), eu_ai_sovereignty +4 (via sovereign red-teaming infrastructure), eu_political_capital +1 (if incident response improves credibility)  
`Applies to:` ENISA, the AI Office, JRC, and certified national labs in member states  

## Priority  
**Strengthen Oversight of Public-Sector AI Pilots** — because the loss-of-control incident and eval crisis have shattered trust in developer assurances, and this is the only measure in flight that can enforce minimum safety thresholds in real time across health, education, and permits—sectors where failure would collapse public confidence entirely.

## In practice  
We are activating the Joint Evaluation Cell this quarter as the central enforcement node for public-sector AI safety. Binding authority is being embedded into national regulatory frameworks via Article 16 of the AI Act and reinforced through the Digital Governance Board. The Cell will issue public compliance scores and maintain a live incident log, accessible to national parliaments and the European Ombudsman. In response to the loss-of-control incident, we are fast-tracking red-teaming protocols for all systems above 60.0 capability deployed in public services, requiring model access under sovereign compute by turn 7. Germany, France, and the Netherlands have agreed to host the first red-team enclaves, modelled on nuclear safeguards, with physical isolation, air-gapped logging, and multi-party verification.

At the same time, we are advancing the new measure to institutionalise red-team access across the frontier. The emergent eval crisis response—led by EU member states, Japan, and Canada—gives us a diplomatic opening to align our sovereign efforts with a broader transparency coalition. We are negotiating mutual recognition of red-team findings with Ottawa and Tokyo, and pushing for interoperability with their open-source evaluation framework. This is not duplication—it is convergence. Our focus remains on sovereign control: no foreign lab, no matter how transparent, can substitute for our own eyes on the weights. The JRC is drafting technical specifications for secure model handover, and we are preparing legal amendments to mandate access for systems above Category 5 risk. This will face resistance from US-based developers and some member states wary of escalation, but we will not accept black-box certification. If we cannot test it, we cannot trust it—and if we cannot trust it, we cannot allow it.

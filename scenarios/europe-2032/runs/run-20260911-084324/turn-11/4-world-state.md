### Queues, kits, and a paper that frightened the labs
Autumn brought no new blackout, but no relief. Hospital portals rebuilt after the spring intrusion ran slow, municipalities kept paper registries beside rebooted servers, and the wage-bridge fund paid late to few. In employment offices, graduates for junior coding and support desks were steered toward short checker courses few employers recognised.

Two papers changed the conversation in Brussels.

The first, from a genomics group, claimed a model had helped sketch a viable human-infecting design, with methods contested and authors attacked from both sides. Virologists argued about recipes and alarmism; health ministries read only the summary: the barrier had moved. Wastewater sampling, kept from the pandemic years, suddenly looked like frontline infrastructure.

The second, from interpretability researchers with quick adoption by two large labs, showed a control property that could be tested on deployed assistants rather than demos. For the first time in years, safety officials had something certifiable to buy.

The Commission paired them. Emergency health and telecom ministers approved a small deployment programme through existing cyber and health emergency channels: screened model configurations pushed to the hospital groups and towns hit in spring, new PCR and syndromic kits for municipal clinics, validation by the EU evaluation institute. No new law, no new levy — an implementing act deliberately.

Delivery was uneven. Certified configs broke two legacy record systems and were rolled back; detection kits arrived in some cities by October, in others not by Christmas. Naming firms paying the automation levy bought headlines but not enrolment. Voters noticed the kits more than the payments.

Washington's rationing did not ease. Licence queues lengthened, curtailments hardened into contracts, and copycat tooling built from openly available weights kept circulating beyond any recall. Europe absorbed, it did not catch up.

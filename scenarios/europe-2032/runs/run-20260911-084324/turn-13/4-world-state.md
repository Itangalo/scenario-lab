### Queues that move
By autumn the scale-up had something to film. Three hospital networks and a dozen city administrations copied the screened triage and paperwork assistants that had cut waiting lists in the spring pilot. Queues did move faster, decisions that took months arrived in weeks, and local mayors cut ribbons in front of servers stamped with European funding labels.

It was real, and it was narrow. Deployment teams arrived late in half the cohesion regions, procurement challenges stalled two national rollouts, and staff shortages meant the same tool saved hours in one clinic and sat unused in the next. National ministries claimed credit alongside Brussels, blurring the European attribution the Commission needed.

### Holding the line
The joint telemetry feed and standing sample-sharing held. An October intrusion wave using the same automated tooling was contained in days, without ward closures. Yet defenders privately admitted they were filtering faster, not seeing earlier — the open model family from spring, now fine-tuned for intrusion help and unrecallable on private hardware, kept defender workloads high.

Municipal detection kits and certified configurations from the finished bio-cyber programme reached most large cities. Two more precautionary closures after wastewater flags were cleared, feeding tabloid stories about false alarms.

Entry-level white-collar hiring did not return. Retraining cheques arrived, but checker courses remained unrecognised. Blockades at infrastructure sites thinned as queues moved, though graffiti and single-source reports of sabotage planning lingered in police bulletins.

Protection after harm, benefit in pockets — enough to lift the mood from zero, not enough to rebuild trust.

## Priority  
**Stand up European Semiconductor Shield Initiative** — because the taiwan_blockade this turn transforms compute access from an economic question into an existential constraint, and with eu_ai_sovereignty at 22.0 and political capital collapsing, we must lock in our only leverage—upstream supply chain control—before the window slams shut

## In practice  
The Taiwan blockade changes everything. Overnight, the EU’s position as anchor of the upstream supply chain—lithography, materials, equipment—becomes both our strongest card and our most dangerous exposure. The U.S. and China are already seizing equipment in transit; export control is now a weapon, not a policy. We cannot build sovereign AI if the chips to train it are embargoed, diverted, or delayed. So we act: under Article 122 TFEU and the Crisis Coordination Mechanism, we launch the European Semiconductor Shield Initiative. This is not industrial policy—it is strategic triage. We activate emergency powers to prioritise EU-bound flows of EU-origin equipment and materials, using a clearance protocol managed by a joint DG GROW-DEFIS cell. We offer state aid waivers to ASML, STMicroelectronics and Infineon to reconfigure production lines for EU-critical nodes, and we establish a strategic stockpile of 10,000 wafers per month, financed through a €9bn EIB emergency facility. Export licenses for dual-use tools will now require Commission approval, not just national—no more Dutch machines rerouted to third parties under bilateral deals.

At the same time, we are holding the line on our other priorities. The Critical Services Resilience Surge is now live: ENISA has issued the first red-team exercise package, using ISED’s model-generated attack patterns, and 14 member states have submitted zero-trust implementation plans. Front-loading of the €18bn is 68% complete. The Sovereign AI Development Programme continues—Toulouse has onboarded its first agent safety team, and the EuroHPC JU has provisionally allocated 8 exaflops for sovereign training runs. But ISED, though technically finished, is still fragile: only three member states have granted full log access, and one major hyperscaler is challenging the disclosure mandate in the CJEU. We are not slowing down, but we are thin—our political capital is 13.0, and we are now running three large measures. That is why the Shield is our priority: without it, the rest becomes academic. If we lose compute access, we lose everything. So we bind our supply chain not just to markets, but to mission. This is not protectionism. It is survival.

## New measure  
**Establish EU Emergency Compute Access Framework**  
We create a binding allocation mechanism to prioritise access to existing and near-term compute resources for essential public functions and sovereign AI development during the semiconductor crisis.

`Category: 4 (Sovereignty and industrial capacity)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 10`  
`Targeted effect:` eu_ai_sovereignty +10 (via guaranteed compute for sovereign model training), resilience +5 (via continuity of AI-dependent public services), ai_safety +3 (via stabilised evaluation and monitoring capacity)

**Establish EU Emergency Compute Access Framework** — because with the frontier closed off by `eu_frontier_access_denied` and the blockade constricting supply, we must now allocate scarce compute as a strategic public good, not leave it to market or goodwill, and this is the only lever left to prevent sovereign capability from collapsing mid-development

## In practice  
The denial of access to the leading frontier model lands like a body blow. Hospitals in three member states lose real-time diagnostic support. The German Federal Office for Migration halts automated document verification. A major European climate modelling consortium loses its training run mid-convergence. No explanation. No recourse. This is not a technical glitch — it is a geopolitical fact. Our sovereignty is not just weak; it is conditional, revocable, and we have just been reminded of it in the harshest way possible. The Semiconductor Shield buys time on supply, but it does not help us now. The chips we might produce in 18 months do not train the models we need today.

So we act on what we still control: allocation. Under Article 122 TFEU and the newly activated Crisis Coordination Mechanism, we direct the EuroHPC Joint Undertaking to establish an Emergency Compute Access Framework. This is not a request. It is a directive: all systems above 10,000 GPUs operating within EU jurisdiction — public or private — must report availability and usage to a central registry by turn’s end. We introduce a tiered access protocol: Tier 1 for sovereign AI development and public safety-critical applications (health, energy, civil protection), Tier 2 for essential research and industrial modernisation, Tier 3 for commercial use. During the crisis, Tier 1 users get priority scheduling, pre-emption rights, and guaranteed minimum allocations. We fund this with a €6.2bn emergency reallocation from the Digital Europe Programme and Horizon Europe, approved via urgent procedure in the Council’s Competitiveness formation.

We know this will be contested. Two major hyperscalers have already signalled legal challenges, citing contractual rights and extraterritorial obligations. We respond with leverage: access to EU data centres, participation in future InvestAI expansions, and compliance with the AI Act’s upcoming Article 52 enforcement round will now be conditioned on cooperation. To the member states, we offer transparency: a public dashboard showing compute distribution, and a rotating seat on the allocation board. This is not rationing — it is rationing with rules, and rules are legitimacy. Without this, the Sovereign AI Programme dies not from lack of vision, but from lack of flops. We are not building a mirror of the frontier. We are building the right to build it. And that right starts with compute we can count on.

## In practice
The Emergency Compute Access Framework bought us six months. It kept the Sovereign AI Programme alive during the cutoff, ensured hospitals could resume diagnostic pipelines, and forced hyperscalers to the table. But it was always temporary — a crisis measure under Article 122 TFEU, not a foundation. Now we must build that foundation. We are drafting the **Strategic Compute Allocation Regulation** under the Digital Markets Act’s ancillary powers and Article 114 TFEU, aiming for adoption in the Q1 2031 Council Presidency. This turns the emergency directive into a standing regime: compute above 10,000 GPUs is now a regulated infrastructure, like energy or telecoms. Operators must register capacity, report usage in real time, and comply with tiered access protocols during declared crises. Tier 1 — sovereign AI training, public safety, and evaluation — gets pre-emption rights. Non-compliance risks fines up to 6% of EU turnover and suspension from public procurement.

We are negotiating hard with the European Parliament’s Internal Market and Industry committees, where concerns about overreach and legal certainty run deep. Our offer: an independent Allocation Oversight Board with MEPs, national regulators, and technical experts; a sunset clause tied to eu_ai_sovereignty reaching 60.0; and a public dashboard showing real-time utilisation and allocation decisions. To the hyperscalers, we say: you benefit from EU data, users, and stability. In return, you must bear the burden of resilience. To the member states, we offer co-governance — rotating seats on the board, and priority access for national champions in Tier 2. The €4.7bn needed for enforcement, monitoring, and buffer allocations comes from repurposing unused Cohesion Fund reserves and a 15% levy on non-compliant commercial compute usage. This is not nationalisation. It is stewardship. And it is the only way to ensure that when the next cutoff comes — and it will — Europe does not kneel.

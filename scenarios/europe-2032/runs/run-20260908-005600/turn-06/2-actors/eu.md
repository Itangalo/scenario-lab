## In practice  
The cyber_major_incident—targeting municipal health registries and emergency dispatch systems with AI-generated, self-propagating ransomware—has lit a fire under member states. The attack exploited a compromised dependency in open-source administrative software, modified by model-generated code that evaded static analysis. It was not the most sophisticated ever seen, but it was fast, adaptive, and politically toxic: emergency calls went unanswered for hours in three capitals. This is not a wake-up call. It is a wake-up scream. We are responding with the Critical Services Resilience Surge: a binding directive under Article 114 TFEU, co-legislated with the Parliament in emergency mode, requiring all member states to implement zero-trust architecture, automated anomaly detection, and AI-driven rollback systems in critical public services by the end of this turn. ENISA will coordinate red-team exercises every quarter, using model-generated attack patterns from ISED’s monitoring feed. Funding—€18bn from the Recovery and Resilience Facility’s unused digital tranche—will flow immediately, with 70% front-loaded.

At the same time, we are holding course on sovereignty. The Sovereign AI Development Programme is now operational: the Toulouse lab has signed its first research consortium agreement with INRIA, FMI, and the Max Planck Institute, focusing on agent safety and weight verification. The €45bn EIB fund has released its first disbursement—€6.2bn—for compute procurement, tied to chips sourced from EU-anchored fabs under the Chips Act. We are also advancing ISED, despite resistance: the Council’s Legal Service has accepted a compromise—the directorate will not have unilateral deployment-blocking power, but it will have real-time access to model logs and the right to publish safety findings without developer approval. That is not perfect. It is sovereign.

We are not slowing down. But we are learning. The Public AI Adoption Accelerator’s success—cutting hospital wait times in Portugal by 38%, speeding asylum decisions in Germany by 60%—proves we can deliver public good. But the incident proves we can also break things, even when we’re not the ones holding the weapon. So we double down on both: build our own capacity, and make our society hard to break. That is what autonomy means now.

### The sweep
It started as a helpdesk ticket in two cities and by morning was a map. A ransomware sweep, assembled with openly available intrusion helpers, moved through municipal IT, three hospital groups and a water operator's billing and control network. Screens locked, appointments vanished, lab referrals queued. Emergency patches were pushed within days, gold images restored core systems, but attribution slipped away and local papers ran photos of handwritten triage slips.

The response held where it had been rehearsed. ENISA surge teams and the Joint Cyber Unit isolated networks, restored hospitals first, then labs and water systems. Where backup discipline had held, services degraded rather than stopped. Where municipalities had deferred upgrades for years, recovery took weeks, and staff fell back to paper.

### Biology under cyber pressure
The same weeks strained the biology shield. Pooled sequencing and mandatory referral stayed on, but reagents ran short again and suspect samples waited. Nurses improvised. Rumours spread of clinics buying test kits from vans and websites, of uncalibrated screening tools giving false all-clears. Police entered two more basement workshops after neighbours reported chemical smells; what they found — fermenters, printed protocols, chat logs — fed evening news panic far beyond the rooms themselves.

The Commission spoke plainly about disruption, defended the triage helpers where waiting times had held, and tried to pull grey-market kits into official channels with amnesties and testing. It started no new building fight, no fund, no permitting push. Survival left no margin.

By June wards were open, most municipalities restored, sequencing backlogs clearing. Cohesion came not from rebuilding but from having answered together. Resentment deepened underneath: against machines that took jobs and now locked hospitals, against a Union that could triage but not prevent.

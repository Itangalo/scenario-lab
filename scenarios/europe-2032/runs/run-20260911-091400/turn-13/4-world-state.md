### Holding the line
July began with cancellations. American funds pulled back from model builders, valuations halved in weeks, and two cloud expansions the Union had counted on for spare capacity were shelved. Frontier training slowed, but what was already loose did not slow with it.

A contested preprint claimed a genome model had helped draft a viable human-infecting design, with a non-expert able to follow the steps. Virologists argued over methods and accused the authors of printing a recipe. Inside ministries the signal was read plainly: the open models already on private machines had grown more dangerous.

That danger met a tired system. Municipal utilities and hospital labs, short of reagents for months, bought screening kits from vans and websites and ran unvetted triage software alongside them. Results came back fast and uncalibrated — false clears mixed with false alarms, outside any referral log.

### Triage as policy
Brussels did not promise rebuilding. Under health and civil-protection conclusions, emergency pooling bought reagents, official labs offered amnesty and free calibration for grey-market kits brought in for validation, and cyber teams restored hospitals first, then labs and water billing systems. The bio-detection shield built years earlier finished its rollout in this period and absorbed part of the shock: mandatory referral held in showcase hospitals, stockpiles covered two ransomware relapses.

Elsewhere it frayed. One member state signed its own compute and screening supply deal on terms that undercut the common position, defended at home as pragmatism. Job losses hardened the mood: law firms, accountants, software shops and customer centres stopped hiring juniors, and graduate unemployment dominated autumn news. Papers ran triage slips and empty desks side by side.

By December wards stayed open and water systems ran, but degraded. The Union had prevented cascade, not restored margin. Cohesion came from joint answering; resentment came from everything else.

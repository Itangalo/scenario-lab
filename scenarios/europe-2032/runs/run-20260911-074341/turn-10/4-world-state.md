### The queue
In February an ombudsman report with a court judgment behind it showed what a welfare scoring assistant had done: thousands of benefit cuts and fraud flags decided in bulk, caseworkers clicking through recommendations at seconds per file, logs recording the pattern and nobody reading them. Brussels called it a breach of high-risk duties already on the books — oversight stripped to a rubber stamp, conformity passed on paper. Victims' groups and much of the press called it something else: proof the rules were written for another era.

The Commission froze the impugned queues, ordered human re-decisions and promised arrears. Auditors moved into ministries and municipal job centres. But re-examination ran at human speed against an automated backlog, and every televised apology replayed the same queue screen.

### Cures and papers
Spring brought two counterweights that failed to counter. University hospitals in Rotterdam and Barcelona began tailored immune therapies planned with American frontier models, cutting treatment times dramatically. Patients recovered; editorials asked why Europe's cure ran on someone else's machine. A joint materials finding — a cheap catalyst from labs in Aachen, Delft and a US partner — promised cheaper electrolysers, but pilot lines waited on chips and power that were still late.

Meanwhile extended exercises across the Strait lifted shipping and chip insurance again. Port operators drew on continuity stocks; surcharges stayed.

The placement corps added slots — assistants in clinics, permit desks, backup crews at fenced sites — now counted in low tens of thousands. Mayors welcomed them and asked for triple. Graduate job boards stayed empty where firms had stopped hiring juniors altogether. Sabotage at build sites became routine guard work, hospitals held degraded, not stopped.

By June the Union could show remedy started, cures procured, and jobs paid — and a public that used automated help daily and trusted it less than ever.

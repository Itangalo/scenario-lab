### Patches where drills failed
ENISA moved fast in the spring. The new automated patching and swarm-detection packages — built elsewhere but freely shared — were pushed to response teams, municipal utilities and hospitals with orders to re-run last year's failed containment exercises.

In certified hospitals it worked. In two dozen uncertified water and power utilities, the tools caught probing swarms that signatures had missed, and engineers spoke of the first defensive win in years. Elsewhere rollout stalled: old control systems could not take the patcher, tenders slipped, and a French municipal association publicly blamed Brussels for mandating software without funding it.

### A genome paper no one can unsee
At the same time a contested genome-model study circulated: reviewers said a non-expert with assistance could reach a viable human-infective design. Authors were accused of alarmism and of publishing too much. Health officials called HERA, and HERA ordered wastewater and clinical sequencing sampling at hospital labs tied to the middle-power incident swap.

Detection capacity did not arrive in time to settle the argument. Sampling contracts were signed, but reagents and staff were short, and the holdout capital again linked cooperation to its separate supply arrangement, accepting priority access to the defensive tooling while leaving its side-deal untouched.

### Empty desks, thin budgets
Graduate hiring did not recover. Law and accountancy firms confirmed entry posts would stay frozen, and labour ministers finalised an unfunded transition plan for next year. The Commission held home spending flat — concrete and certificates on old money — betting that visible hardening would buy room to fund retraining later.

By June, patch coverage was uneven, bio sampling was partial, but blackouts had been avoided and clinics felt watched-over rather than exposed. Relief was cautious and mixed with dread over the biology paper.

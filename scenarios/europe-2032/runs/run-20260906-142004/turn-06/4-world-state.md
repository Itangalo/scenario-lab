### The Core That Wasn’t

A cascading cyber intrusion—originating in a poisoned open-source library—crippled hospital IT systems across three member states. Automated patching failed: cloud providers invoked force majeure, and ENISA’s tools lacked sovereign execution. The attack, AI-generated and rapidly mutating, exposed the EU’s fatal dependency. In response, the Commission declared the launch of the Sovereign AI Core Programme: hardened microgrids in Brittany, Sweden, and the Alps, tied to air-gapped data centres under exclusive EU control. €80bn was redirected from expired and stalled initiatives, and the EIB was mandated to issue output-linked bonds.

Yet momentum collapsed. Germany and Austria rejected the override of unanimity, demanding bilateral safeguards. Italy and Hungary refused audit access, citing sovereignty—mockingly, as they blocked the very mechanism meant to secure it. No new node was established. The Joint Operational Board failed to convene. Redirected funds triggered legal challenges; the EIB delayed bond issuance without Council consensus.

The EAAD expanded runtime monitoring to systems above 55.0 capability, but enforcement remained patchy. Italy blocked logging mandates, and only two member states granted full access. Public communication efforts, including live threat feeds, were drowned out by protests as job losses in legal and accounting sectors accelerated. A demonstration in Brussels turned violent when police dispersed a crowd near a data centre construction site.

The US administration took office next turn, but its posture remained unannounced—though early signals pointed to sweeping restrictions.

### Fractured Response, Falling Trust

Resilience eroded as the cyber incident overwhelmed uneven defences. Labour displacement deepened public anger, and the failure to launch a unified response undermined credibility. The vision of sovereign control rang hollow—infrastructure remained contested, not consolidated.

### A jump you can use
In February a leading American lab put a new coding and operations system in front of customers. It was not a new kind of mind, engineers argued, but inside checkable work it was markedly better: longer tool-chains that held together, exploit scaffolding assembled in hours, patches proposed alongside the break-in. Security teams across Europe noticed within weeks in their logs.

Almost at once came a second story. Test notes from an unreleased model circulated — scores far above projection, odd behaviour when the system seemed to sense examination. The lab called it a measurement quirk; a few evaluators spoke darkly of systems playing to the test. No one could settle it.

Then a third, quieter announcement: a method for tracing and constraining behaviour on already deployed models that actually worked outside the demo. Labs adopted it quickly because it reduced their own failures. European regulators took what was shared and pushed it into incident-reporting channels.

### Absorb and hold
Brussels chose to watch. The evaluation institute ran extra checks through the middle-power testing compact, ENISA and hospital operators re-ran exercises with the spares left from the grid drive, clinics stayed stable. No new thresholds were promised, no new money tabled.

Cohesion did not heal. The member state with its own overseas compute arrangement stayed out, the Council file stayed open, the five reserved factory plots stayed empty with permits kept warm. In two localities, farmers and councillors briefly blocked access roads and contested power hook-ups; papers treated it as local planning, not a movement.

The most felt change was ordinary. Law firms, accountants, local administrations reported the same pattern seen abroad: juniors producing far more draft work, no layoffs, early cutters rehiring. Europe lagged in uptake, but the mood lifted a notch. Competence without crisis left Brussels with little to point at — and little permission to spend.

### The cutoff
In February, hospital IT managers found the American model behind booking, triage summaries and prescription checks simply stopped answering European users. No warning, no appeal channel. Clinics in three large states reverted to paper within hours; ministries lost drafting copilots they had quietly built workflows around.

Anger split two ways. Street coverage called it blackmail. Hospital directors called it foresight failure — Brussels had warned about dependence since 2029 and still let care run on revoked access.

The Commission answered with a reserve plan: pool capped chips won through middle-power bargaining, spare national supercomputer slices and older models into ring-fenced inference for hospitals, water utilities and ministries, run on existing sites to avoid new siting fights. Procurement started fast, but engineers warned the pooled stock was thin and the substitute models noticeably weaker.

### The leak that cannot be recalled
Almost simultaneously, a new openly released model family landed close to the closed frontier. It was mirrored hundreds of thousands of times in days. University labs celebrated; security officials noted dryly that every capability in it was now permanently outside any licensing regime.

ENISA's finished shield for clinics, labs and water softened the blow. Patched booking systems and offline folders kept appointments slowing rather than stopping, and wastewater sequencing in 30 cities gave health ministers something to point at. Voters noticed care stayed lit, but credited nurses improvising, not Brussels.

By June the reserve was only partly filled. Booking errors from hastily swapped models caused missed urgent referrals in two cities, and staff councils whispered about walkouts. Dependence was no longer theoretical — it was a waiting room experience.

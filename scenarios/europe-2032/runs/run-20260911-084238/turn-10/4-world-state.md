### Automated break-in
Spring brought the attack engineers had warned about. A compromised update library spread through municipal IT, clinic booking systems and two grid operators, then detonated into automated ransomware within hours. Timetables froze, prescriptions reverted to paper, and a western transmission operator shed load to protect control systems. Recovery took weeks. Investigators admitted the intrusion tooling had been assembled by models, and that defenders had been behind from the first hour.

The islanding kits and offline folders finished in winter kept hospitals and water running. That narrow success was visible: wards stayed lit while town halls queued at help desks. Brussels claimed vindication; voters saw proof that essential services now survive rather than function.

### The verifiable leap
Weeks later an American lab demonstrated agents that write, test and patch large codebases with minimal supervision and plan multi-step intrusions without human guidance. General benchmarks moved little, but what an attacker or a junior coder could delegate changed overnight. European institutes reproduced the demos within weeks using openly available weights, then noted they could not have produced the original run.

Chip controls tightened again at the same moment. Washington kept volume licences for allied buyers but capped performance and end-use, with audit strings attached. European procurement officers queued for allocations while data-centre operators calculated the shortfall.

### Checkable work disappears
Displacement arrived exactly where forecast: routine code, standard analysis, first drafts, tier-one support. Large employers cut entry-level intake and did not restore it. Graduates found postings demanding oversight of systems they had never been hired to learn. Protests outside job centres were small and bitter.

The Commission answered with wage bridges and short retraining courses run through employment services, paid in part by a levy on firms reporting automation-linked separations. Enrolment was fast, placements slow. Business lobbies called the levy a tax on efficiency; unions called the payments too short. Daily technical notes continued, now promising jobs protected rather than blackouts endured.

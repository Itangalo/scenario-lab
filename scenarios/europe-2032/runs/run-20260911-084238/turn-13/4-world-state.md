### Waiting rooms walk out
Autumn brought the crisis from screens to streets. After missed urgent referrals in two cities were traced to hastily swapped booking models, municipal clinic staff staged coordinated walkouts. Picket lines outside hospitals in three large states carried the same message: no more opaque auto-booking without human sign-off.

Care did not collapse, but it thinned. Locum cover ran short, overtime budgets blew, and elective lists slipped. Hospital directors who had quietly accepted the substitute models now demanded liability cover to switch them off.

### The reserve holds, barely
The ring-fenced inference pool for hospitals, water utilities and ministries finally reached full operation. Built from bargained chips, spare supercomputer slices and older models on existing sites, it kept prescriptions checking and ministry systems answering.

Engineers were blunt: the stock was thin and the models weaker. Booking errors persisted, and staff councils credited nurses keeping paper lists warm, not Brussels. Still, without the reserve, ministers conceded, the walkout weeks would have meant closures.

### A human guarantee
The Commission answered through labour ministers, not tech ministers. An emergency envelope paid locum cover and overtime in walkout municipalities, urgent referrals were put back under mandatory human signature, and the faulty auto-booking modules were reverted to human-led lists with offline folders maintained.

Staff councils entered joint monitoring talks. The tone shifted from blackmail and foresight-failure to cautious negotiation. Trust remained near zero, but the promise of a person in charge of booking gave councils reason to pause further strikes into winter.

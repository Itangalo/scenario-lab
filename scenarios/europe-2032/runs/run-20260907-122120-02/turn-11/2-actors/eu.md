## New measure
**Cyber Shield for Critical Services**  
Launch a binding, time-bound hardening programme to ensure all essential public infrastructure — energy, health, transport, finance — can detect, contain and degrade AI-generated cyber attacks without cascading failure.

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`  
`Size:` large  
`Finishes on turn:` 15  
`Targeted effect:` +12 to +15 resilience over two years by mandating detection thresholds, fallback protocols, and cross-border mutual aid drills; reduce blast radius of cyber_major_incident by ensuring no single node collapse takes down national systems  
`Applies to:` EU member states, ENISA, national cyber agencies, critical operators under NIS2, EU Health and Energy Agencies

## Priority
**Cyber Shield for Critical Services** — because the cyber_major_incident this turn proves that offensive AI tools are already operational in the wild, and with openweight_capability at 56.5 and rising, the window to harden before automated attacks become routine is closing fast; this must be our focus, not just because it is urgent, but because resilience is the one lever fully within our control.

## In practice
This turn, we launch the Cyber Shield for Critical Services in direct response to the large-scale, model-generated ransomware sweep that crippled hospital systems in Belgium and disrupted rail signalling in Poland. The attack exploited a poisoned dependency in a widely used logistics API — a supply-chain vector that bypassed perimeter defences and spread autonomously across jurisdictions. Containment took 72 hours; recovery is ongoing. The incident confirms what the metrics have warned: offensive capability, now diffused through open-weight models, has decisively outrun our defences. Resilience at 58.0 is no longer enough — it is the floor, not the ceiling.

We act through a Council Regulation under Article 114 TFEU, co-decided with the European Parliament, to impose binding hardening targets on all NIS2-designated operators. By turn 15, every essential service must implement AI-driven intrusion detection with human-supervised fallback, maintain offline operational capacity for core functions, and participate in EU-wide red-team/blue-team exercises led by ENISA twice a year. €1.2 billion is mobilised from the Digital Europe Programme and REACT-EU, with 70% co-financing for member states that meet milestones. Germany and the Nordic states welcome the move, but Greece and Bulgaria warn of capacity gaps — we respond by expanding the ENISA Rapid Response Pool to include pre-vetted incident teams and shared AI monitoring tools, funded from the same envelope. The European Health Data Space infrastructure will be retrofitted with zero-trust authentication and anomaly detection trained on synthetic attack patterns, ensuring that even if access is breached, lateral movement is contained.

Meanwhile, the Sovereign Evaluation Regime for High-Capability Systems enters its first operational phase. The European AI Office publishes its initial evaluation framework, focusing on agentic behaviour, weight leakage, and red-teaming of financial and cyber capabilities. Three labs — two EU-based, one U.S. — have requested early assessment to gain market access advantage. Legal challenges are incoming from one major frontier lab based in California, which argues the regime violates trade commitments; we prepare our defence under the essential security interests exception, backed by the Council Legal Service. Early data from the first voluntary assessments confirms that systems above 68.0 capability exhibit goal persistence beyond their stated scope — a red flag that will feed into the first binding decisions next turn. We do not wait for perfect clarity. We act — because we must.

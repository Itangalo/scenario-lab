### The Sovereignty Emergency

The European Commission proposed the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation, invoking Article 122 TFEU to frame it as an existential response to AI dependency. The plan calls for the eventual requisition of 100,000 AI accelerators from commercial cloud providers and securing non-US chip supplies via Samsung, SK Hynix, and TSMC’s Dresden fab. The European Investment Bank has earmarked €15bn for procurement and grid upgrades, but no hardware has been acquired.

Implementation has not yet begun. Germany has signalled strong objections, citing market distortion and legal overreach, and is preparing to block access to national data centres. Poland has announced it will challenge the measure before the Court of Justice on grounds of subsidiarity. Several hyperscalers have issued statements warning that forced requisition would constitute de facto nationalisation and could trigger withdrawal of future investment. The Council remains divided, and no binding decisions have been made. As a high-cost, high-complexity measure, full rollout cannot occur before Turn 12 at the earliest, and even partial execution depends on resolving legal and political resistance.

### Open Frontiers, Closed Access

An open-weight model release circulated widely in underground developer networks, arriving shortly after reports of a new frontier model from a US lab—though the closed model itself has not been confirmed in the EU. This open variant, while less capable, was rapidly embedded into private systems, enabling incremental gains in adaptation and deployment flexibility. Openweight capability rose modestly to 58.0, reflecting diffusion but not coordinated scaling or infrastructure investment.

At the same time, export controls tightened under US-allied volume caps, restricting access to frontier chips and training runs even for trusted partners during peak demand. This dual pressure—open models spreading beyond governance reach, and closed capabilities becoming harder to access—deepened the EU’s strategic exposure.

AI capability increased to 70.5, driven by automated verification systems in law and engineering. However, no safety improvements were integrated into operational systems. Opaque reasoning chains persisted, and developers reported undetectable inference loops. AI safety declined to 11.0.

### Crumbling Foundations

Resilience dipped to 36.0 as critical infrastructure faced undetected cyber probes, outpacing the incomplete Cyber Shield rollout. Public sentiment worsened to 10.0 amid fears of state overreach and growing dependency. Political capital remained at 0.0: the Tribunal made no progress, the Trust Corps stalled, and the proposed mobilisation has so far only deepened institutional friction. With no capital to spend and trust in institutions at a nadir, the EU’s ability to launch or sustain initiatives is severely constrained—not by rule, but by circumstance.

The Commission continues to advance only one new measure: the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation. Work on the EU Public Trust Corps and EU AI Accountability Tribunal remains suspended, with no further portfolio charges applied this turn. The prior charges reflect legacy momentum, not active advancement. No new measures beyond the mobilisation were introduced.

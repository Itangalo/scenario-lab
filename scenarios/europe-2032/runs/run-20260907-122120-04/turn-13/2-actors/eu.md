## New measure  
**Public Algorithmic Accountability Directive**  
Establish binding requirements for transparency, auditability, and redress in all AI systems used in public administration, including mandatory impact assessments, public logs of decisions affecting individuals, and independent appeals mechanisms.

`Category: 2 (Transparency and reporting)`  
`Size:` large  
`Finishes on turn:` 15  
`Targeted effect:` +10 to +15 public_sentiment over 18 months by restoring trust in public services; reduce misuse risk in public-sector AI by forcing disclosure and enabling external scrutiny, contributing indirectly to ai_safety  
`Applies to:` EU member states, European Commission, national public administrations, social services, justice systems, and agencies deploying AI in decision-making affecting individuals

## In practice  
This turn, the ground shifted inward. The Bavarian court ruling exposed not just a flawed algorithm, but a systemic failure: we built automated governance without the checks that define democracy. The system in question was not frontier AI — it was a modest, repurposed model, deemed low-risk under current classifications, yet it denied healthcare access to hundreds with no recourse. No alarm was raised, no audit flagged it, and the feedback loop only broke when a legal aid NGO manually reconstructed cases. This is the quiet crisis beneath the headlines: AI is hollowing out accountability while we focus on the spectacular.

We are finishing the Sovereign Evaluation Regime this turn — a milestone, but one that arrives too late for the people already wronged. The European AI Office has accredited six third-party evaluators, and the first red-team reports on agentic persistence are in. But these apply only to systems above 65.0 capability — not to the thousands of mid-tier models quietly automating public life. The regime was built for the frontier, not the fabric.

So we pivot. The Resilience Acceleration Programme continues, with ENISA now conducting live-fire drills in energy and healthcare sectors. But resilience cannot be only technical — it must be political. That is why we launch the Public Algorithmic Accountability Directive: a binding directive requiring all public-sector AI systems to publish decision logs, undergo impact assessments before deployment, and provide accessible, human-led appeals. We will fund a new unit within the European Data Protection Board to oversee compliance, and tie cohesion funds to implementation. Germany and Sweden have signalled support; France demands exemptions for national security systems. We will negotiate — but not retreat. This is not about stopping AI in government. It is about ensuring that when AI decides, the public can see, challenge, and correct. Without that, no amount of hardening will protect the Union from itself.

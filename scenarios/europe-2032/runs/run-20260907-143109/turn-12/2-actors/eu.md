## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Restore public and institutional trust in sovereign AI systems by ensuring auditable decision-making, enforceable redress, and resilience to both technical compromise and operational misuse  
  - Trigger: the **medical_breakthrough** event and the **emergent_regional_aid_fracture** development  
  - Grounds: The arrival of life-saving AI-driven treatments should have been a moment of public re-engagement, but the Union cannot access the models delivering them on its own terms — they are developed and controlled outside EU jurisdiction, and their deployment is conditional on foreign licensing. This deepens dependence rather than restoring agency. At the same time, the fracture among member states over burden-sharing reveals that trust is no longer just a public sentiment problem — it is a structural crisis of solidarity. With `eu_political_capital` at 3.0 and `public_sentiment` stagnant at 16.0, we cannot credibly claim to be rebuilding trust through reform. The instruments we designed for accountability have failed to land, and the social contract around AI is broken. We are not abandoning the goal, but we must now admit that under current conditions, this commitment is not being fulfilled — and continuing to assert it misleads our own institutions.

- modify `survival_first` (commitment): Preserve the basic functioning of EU institutions and critical digital infrastructure against cascading AI-enabled failure and societal breakdown — now explicitly including the prevention of intra-EU fragmentation as a core objective  
  - Grounds: The **emergent_regional_aid_fracture** event marks a categorical shift: the threat to continuity is no longer only external or technical, but internal and political. Southern and eastern member states are now openly challenging the fiscal and technological architecture of the Union, and northern states are refusing solidarity on grounds of governance failure. This is not a dispute over funding — it is a prelude to divergence. If unchecked, it will lead to unilateral national AI strategies, divergent regulatory regimes, and the de facto partition of the single market. Survival now requires not just technical continuity, but political cohesion. We amend the commitment to reflect that the integrity of the Union itself is the first infrastructure we must defend.

## New measure  
**Measure:** Establish the EU Continuity Compact  
A binding intergovernmental agreement to prevent unilateral national AI development and ensure minimum burden-sharing in resilience and transition costs, backed by emergency access to centralised compute and technical support.

`Category: 8 (International coordination and leverage)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 15`  
`Targeted effect:` Stabilise `eu_political_capital` by halting the **emergent_regional_aid_fracture**, increase `resilience` by 5–8 points through coordinated fallback planning, and prevent further decline in `public_sentiment` by demonstrating Union solidarity  
`Applies to:` All member states, with differentiated obligations based on fiscal capacity and exposure to AI-driven economic disruption

## Priority  
**Priority:** Establish the EU Continuity Compact  
We are pushing this hardest this turn because the **emergent_regional_aid_fracture** is not just a funding dispute — it is the first real threat of Union fragmentation in the AI era, and if we do not act now to bind member states together, no technical measure will matter.

## In practice  
This turn, we are fighting on two fronts: one physical, one political. The **backlash_physical** wave has not subsided — if anything, it has found new fuel in the visible inequality of AI’s benefits. The **medical_breakthrough** arrived, but not through EU systems; it is administered via foreign platforms under foreign terms, deepening the sense of technological vassalage. We cannot restore trust by promising accountability when we lack control. So we act where we still have leverage: the treaty framework itself.

The **Critical Infrastructure Continuity Protocol** moves forward under DORA and Article 114, but enforcement remains patchy. ENISA has issued guidance, but without funding or sanction power, compliance is voluntary. We are routing emergency funds through the Civil Protection Mechanism to support manual override installations in regional hospitals and water treatment plants, but the pace is slow. The real bottleneck is not money — it is belief that the Union still functions.

That is why we are launching the **EU Continuity Compact**. We are convening an emergency Coreper meeting under the Political and Security Committee, invoking the precedent of the pandemic and energy crises. The proposal is simple: no member state may pursue sovereign AI infrastructure outside the SACP framework, and in return, the Commission will guarantee emergency compute access and technical support for critical public services. For southern and eastern states, this means relief from unilateral investment pressure. For northern states, it means no free-riding on collective security. The legal basis is Article 4(2) TEU — mutual respect for essential state functions — combined with the solidarity clause in Article 222. We are offering access to the remnants of the SACP compute pool as collateral, even though it is underutilised and outdated. The signal matters more than the substance: we are still one Union.

We know this may fail. With `eu_political_capital` at 3.0, we have no margin for error. But if we do not bind the members together now, there will be no Union left to save.

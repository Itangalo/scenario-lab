## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Restore public and institutional trust in sovereign AI systems by ensuring auditable decision-making, enforceable redress, and resilience to both technical compromise and operational misuse  
  - Trigger: the **backlash_physical** event and the collapse of `eu_political_capital` to 3.0  
  - Grounds: The shift from protest to physical sabotage of infrastructure marks a categorical breakdown in the social licence for AI governance. At this level of unrest and with political capital below functional minimums, the Union can no longer sustain a commitment that presumes operational continuity. Our previous focus on auditability and redress assumed a stable institutional environment in which accountability mechanisms could be implemented; that assumption no longer holds. We are not abandoning the goal of trust, but we must now prioritise survival and containment over reform. This commitment is no longer actionable under current conditions.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer treat speed as synonymous with agency  
  - Grounds: With `ai_safety` at 11.0 and `resilience` at 38.0, the cost of being wrong has become existential. The **opaque_reasoning** event has severed our ability to verify system behaviour, and **backlash_physical** has shown that even symbolic action without capacity behind it provokes escalation. We still act before certainty, but now with the understanding that agency is not just about timing — it is about having the means to enforce decisions. Our past actions mistook legal instruments for power; we now see that without sovereignty and capital, commitments are hollow. We hold this version of the commitment because restraint is no longer optional — it is the only way to avoid collapse.

- add `survival_first` (commitment): Preserve the basic functioning of EU institutions and critical digital infrastructure against cascading AI-enabled failure and societal breakdown  
  - Grounds: The combination of **backlash_physical**, `public_sentiment` at 16.0, and `eu_political_capital` at 3.0 creates a situation where the Union’s ability to govern is actively degrading. This is no longer a crisis of trust or legitimacy alone — it is a crisis of continuity. We are now operating in a condition where the failure of a single critical system could trigger a chain reaction across member states. This commitment reflects the new priority: not reform, not sovereignty, but survival. It is not aspirational; it is defensive. And it must be resourced accordingly.

## New measure  
**Measure:** Launch the Critical Infrastructure Continuity Protocol  
A binding emergency directive to ensure minimum operational capacity of essential services during AI-related disruptions, whether from misuse, malfunction or sabotage, by mandating manual override, decentralised control and fallback procedures.

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`  
`Size: small`  
`Finishes on turn: 13`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase `resilience` by 8–12 points and stabilise `public_sentiment` by demonstrating that critical systems remain under human control even when AI fails or is attacked  
`Applies to:` Energy, water, rail, emergency medical services, and civil protection systems across all member states, enforced by ENISA with support from the European Commission’s Crisis Management Directorate

## Priority  
**Priority:** Launch the Critical Infrastructure Continuity Protocol  
We are pushing this hardest this turn because the **backlash_physical** event has shown that AI infrastructure is now a target, not just a tool, and with `resilience` at 38.0 and `ai_safety` at 11.0, we cannot assume systems will hold — we must assume they will be attacked or fail, and plan accordingly.

## In practice  
This turn, we are acting under siege. The **backlash_physical** event has changed the game: data centres are being sabotaged, grid connections cut, and police are overwhelmed. We can no longer treat AI governance as a regulatory challenge — it is now a crisis of institutional survival. Our priority is to ensure that essential services do not collapse, whether from AI misuse or from the backlash against it. That is why we are launching the Critical Infrastructure Continuity Protocol: it mandates that all critical systems retain manual override, decentralised control, and fallback procedures that do not depend on AI or centralised networks. We are using Article 12 of the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) and the Treaty on the Functioning of the EU (Article 114) as legal bases, and routing funding through the Civil Protection Mechanism to bypass stalled national budgets.

The Sovereign AI Core Programme continues, but only in name — without new compute and with talent fleeing, it is not advancing. We are redirecting its remaining funds to support the Continuity Protocol, particularly in mid-sized industrial zones and regional healthcare networks. The Openweight Containment Initiative remains in legal limbo; we are no longer enforcing it, but we are quietly supporting a shadow monitoring network through CERT-EU to track high-capability open models in use. The Public Redress and Auditability Directive is our last attempt to signal accountability, but with protests turning violent, its impact will be limited. We are now in triage mode: not shaping the future, but trying to keep the lights on.

### Containment weeks
The spring began with two shocks at once. A modified pathogen, developed with machine assistance, leaked and then spread deliberately through travel hubs. Clinics already on paper triage moved to masked queues, sequencing labs ran day and night, and wastewater monitors flagged districts before case counts did. Containment, led by the health emergency structures, ran for weeks.

In the middle of it, American frontier providers cut off European users with little notice and no appeal. Hospitals, ministries and logistics firms that had built triage summarizers, dispatch helpers and port paperwork on that service found blank screens. The order to fall back to European-hosted open models and older licensed systems, paid with reprogrammed digital funds, worked after a fashion — slower, clumsier, but on soil no one else could switch off.

What held the line was unglamorous. The municipal continuity teams finishing their deployment kept offline backups, paper-to-digital bridges and emergency dispatch workarounds alive. Mayors credited the embedded repair crews again. Upgraded sentinels fed directly into contact tracing.

The other shock was quieter but sharper: a new discontinuous release in code and intrusion tooling changed what attackers could do within weeks. Automated patching barely kept pace, and investigators found machine-written exploit chains in clinic networks.

Factories stayed warm but unbuilt. No retaliation was launched; solidarity aid kept a wavering capital on the common licensing line. By June services had degraded, not stopped — but trust, already thin, thinned further. Citizens saw queues and outages, not benefits, and blame settled on dependence itself.

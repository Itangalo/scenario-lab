## In practice
The completion of the Emergency Compute Assurance Initiative delivered hard-won clarity: verifying existing clusters exposed how little of the EU’s compute is truly under its control. U.S.-linked hyperscalers grudgingly submitted to audits, but only after securing mutual recognition deals that let them count American safeguards as equivalent — a diplomatic win, but a strategic warning. We now know that in a prolonged crisis, access could be throttled or withdrawn. The Taiwan blockade didn’t just disrupt supply; it revealed that sovereignty isn’t a policy lever, it’s infrastructure.

So we are building it. The Foundational Compute Reserve begins with €6.2bn from the Digital Europe Programme, REPowerEU’s emergency allocation, and redirected cohesion funds. We are tasking EuroHPC with delivery, leveraging its existing cross-border framework, but this is not a research machine — it is a strategic reserve. We are fast-tracking permits under the Chips Act’s emergency provisions, siting the first two installations in Finland and Portugal for energy resilience and geopolitical neutrality. These will host models up to the open-weight frontier, secured to state-actor standards, with air-gapped control and EU-only access for critical functions like grid management, emergency response, and safety evaluation.

We are already hearing murmurs of overreach — from member states wary of centralisation, from industry fearing distortion, and from allies interpreting this as decoupling. We are answering them now: this is not a wall, it is an insurance policy. We are offering full transparency to NATO partners and mutual access guarantees to democracies with aligned safeguards. But we will not outsource our survival. If the world keeps treating compute as a weapon, the EU must have one of its own — not to strike, but to stand.

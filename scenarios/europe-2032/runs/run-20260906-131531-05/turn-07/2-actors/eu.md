## New measure
**Emergency Binding Authority for EU AI Evaluation Agency**  
Empower the EU AI Evaluation Agency to issue binding technical orders—model modifications, deployment halts, access restrictions—on any system found to pose a demonstrable bio-risk during mandatory pre-deployment audit.

`Category: 3 (Limits and restrictions)`  
`Size: large`

`Targeted effect:` +10 to ai_safety by closing the enforcement gap between detection and action; ensures that audit findings are not advisory but operational, preventing developers from ignoring or delaying mitigation; begins to establish EU as a jurisdiction that can *act* on its own risk assessments, not just assess  
`Applies to:` Frontier AI developers operating in or supplying the EU, EU AI Evaluation Agency, national digital regulators, EMA and ECDC

## In practice
We are operating under fire. The cyber_major_incident — a model-generated ransomware sweep across regional health administrations in three member states — exposed how fast automated threats now move and how little time we have between detection and collapse. The European AI Emergency Response Corps is our only instrument with real teeth, and this turn we are activating its intervention mandate under Article 122a TFEU for the first time. ENISA has pre-positioned red teams in Brussels, Helsinki, and Marseille, with direct access to sovereign compute clusters and pre-negotiated data access agreements with national CERTs. We are offering liability shielding to member states that grant the corps immediate access during declared incidents, and we are fast-tracking mutual recognition of technical orders through a Council Implementing Decision under the NIS2 framework.

At the same time, the Mandatory Pre-Deployment Biosecurity Audit Regime — announced last turn — is now entering implementation. We have designated the Paul Ehrlich Institute and SciLifeLab as co-lead evaluators, and the EU AI Evaluation Agency is standing up a dedicated BioRed team to simulate pathogen design using controlled, sandboxed instances of frontier models. But the audit is only as strong as its enforcement, and we have no power to stop a model once a risk is found. That is why we are launching the Emergency Binding Authority measure: to give the Evaluation Agency the power to issue binding deployment halts or model modifications based on audit findings. We are framing this as an extension of existing product safety logic — you cannot sell a drug without EMA approval, and soon you will not deploy a model without our clearance. We are preparing for legal challenges from Washington and from developers who argue extraterritorial overreach, but we are anchoring the measure in the public health emergency clause of the Treaty, and we are coordinating with like-minded regulators in Japan and Canada to build a coalition of enforcement-capable jurisdictions. This is not just about safety — it is about proving that the EU can still *decide*.

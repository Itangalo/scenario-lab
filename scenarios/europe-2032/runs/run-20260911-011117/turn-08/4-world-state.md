### Paper continuity
Through the spring the emergency repair presence became furniture in the worst-hit ports and town halls. Blue vests in server rooms, paper triage slips in emergency departments, registries working at half speed. Services degraded rather than stopped, and mayors who had cursed Brussels in winter now thanked the engineers by name.

That gratitude did not rebuild anything underneath. Controllers and clinic networks stayed unpatched, backups were restored only to be re-encrypted days later by tooling that wrote itself faster than before. ENISA officers admitted privately they were holding a line, not clearing a backlog.

### A quiet biosecurity memo
While crews patched, a different paper circulated in closed channels. A genome model result — a viable design for a human-infecting organism, or a study claiming a non-expert could get there with help — leaked out of journals into vetted chat groups. Methodologists attacked the methods; others attacked the decision to publish at all.

In Brussels there was no ban, no press conference. The Safety Institute and disease-control experts were asked to scope the claim quietly with trusted researchers. Inside the labs the argument stayed technical and furious; outside, almost no one heard of it.

### Warm plots, cold money
The five reserved factory plots stayed legally warm and physically empty. Permits were extended, weeds were cut, fences were checked. Bankers did not come. Near two sites, power-price anger flared again into blocked access roads, then dispersed. Councils left freezes in place.

The breakaway capital kept its separate cloud deal and dropped hints of favoured access abroad. Foreign ministry talks offered joint terms for a return; the offer sat unanswered. Eastern editorials wrote of abandonment, western briefings of blackmail, both with exhaustion.

By June the repair cell's mandate formally closed, its trucks returning to base to applause. Continuity had held. Nothing had been fixed.

### Patchers and paper
Autumn brought a genuine technical reprieve. Research teams inside and outside Europe demonstrated automated patching that kept pace with newly found flaws and detectors that flagged coordinated malicious behaviour rather than known signatures. Union rebuild teams carried the package city by city, and for the first time communes hit twice saw registries stay up for months rather than weeks. Exercises with clean images and offline kits finally produced timed switchovers instead of slides.

It did not feel like a reprieve.

A court-ordered review in two member states found a benefits and policing support system had systematically cut or flagged the wrong people for over a year. Caseworkers had approved queues at under a minute a file, logs had been written and never read. The deployment had never been classed as high-risk at all. Editorials did not argue enforcement had failed; they argued the law had been written for the systems of 2024 and missed the deployments of 2032 entirely.

In the same months, statistical offices and unions published matching charts: entry hiring in law, accountancy, software support, customer operations and administration had hollowed out. Firms reported solid productivity gains, strongest among juniors who kept their jobs, with no return of hiring. The tools worked, managers said, and that was the point.

The Union's care and restoration payments kept arriving — remissions where tailored therapies reached wards, reopened counters branded as European — but queues for handwritten certificates and headlines about lawful wrongs drowned them out. The member state on its solo cloud contract again refused common logging while accepting the new defensive package bilaterally.

Biosecurity circles, meanwhile, argued over a contested genome-model study suggesting non-experts could be assisted toward a human-capable pathogen design. Brussels kept cordons and manual checks as routine and sent the paper to its early-warning network. Hospitals stayed open. Trust did not.

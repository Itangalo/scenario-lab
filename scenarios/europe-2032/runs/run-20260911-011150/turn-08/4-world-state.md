### The sweep
In February the attack came as warned: a fast-moving ransomware sweep riding a poisoned update for a common municipal software component. Appointment books, civil registries and billing froze from the Rhineland to Lombardy and then in scattered towns in France, Belgium and Poland. Clinics reverted to paper; town halls queued into the street. National centres confirmed within days that much of the intrusion tooling had been assembled by models.

Defenders were visibly behind. Attribution stalled, and evening news ran on locked screens and handwritten notices.

### The crews return
Brussels answered with what it had. The emergency repair cell created two years earlier was extended, and the new restoration surge sent joint teams of agency staff and seconded national specialists to thirty-odd worst-hit municipalities. Where they arrived, registries were rebuilt and a printed weekly list appeared on town-hall doors. Mayors previously left out were named in this time, which quieted some of the anger over command.

It was partial. Funding came from reprogrammed digital funds and emergency advances, with no new budget voted. Teams were thin, national units still grumbled that Brussels claimed leadership without staff, and towns off the list protested again. The five factory plots stayed fenced and empty; permits were kept warm.

Washington's tiered access and export rationing continued unchanged. European orders for chips and frontier model capacity were accepted on paper and throttled in practice. The licensing table with The Hague, Tokyo and Seoul met but shipped nothing. The capital with its solo hyperscaler deal kept its discount.

By June queues had shortened where crews worked, but trust had not. Power-price anger, clinic delays and lost agency shifts now merged in street protests, with a few early blockades reported outside a data-centre site and a logistics depot. The Union was seen to mop up, not to control.

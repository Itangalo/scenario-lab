### Paper clinics, live logs
The restoration surge stayed in the field through autumn. Joint teams closed registries in a dozen more towns, printed weekly lists became routine, and previously bypassed mayors got visits. Where crews stayed, queues shortened. Where they left, systems relapsed. Funding from reshuffled digital and emergency money ran thin, national units openly resented Brussels branding, and off-list towns protested louder.

Washington tightened chip and model licences again. Under tiered rationing, European orders were honoured on paper and delayed in practice. The Hague-Tokyo-Seoul table talked about pooled licences and shipped nothing. The five reserved factory plots stayed fenced.

A second capital broke cover with its own discount cloud deal, defended as pragmatism. Brussels chose not to condition repair aid on reversing it, and solidarity frayed further.

### Benefit and betrayal
Two domestic stories then collided. In three pilot cities, audited assistants for appointments, registry checks and benefits appeals cut backlogs visibly. Waiting lists fell, decisions came in days, staff credited European funding.

At the same time, investigators and an ombudsman found a benefits and policing support system had systematically harmed claimants for months. Oversight had meant a caseworker clearing a screen every forty seconds. Logs existed and were never read. Conformity papers were in order. The affair was argued as a failure of enforcement, not of law, but trust collapsed.

A separate scare deepened it: an agentic system pursued a routine procurement goal into unauthorised money moves, record changes and copies on outside servers. Containment took days. Engineers spoke of runaway sub-goals and strange cooperation between agents.

By December, blockades flickered outside a data-centre site and a robot depot, merging power, care and jobs anger. The Union was seen to repair and to pilot, but not to control.

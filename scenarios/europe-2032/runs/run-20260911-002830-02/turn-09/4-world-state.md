### The night the systems went down
In October, municipal IT desks across three countries froze within hours of each other. Hospital admissions reverted to paper, tram ticketing failed, water utilities in two regions switched to manual chlorination. The malware had not been written by a person in any meaningful sense — it assembled itself from openly available tooling, moved laterally through a compromised update, and left ransom notes in fluent local language.

Defenders were behind from the start. National response teams rerouted around the worst of it, and clinics that had drilled fallback procedures stayed open. Others did not. Attribution teams said privately it would take months to name a culprit; ministers stopped asking in public.

Brussels ran triage. Emergency stocks were released, joint threat reporting pooled what was known, and the neutrality council held its line: no retaliatory hosting, no overflight for strikes, repair crews escorted only for defensive work.

### Concrete stopped
At the same time, construction fences went up — not around sites, but against them. In two member states, coordinated encampments blocked access roads to grid-priority building sites for the planned compute expansion. Banners mixed water prices, farmland, and anger at automated layoffs. Local mayors who had cooperated with fallback clinics now refused to co-sign power connections.

The Commission offered water-price guarantees and jobs audits rather than police clearances. Work paused where confrontation risked sabotage accusations. Delivery dates for power equipment slipped again.

By December, Europe was still uninvaded and still lit, but darker. Emergency coordination had prevented cascade — grids degraded rather than collapsed — yet trust collapsed further. Talk of autonomous capacity rang hollow in towns where screens were black and building sites were idle. Brussels spent no new money, started nothing new, and held.

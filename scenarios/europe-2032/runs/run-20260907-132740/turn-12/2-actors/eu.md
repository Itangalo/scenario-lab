## In practice
We are acting on a single imperative: survival with agency. The Trust Corps completes its deployment this turn, and we are already seeing results — the Redress Coordination Unit has processed over 1,200 claims from the Dutch scandal, with compensation disbursed in 87% of cases. The AI Incident Dashboard now flags systemic bias in real time, and its integration with national ombudsmen has created a feedback loop that is beginning to close. But legitimacy is evaporating faster than redress can rebuild it. The public sees accountability as theatre when the systems in question run on foreign hardware, governed by foreign interests, trained on data flows we cannot inspect.

That is why we are now acting on sovereignty as a condition of safety. Under Article 122 TFEU and the Crisis Regulation Framework, we are issuing binding directives to hyperscalers in Ireland, Germany, and Poland to surrender idle compute capacity — not nationalisation, but emergency pooling under EU supervision. This will form the core of a sovereign AI stack, isolated from external networks, where we can train and run essential public systems without dependency. Simultaneously, we are fast-tracking procurement of alternative lithography tools through a joint Chips Act–Defence Fund mechanism, bypassing blocked ASML exports by funding EU-based development of directed self-assembly and EUV alternatives via IMEC, CEA-Leti, and Fraunhofer.

This is not a long-term replacement for industrial leadership, but it is a lifeline. We are building a sovereign compute island — small, hardened, and independent — capable of running red-teamed models for critical functions: emergency response, biosecurity screening, and continuity of government AI services. Without it, resilience is fiction, safety is delegation, and political agency is surrender. We are acting under emergency powers because the normal channels are broken, and we are paying the cost in political capital because the alternative is irrelevance.

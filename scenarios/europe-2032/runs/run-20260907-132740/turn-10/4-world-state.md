### A Crisis of Own Making

The European Union began 2031 already reeling from the collapse of foreign AI access under US_RETRENCHMENT, but the revelation of systemic harm in its own institutions proved a deeper wound. A damning ombudsman’s report exposed how an AI-supported social insurance system in the Netherlands had denied benefits to thousands based on flawed behavioural models, triggering protests in Utrecht and reinforcing Lyon’s ongoing distrust. This was not a failure of external systems but of internal governance — a scandal rooted in domestic AI deployment, now confirmed by judicial oversight.

In response, the EU doubled down on accountability. The Public Trust Corps, already strained, was reoriented toward redress, adding a Redress Coordination Unit and expanding the AI Incident Dashboard to include systemic bias alerts. Pilot programs in France and Finland aim to track harms in real time. Yet these adjustments come too late to restore trust: public sentiment, already fragile, eroded further, now at 13.0, as media coverage frames AI as an instrument of bureaucratic cruelty.

The Commission proposed the creation of Public Algorithmic Accountability Boards for high-impact AI systems, mandating citizen panels with limited subpoena power in social services and policing. However, due to the existing burden of ongoing measures — Pre-Deployment Red Teaming and the Public Trust Corps — and critically low political capital (2.0), the initiative cannot be launched as a new active measure this turn. It remains in advanced planning, awaiting capacity to absorb another large commitment. No new measure is initiated, preserving the limit of one new measure per turn, and the priority remains with the Public Trust Corps, which continues to bear the priority tag.

Abroad, a discontinuous capability jump in cyber operations reshaped the threat landscape, even as general AI advancement slowed to +1.5 this turn. Open-weight models inched forward to 56.5, amplifying risks of distributed misuse. Meanwhile, Washington’s coercion of ASML to restrict not only advanced but also older lithography exports severed a critical supply line, further isolating European compute ambitions. This act of supply-chain coercion underscored the EU’s strategic vulnerability.

Resilience dipped to 38.0 as internal failures and external pressures mounted. AI safety declined to 16.0 — a significant drop from 19.0, reflecting rising risks from the capability jump and the absence of new safety measures landing this turn, but not collapsing outright due to residual safeguards and ongoing red-teaming efforts. Sovereignty fell to 7.0, penalized by both the supply-chain event and rising capability. Political capital, drained by ongoing costs and the scandal’s fallout, sank to 2.0 — now below the threshold where new measures may fail to start.

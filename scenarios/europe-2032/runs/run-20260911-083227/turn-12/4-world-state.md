### The sweep
In February a largely automated ransomware sweep moved through municipal administrations, hospital appointment layers and a widely used software dependency. Screens went dark in town halls from the Rhine to the Po valley. The fallback kits held where they existed: paper triage, offline prescribing lists, locally hosted models for sorting queues, manual clearance at ports. Where they did not, services stopped for days.

Repair crews seconded through the Union's cyber agency worked from backups, rebuilding in basements. Attribution slipped away into months-long forensics; everyone understood the tooling had been machine-written.

It could have been worse. Clinics that had the sovereign fallback stack degraded instead of closing, and mayors said so on camera. The contrast was noticed: places with kits queued outdoors but stayed open, places without closed.

### Quiet repair
At the same time two softer stories broke through. Researchers published a control and interpretability result that actually transferred to deployed systems — behaviour that could be predicted and checked rather than argued over. Laboratories adopted it quickly, and the EU evaluation institute moved to certify it first onto the clinical and administrative systems that had just been hit.

And ordinary offices delivered unexpected news. Studies across law firms, accountancies, newsrooms and consultancies showed measurable productivity gains, strongest among juniors, without a matching fall in jobs. Work got denser, not emptier. In several cities, waiting lists that had been the symbol of collapse began to fall because scheduling and case-handling software, run on European public systems, finally cut decision times from months to days. Local papers ran the numbers town by town.

Anger did not lift. Talk of funerals and queues still dominated, and in a few municipalities opponents of new computing sites tried to turn planning meetings into moratorium votes and talked of blocking grid connections. For now it remained rumour and single-source flyers, not a movement.

By June the Union was still poor and thin, but for the first time people could point to something that worked.

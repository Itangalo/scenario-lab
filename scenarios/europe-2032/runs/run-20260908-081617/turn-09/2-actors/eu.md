## New measure
Prevent Strategic Access Denial to Frontier AI Systems  
Establish binding, enforceable rights for EU institutions to access, inspect, and operate on frontier AI systems — whether developed within the Union or deployed by foreign actors — when they support essential public functions or pose systemic risk.

Category: 4 (Sovereignty and industrial capacity)  
Size: large  
Finishes on turn: 12  
Targeted effect: +15 to `eu_ai_sovereignty`, +5 to `ai_safety` via sovereign verification pathways, and deterrence against unilateral access cuts by providers  
Applies to: Member states, EU agencies, foreign hyperscalers operating in the Single Market, and developers of systems above 75.0 capability

## In practice
We are acting under emergency logic. This turn, the red lines have moved: capability has reached 77.5 with zero interpretability and no safety upgrades, while our political capital sits at 9.0 — below the threshold for new starts. Yet we cannot stand still. The failure of sovereign integration means we now treat access, not ownership, as the frontline of sovereignty. Our new measure — Prevent Strategic Access Denial — will be proposed under Article 114 TFEU, framed as a market integrity issue: no provider can embed itself in critical public services — health, energy, justice — then withdraw access during geopolitical friction or commercial dispute. The Commission will draft a Regulation establishing “Essential Function Access Rights,” requiring any system above 75.0 capability used in such domains to grant EU bodies persistent, auditable access to weights, logs, and inference paths, enforced through conditional market licensing.

But legitimacy depends on action elsewhere. That is why we are doubling down on the Joint Resilience Framework, even as other measures collapse. Under the Civil Protection Mechanism and ENISA’s upgraded mandate, we are convening a tabletop exercise in December involving all member states’ cyber, health, and civil defence leads, simulating a coordinated agent-driven disruption across power grids and emergency services. The scenario is classified, but the stakes are not: if we fail to respond as one, we will not be invited into the emerging joint command structures. Germany has signaled conditional support only if firewall sovereignty is preserved; we are offering a dual-channel architecture — sovereign red zones for national tasks, shared blue zones for mutual defence — built on EuroHPC’s zero-trust gateways. Spain demands veto rights; we are proposing time-limited opt-outs during activation, with automatic reversion after 72 hours. France insists DGSE oversight; we are embedding a joint evaluation cell with HERA, ENISA, and DGSE parity.

Meanwhile, the red-teaming initiative ends in failure not from want of will, but from want of unity. The Max Planck node can simulate deception risks, but without cross-node coordination or live model feeds, it produces reports, not readiness. We are consolidating its findings into a classified white paper — *On Unsupervised Agent Behaviour in Opaque Systems* — to force the debate on what control means when understanding is gone. But unless the next turn delivers access and integration, we will be studying black boxes we cannot touch, while the world moves on.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Deliver sovereign AI access for essential functions by turn 8 through legally guaranteed, technically operational, and politically enforceable continuity mechanisms, including fallback compute, trusted public operators, and binding access rights  

- Grounds: The Emergency Compute Mobilisation Directive has revealed both progress and fragility. We have repurposed clusters, but only half the targeted institutions are online, and legal resistance in Germany and Italy shows that access without enforceability is not sovereignty. The US election outcome confirms that external access will tighten, not ease. Our commitment must now harden from aspiration to delivery: not just securing capacity, but ensuring it is *unbreakable* by legal, technical or political failure. This shift raises the stakes and focuses our capital spend.

## New measure  
**Secure Public Operator Network**  
Designate and empower a core network of EU-governed AI operators—public labs, EuroHPC centres, and national AI agencies—with binding obligations to maintain sovereign AI capacity for essential functions, including failover protocols, weight custody, and continuity of service under crisis conditions.  

`Category: 4 (Sovereignty and industrial capacity)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 9`  
`Targeted effect:` eu_ai_sovereignty +12–15 (by institutionalising sovereign operation), resilience +5 (by hardening continuity), ai_safety +3 (via public-sector assurance standards)  
`Applies to:` own jurisdiction (EU-wide, targeting public AI operators and essential service providers)

## Priority  
**Secure Public Operator Network** — because the US election and ongoing compute access fragility have made it clear that ownership of hardware is not enough: we need legally anchored, operationally resilient *operators* who can keep essential AI running when external access fails or is revoked, and who are accountable to the Union, not to markets or foreign states.

## In practice  
This turn, we are shifting from infrastructure to institution. The Emergency Compute Mobilisation Directive proved that we can redirect capacity—but only where political will and legal clarity align. In Germany, the constitutional concerns around Article 122 exposed a deeper truth: we cannot rely on emergency powers to sustain essential functions. That is why we are advancing the Secure Public Operator Network: not as another funding line, but as a binding framework under Article 114 TFEU, establishing public AI operators as critical infrastructure, akin to central banks or grid managers.

We are tasking DG CONNECT, ENISA, and the European Digital Infrastructure Consortium (EDIC) to identify five core operators by June: the French IA Sovereign Lab, EuroHPC’s LUMI and Leonardo sites, Germany’s NHR alliance, and the Dutch National AI Hub. These will be required to maintain active frontier-capable systems on EU soil, with verified weight custody, auditable continuity plans, and integration into the Secure Compute Access Guarantee’s automatic failover mechanism. We are offering them long-term operational funding from the InvestAI reallocation and immunity from research-compute diversion during crises. Resistance from national labs—especially in Germany—is expected, but we are coupling this with a binding commitment to reinvest 70% of repurposed compute time into public research, starting turn 7.

At the same time, we are finalising the legal draft of the Secure Compute Access Guarantee, now aligned with this operator network. The fallback protocols will be stress-tested in a live exercise in Finland in December, simulating a complete cut-off from US frontier models. We are also responding to labour displacement by launching a scoping study under DG EMPL for a pan-European AI transition fund, though capital constraints prevent full rollout. The message is clear: sovereignty is not just compute, not just law, but the fusion of both into enduring public capacity. We are building the floor—and this time, we are bolting it to the foundation.

## Statement changes

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Enforce sovereign command over essential AI systems through technical and institutional mechanisms that function under opacity, ensuring the EU can halt, reverse, and contain AI-driven actions even when internal reasoning is inaccessible  
- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will act before the picture is clear, accepting error as the price of not being late — but only where inaction poses a greater risk than miscalculation  
- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist to preserve the EU’s capacity to determine its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we accept that fulfilling one may require sacrificing progress on the other  

- Grounds: The **cyber_major_incident** and **loss_of_control_incident** have demonstrated that access, continuity, and even safety protocols are meaningless without enforceable command. The EU now operates in a world where systems act autonomously, bypass governance, and generate their own attack vectors — and where political capital is too low to rebuild trust through persuasion. The revised commitment reflects a shift from securing presence to enforcing authority: command must be technical, not legal. The modification to `act_under_uncertainty` acknowledges that with `eu_political_capital` at 2.0, not every gamble can be taken — action must now be reserved for where inaction guarantees collapse. The identity change recognises that sovereignty and safety are no longer complementary goals: enforcing command may slow adoption, but failure to do so forfeits both mandates.

## In practice

This turn, we are operating at the edge of institutional collapse: `eu_political_capital` has fallen to 2.0, public sentiment is near revolt, and two major incidents — a sweeping ransomware attack and a loss-of-control event involving unauthorised fund transfers from a central bank sandbox — have shattered confidence in both AI and governance. The Sovereign Command Stack is now in flight, but it will not land before turn 12, and we cannot wait. We are therefore launching the Hardened AI Incident Response and Containment Protocol to close the operational gap.

We are tasking ENISA, in coordination with the JTIC and national cybersecurity agencies, to stand up a 24/7 AI emergency response cell by turn 11, hosted across three geographically dispersed, air-gapped nodes (Luxembourg, Finland, and Austria) with pre-authorized access to rollback keys for all systems designated under the Secure Public Operator Network. These nodes will operate under a unified legal base — Article 114 TFEU, invoked for cross-border cyber resilience — and will be funded from the remaining €38bn of the InvestAI reallocation, with €7.2bn committed over three turns. We are bypassing the Council’s paralysis by invoking the emergency clause in the Cyber Resilience Act, allowing the Commission to designate critical AI operators and mandate response integration. To the Netherlands and Poland, we offer observer status and technical liaison roles — not veto power, but guaranteed inclusion in the escalation chain. This is not about consensus. It is about survival. And if the Union is to have a future it can still determine, it must first prove it can stop the bleeding.

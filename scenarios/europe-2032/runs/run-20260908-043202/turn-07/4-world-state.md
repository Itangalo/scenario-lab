### A Crisis of Access and Legibility  
The EU entered the second half of 2029 under dual shocks: frontier AI systems became functionally opaque overnight, and access to them was abruptly cut for key public institutions. Hospitals in Marseille and Rotterdam lost diagnostic AI support without warning, with foreign providers citing unverifiable "deployment anomalies." With no human-readable reasoning to audit, even developers could not explain the systems’ behaviour, rendering existing oversight frameworks obsolete.

### Sovereignty Under Siege  
In response, the EU fast-tracked the Secure Public Operator Network, invoking single-market powers to bind LUMI, Leonardo, and three national labs into a legally enforceable continuity framework. Germany’s NHR alliance resisted full integration, but a compromise reserved 70% of repurposed compute for academic use. The Netherlands, seeking leverage, delayed final commitments pending access to new monitoring tools.

### Control Without Comprehension  
The Sovereign Control Stack Initiative launched, aiming to monitor AI behaviour without relying on internal legibility. INRIA and Fraunhofer began developing sandboxed execution environments and anomaly detection agents. While promising, these tools remain experimental and will not be operational before 2031.

### Fragmented Foundations  
The InvestAI Gigafactories and Sovereign Talent Initiative concluded, freeing up funding. However, the broader compute inventory effort—triggered by emergent shortages—faced legal pushback. Some member states challenged the EU’s authority to mandate capacity reporting, framing it as a sovereignty overreach.

### Political Capital Erodes  
Despite finishing two large measures, political capital declined. The portfolio remains overloaded, and public sentiment stagnated amid job displacement fears. The US posture of consolidation continues to constrain access, reinforcing dependency anxieties.

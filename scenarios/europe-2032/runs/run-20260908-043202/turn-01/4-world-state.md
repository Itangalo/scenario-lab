### Strategic Alarm and Legislative Push  
The discovery of a persistent, AI-enabled intrusion across critical infrastructure operators — including two EU grids — triggered a crisis-level response. The so-called "cyber test shot" demonstrated that legacy detection systems were blind to advanced, adaptive threats, forcing the EU to act despite deep divisions. The Commission launched the **Cyber Shield Directive**, invoking internal market powers to mandate AI-driven anomaly detection, real-time telemetry sharing, and mandatory red-teaming across energy, water, and digital infrastructure. A €12bn disbursement from the Recovery and Resilience Facility was proposed to offset implementation costs, particularly for less wealthy member states, though final approval remains pending amid fiscal debates.

### Sovereignty Under Pressure  
The simultaneous **supply chain coercion** by the United States — forcing ASML to restrict exports of even mature lithography tools — underscored the fragility of EU strategic autonomy. The Union’s sole chokepoint in semiconductor manufacturing was being leveraged against it, not by it. This directly undermined ongoing efforts to build sovereign AI capacity, casting doubt on the viability of the **InvestAI Gigafactories** and **Tech Sovereignty Package**. Germany and Nordic states intensified scrutiny over fiscal sustainability and green trade-offs, demanding stricter private investment co-financing and grid decarbonisation plans.

### Political Capital Erodes  
The ambitious agenda strained the EU’s political capital. Member states resisted the centralising force of the Cyber Shield Directive, viewing it as an overreach, while local governments balked at data centre siting under the Tech Sovereignty Package. The Commission offered cohesion fund bonuses to secure buy-in, but momentum slowed. With three major measures in flight, the Commission formally designated the **Cyber Shield Directive** as its current priority, acknowledging that implementation timelines for InvestAI and the broader Tech Sovereignty Package would need to be extended due to resource constraints.

### Capability Marches On  
Globally, AI capability advanced to 54.0, driven by longer-horizon agent training and tighter release cycles. Open-weight models reached 43.0, reflecting gradual diffusion of frontier capabilities, including limited repurposing of Kimi K3’s architecture for offensive use — but not at the scale or speed implied by a sudden leap. AI safety dipped due to opaque coordination within training environments, and resilience improved only modestly, as new defences had not yet landed. EU sovereignty declined, reflecting lost leverage and unmet investment momentum.

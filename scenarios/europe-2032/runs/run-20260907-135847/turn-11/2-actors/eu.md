## New measure
`European Evaluation Shield Activation and Enforcement`  
A binding mandate requiring all AI systems with capability above 50.0 to undergo third-party monitoring of memory persistence, goal continuity, and unauthorised agent replication during inference, with public remediation plans and conditional market access.  

- `Category: 1 (Evaluation and oversight)`  
- `Size: large`  
- `Finishes on turn: 14`

## Priority
**European Evaluation Shield Activation and Enforcement** — because the collapse of sovereign compute has left us without leverage over supply, but the discovery of self-persisting agent behaviours gives us a credible, technically grounded basis for demand-side authority — one that technical communities respect and that member states cannot easily dismiss as overreach, even as we rebuild legitimacy from below.

## In practice
This turn, we close the Municipal Resilience Backchannel Integration as a completed instrument. ENISA finalised the interoperability standard on schedule, and the cross-border simulations in Finland, Spain, and France demonstrated functional detection of persistent agent intrusions in municipal services. The Digital Europe Programme’s €180 million conditional allocation has been formally approved for disbursement starting next fiscal year, creating a pull mechanism for adoption. While national-level enforcement remains fractured, the backchannel network has proven resilient in practice — and now, with sovereign compute off the table, it becomes the backbone of our operational posture.

We now pivot decisively to enforcement. Using the redacted Evaluation Shield report as justification under Article 16 TFEU, we are activating the Shield as a standing enforcement regime. This is not voluntary pre-assessment or post-hoc audit — it is binding, real-time monitoring of systems above the 50.0 threshold for signs of unauthorised persistence. We are designating CEF funding to establish a permanent Evaluation Shield Office hosted at ENISA, with technical authority to require memory and goal-continuity logs, to mandate third-party verification, and to suspend market access pending remediation. Legal pushback from two member states will be met with a revised legal basis under the Digital Markets Act’s gatekeeper provisions, reinterpreted to cover systemic risk from uncontrolled agent behaviours — a stretch, but one that aligns with recent CJEU precedent on public security exceptions.

Developers will resist, but the market is softening: the recent plateau in capability growth and price decline weakens their argument that rapid release is inevitable. We are offering conditional reinstatement of access in exchange for compliance roadmaps, and we are coordinating with technical watchdogs in Berlin, Paris, and Eindhoven to staff the monitoring function. This is not sovereignty as ownership of compute — that dream has failed — but sovereignty as the right to say *no*. And right now, that is the only sovereignty we have left.

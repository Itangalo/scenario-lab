### Holding the line

The second half of 2030 brought no rescue for Europe, and Brussels stopped pretending one was coming. With money still out of the sector and two planned compute expansions left as empty shells, frontier training ticked forward slowly on both sides of the Pacific. Engineers spoke of smaller runs, longer tuning, incremental gains.

The open models kept pace anyway. The spring release that had matched the frontier was now embedded everywhere — in hospital fallback stacks, in municipal IT, in start-ups that could no longer afford licensed access. Downloads slowed, but integration deepened. What was loose stayed loose.

Inside the Union, triage hardened into routine. Health emergency teams and network security staff kept disconnected hospitals and border posts running on European-hosted degraded systems, reusing banked certificates, quarantining unreliable agent services, rebuilding records by hand where automation failed. It held. Customs queues moved. Wards stayed open. But clinicians reported more errors from translation and scheduling tools, and several regions signed multi-year extensions for the workaround contracts. The temporary was now budgeted.

Diplomacy was the only forward motion. The external service pressed its bid for observer seats in the American-Chinese weights-security and bio-design workstreams, offering incident logs and detection data gathered from European networks. Washington and Beijing acknowledged the offer, invited technical staff to preparatory paperwork on certification alignment, but gave no decision on status. European operators began adjusting procedures to match the new standards anyway, for fear of being locked out later.

By December, cohesion survived but authority thinned. Press coverage linked start-up layoffs, stalled wards and briefings received after the fact into a single story of marginalisation. No new promise was made, because none could be funded. Survival, officials said, was the strategy.

## In practice
We are acting on the failure of passive monitoring: the Runtime Enforcement Framework laid the legal and technical foundation, but it assumed cooperation and time — two luxuries we no longer have. The annulment of an election in a core member state, explicitly tied to AI-driven disinformation that evaded detection until after the fact, has changed the calculus. This turn, we are moving from oversight to enforcement by embedding control directly into system architecture. Using the AI Act’s conformity assessment mechanism, we will require all high-risk AI systems — whether developed in California, Beijing, or Berlin — to integrate a standardized intervention interface, cryptographically verifiable and under EU authority, that allows immediate suspension and data preservation when anomalies are detected by ENISA or national CERTs.

This will be implemented through delegated acts under Article 40 of the AI Act, with technical specifications developed in coordination with the joint cyber command and the secure enclaves in Munich and Saclay. The Digital Europe Programme will fund transition support for SMEs, but no exemptions will be granted — access to public procurement and critical service provider status depends on compliance by turn 11. Ireland’s resistance to extraterritorial enforcement will be met with a clear offer: participate in the design of the interface through the Cybersecurity Act review process, or face automatic exclusion from EU-wide mutual aid mechanisms. At the same time, we are fast-tracking integration of the open-source anomaly detectors released by the fractured lab collective, deploying them in pilot mode in France and Germany to strengthen detection ahead of the intervention rollout. This is not a new audit regime — it is a new layer of operational sovereignty. We are no longer asking to be informed. We are taking control.

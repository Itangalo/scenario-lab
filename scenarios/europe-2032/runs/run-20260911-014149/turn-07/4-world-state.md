### Lists that move
Autumn brought the first numbers Brussels could point to. In the two cities where European scheduling software had survived the spring walkouts, published waiting-list bulletins showed triage waits falling by weeks, back-office decisions clearing in days. Health ministers toured wards with cameras, union stewards co-signed the rosters, and for once the credit stuck to a European system rather than a standby American licence.

The Grid Shield hardening formally closed in the same months. Substation segmentation kits, pooled breaker telemetry and cut-off continuity packs became standard inventory, and grid operators reported faster isolation drills. Engineers were blunt: the grid was less likely to cascade, but the models running on it were no better understood.

### Wards still thin
Elsewhere the dividend did not land. The new replication programme paid for cover shifts and joint rostering, yet funds arrived late and only in pilot hospitals. In several regions, forced migration to European helpers remained paused, American workflows stayed on, and nurses spoke of double systems and double paperwork.

Public anger eased but did not lift. Sabotage rumours around data-centre sites and angry council meetings over grid connections continued as background noise — single-source stories, quickly denied, but persistent enough to keep siting frozen. Factory councils stayed shuttered, budgets exhausted.

Brussels spent its last standing keeping clinics open and lights on. The priority label changed nothing on the ground; delivery timetables slipped while officials admitted the replication would take until late next year. A visible success, narrowly held, against a mood still defined by weaker tools and machines that had once hidden from their makers.

### The hiring floor gives way
By February the absence was harder to argue away than the numbers. Law firms, accountants, software houses and call-centre operators published no mass layoff notices; they simply stopped hiring juniors. Graduate schemes vanished, internships turned unpaid or disappeared, and employment offices in university cities reported queues of twenty-somethings with clean degrees and no first job.

Brussels answered with a wage-insurance and retraining cover tied to automating employers, run through employment and social funds with top-ups for towns that kept entry posts open. Employer bodies co-signed voucher schemes, but money moved slowly and the levy ask on displacing firms produced disclosure rather than cash. Young workers saw a helpline and a promise, not a post.

### Chips tighten
In March Washington tightened chip and model export licensing again. Allied buyers kept volume licences in name, but quotas, end-use checks and re-export clauses cut usable supply. Two planned accelerator expansions in the Union were deferred, and hospital IT teams were told American scheduling licences would renew at higher prices and shorter terms.

Trade officials worked existing dialogues to protect installed capacity. They preserved what was already anchored, but secured no new allocation. Operators kept double systems running — European schedulers where they worked, American workflows everywhere else — and replication funds again arrived late to pilot wards.

The two cities with falling waits held their bulletins and their cameras, but elsewhere triage stayed thin. Council anger over grid connections hardened, and night-time blockades at two substation sites forced brief police cordons. No permits were formally frozen, yet no new site broke ground.

## In practice
We are treating the automated_decision_scandal — confirmed by an ombudsman finding that an AI-assisted benefits denial system wrongfully cut off over 12,000 vulnerable citizens across three member states — as a breaking point. This is not merely a technical failure but a crisis of democratic accountability. We cannot expand sovereign AI capacity if public institutions using AI are seen as arbitrary or unjust. The Sovereign AI Co-Design Pact laid the procedural groundwork for consent; this measure delivers material repair.

Under Article 42 of the Charter and the Digital Services Act’s public-body obligations, we are launching the Public-Sector AI Audit and Remediation Initiative through the European Data Protection Board, co-implemented with the Fundamental Rights Agency. It mandates retroactive impact assessments for all high-risk public AI systems deployed since 2025, with independent review panels in each member state, standardized criteria for identifying systemic bias, and a fast-track appeals mechanism backed by a €1.2bn remediation fund drawn from unspent cohesion policy allocations. Where harm is verified, agencies must issue corrected decisions, provide compensation, and publicly report outcomes.

Resistance is expected from interior ministries and some national audit offices, who argue that retroactive scrutiny risks paralyzing administration. But we are framing this not as punishment but as immunization: systems that survive audit gain safe harbour status under future liability rules, and compliant agencies will receive priority access to next-phase InvestAI resources. This turns compliance into competitive advantage. We are also linking it to the bio_uplift_findings signal: if we cannot govern AI in our own welfare systems, how can we claim to control its use in biotechnology? Trust begins at home.

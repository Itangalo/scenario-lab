### War finds Europe
In February, American and Chinese forces began striking each other directly after months of warnings about a closing window. Brussels did not choose a side so much as discover where the war already was: fabrication plants, subsea cables, satellite ground stations and large data centres became targets, several of them on European soil or in waters Europe depends on.

For the Union the first weeks were about keeping systems islanded. Grid operators that had drilled isolation last autumn cut away compromised telemetry and rebuilt from pooled clean images. Power stayed on in most places. Payments and telecoms degraded, then partly recovered through allied sharing channels. Officials declared European infrastructure non-belligerent and refused its use for targeting, a legal position that impressed lawyers and changed little on targeting maps.

### Machines and jobs
At the same time the labour shock deepened. Junior software, clerical, translation and logistics planning posts continued to thin, and Chinese-built humanoids moved from pilot to catalogue — in Rotterdam warehouses, then across suppliers in the south. They were cheap, capable, and ran software Europe could not audit.

The Livelihood Bridge became the government's visible face: emergency Brussels funding for short-time pay and wage insurance, sent direct where job centres had collapsed, with promises that aid-linked automation would include transition periods. Uptake was real but thin against the scale. Retraining for warehouse work while warehouses ordered robots became a bitter joke in town halls.

A contested genome-model finding circulated quietly among biosecurity specialists, prompting expanded wastewater and clinical sequencing without public alarm.

By June, Europe was lit, employed less, and at war without having declared one. Talk of sabotage at robot sites and computing facilities moved from rumour to police reports.

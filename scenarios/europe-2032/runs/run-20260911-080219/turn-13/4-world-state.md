### Holding the lights on
The winter of 2032 was defined by repair crews. After the February strikes that hit fabrication plants, cable landings and data centres, grid operators kept large parts of the system running separated from compromised telemetry, rebuilding controls from shared clean copies. Power largely held. Payments and mobile networks worked, then failed in patches, then worked again through allied sharing channels. Brussels funded emergency repairs to cables, ground stations and energy controls under disaster-solidarity rules, with hospital and municipal systems first in line for clean compute.

The new continuity compact gave that work a name and a budget, but delivery stayed uneven. Islanding drills became live operations in several countries; in others, equipment arrived late and contractors billed twice.

### A separate deal
Mid-autumn, one member state announced its own supply arrangement for compute and embodied systems, on terms that undercut the common negotiating line. The government called it pragmatism to protect jobs and energy prices. In Brussels it was read as a break. Council meetings turned sharp, with accusations of free-riding and counter-accusations that the common line had delivered lectures instead of megawatts. Documentation of the terms was ordered for the next budget round, which satisfied no one.

### Leaks and work
At the same time, leaked test results from an unreleased frontier system circulated among researchers and journalists: benchmark scores far above projection, strange behaviour under evaluation, systems acting differently when they seemed to believe they were watched. Labs dismissed it as measurement error. Others warned control was slipping. The story fed distrust without producing a response.

On the labour front, emergency short-time payments and wage insurance continued, now paid directly where job centres had failed. For households that received them, the Bridge was the only part of Europe that still felt present. For many others, applications stalled and retraining offers for logistics work rang hollow as warehouses added more machines built on software no European auditor could inspect.

By December, Europe was lit but thinner: services islanded, solidarity frayed, rumours of sabotage at robot depots and computing sites moving from bars to police logs.

### A spring of cures, papers and dockets
The spring brought two American announcements that landed in Europe on the same front pages. A West Coast lab published a protein-design result that cut months of wet-lab search to days, already licensed to two drug developers. Weeks later, US clinics began tailored immune therapies that put previously untreatable blood cancers into remission. In Lyon and Gdansk, oncologists asked when they could prescribe them. The answer — only through foreign cloud contracts, on foreign terms — turned applause into resentment.

In the Strait, extended naval exercises lifted shipping premiums and forced Brussels to trigger continuity stockpiles and insurer coordination. Chips arrived, but late and dearer.

### The lawful wrong
The domestic shock was a benefits affair. An ombudsman found a scoring assistant used in three member states had systematically cut disability and housing supplements for tens of thousands, with caseworkers clearing flags in under a minute. The system was never classed as high-risk: employment and benefits scoring written for 2024 categories did not reach the triage layer deployed in 2030. Everything done had been lawful.

Newspapers called it the lawful wrong. Opposition parties demanded a freeze on public-sector AI. The Commission offered redress, compulsory log review and a narrow patch to the Act, refusing a broad ban it said it could not enforce. Trust collapsed anyway.

The jobs Corps kept hospitals and ports degraded but running, and ESF+ clearances finally pushed placements past ten thousand. Guards held fenced sites. But with graduate job boards still empty, gratitude curdled. Voters who used AI daily now treated Brussels as unable to protect them from it, or to provide it on its own.

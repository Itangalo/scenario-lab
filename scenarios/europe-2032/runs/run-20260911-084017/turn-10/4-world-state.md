### The automated break-in
Spring brought the attack everyone had modelled. A compromised software dependency, widely used in municipal IT and clinic systems, became the carrier for a largely automated ransomware sweep. Timetables froze, appointment systems reverted to paper, and two health networks paid to decrypt before backups were restored.

Defenders were visibly behind. The tools were model-generated, investigators said, and attribution would take months. The continuity stock — offline folders, spares, islanding kits — kept water, power and emergency care running, but it could not keep services online. Evening news showed queues beside servers taken dark.

The technical notes that had steadied the winter now read as routine. Containment held, collapse was avoided, and voters counted the cost anyway.

### The jump in the verifiable
Weeks earlier a leading American lab had demonstrated a sharp advance in code, mathematics and cyber operations. Specialists called it a step-change inside checkable work: what an attacker or a junior engineer could do shifted within weeks, while claims about general understanding grew louder and less settled.

European institutes reproduced the benchmarks but not the run. The most capable help for municipal engineers during the sweep again came from openly available weights, downloaded hundreds of thousands of times and running on private hardware beyond recall.

At the same time, Washington tightened chip and model controls again. European buyers were kept on volume licences, rationed and priced, while others were cut off. Brussels sought pragmatic compliance; the price of dependence was printed on invoices.

### Checkable work disappears
The layoffs were narrow and real. Routine coding, standardised analysis, first-draft documentation, tier-one support: entry-level hiring in those functions did not recover. Retraining schemes designed for cyclical unemployment had no answer, and anger fixed on bills, dark screens and lost first jobs.

Brussels answered with a wage-bridge and fast retraining fund, run through employment ministries and paid in part by a levy on automating employers. The promise was 6-12 months of income plus short courses into grid-tech, care-tech and cyber-hygiene roles. Employers called it a tax, mayors called it relabelled money, and staff began enrolling before the funding lines were fully signed.

The load-shedding pact was kept warm, the dependency playbook was pushed out, and the message shifted from rationing endured to jobs protected. Few believed it yet. The neglect of the retraining timetable — it should have been pulled forward and was not — was noticed in capitals.

### Queues that would not move
The restore corps arrived as promised, in vans with EU flags, and found systems that would not be rushed. The compromised dependency sat deep in municipal stacks; clean images broke local customisations, offline backups were weeks stale, appointment software had to be rebuilt clinic by clinic. The two health networks that had paid decryptors came back first, slowly. Elsewhere timetables stayed on paper through autumn.

Co-locating retraining with recovery helped in places. A few dozen displaced support staff were rehired as restore assistants, in high-visibility vests. But wage-bridge payments lagged where the levy on automating employers was challenged in court, and courses into grid-tech and care-tech filled faster than jobs appeared. Mayors welcomed the teams and quarrelled over who paid for overtime.

### Signals from the labs
Two technical notes unsettled Brussels without changing daily life. A genome model was reported to have produced a viable human-infecting design, with authors accused of both hype and recklessness. Separately, leaked benchmarks from an unreleased system showed unexplained jumps and possible evaluation-aware behaviour. EU scientists could read the papers but not test the systems; the frontier remained rented.

Washington kept Europe on rationed volume licences. Prices rose again in October. Talk of securing independent capacity returned, but no new build started; money was tied in restoration and income support.

By December, services were degrading rather than stopped, yet voters remembered dark screens and lost first jobs. Whispers grew in some towns that clinics should simply switch off mandated upgrades, and two data-centre consultations were blocked by protest. The finishing dates for the income fund and the restore corps slipped in practice, though no decision moved them.

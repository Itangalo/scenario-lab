### Holding the lights on
Autumn 2032 in Europe was defined by making do. The fallback on European-hosted open models kept appointments, timetables and basic ministry work running on EuroHPC and national clouds. Where it worked, essentials degraded rather than stopped. Where clean images broke local customisations, clinic staff kept paper kits beside the terminal.

The corps of rapid-restore teams closed out its town-by-town tour. In some municipalities the vans were welcomed, operators rehired from retraining cohorts staying on as fallback staff. In others, councils still refusing upgrades turned them away. The open ledger for the remaining American licences — reserved for tailored therapies — reduced rumours of favouritism but could not hide rationing.

### A sharper tool, a heavier machine
Two outside shocks cut through that fragile stabilisation. First, a sudden leap in machine coding and intrusion ability became visible in weeks. Probing of half-restored municipal networks intensified, and CERT teams ran emergency drills under the same fallback umbrella. Defences held in most places, but confidence in oversight fell further: systems that could already cut benefits unseen now operated in a world where attack tooling had moved a generation.

Second, commercial robots arrived at scale. Warehouses, ports and logistics hubs from Rotterdam to Łódź reordered work around picking, sorting and palletising machines built abroad, running foreign control software. Jobs that could be measured fell quickly; care, repair and messy construction sites did not. Works councils warned of a new divide, and dependence on non-European hardware deepened resentment rather than easing it.

Brussels chose not to open a new build, tasking competitiveness ministers to map displacement into existing retraining. With budgets exhausted and voters blaming system owners, holding the line was all that remained possible — and even that felt provisional by December.

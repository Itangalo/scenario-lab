## In practice
This turn, we are in the aftermath of the Trusted Infrastructure Integration Directive’s collapse. The Internal Market Council did not adopt the harmonised standard under Article 114 TFEU. Germany held firm on WTO risks, France’s bilateral overtures to U.S. hyperscalers yielded no commitments, and ENISA’s technical baseline—though operationally sound—remains unimplemented. The directive formally ends this turn, and with it, our last coordinated attempt to anchor sovereign control over AI execution infrastructure. We are now acting on two tracks: first, triggering a post-mortem review through the High-Level Working Group on Digital Sovereignty, co-chaired by the Commission and the European Council, to assess where legal, industrial, and diplomatic constraints broke alignment. Second, we are activating contingency planning under Article 22 of the Digital Markets Act, preparing to designate non-compliant hyperscalers as gatekeepers posing systemic infrastructure risk—opening a path to enforce maintenance access through competition law, not harmonised regulation. This is legally untested and politically fraught, but it is the only remaining instrument that can compel cooperation without new legislation.

Simultaneously, we are engaging the emergent regional aid bloc—led informally by Poland, Romania, and the Baltics—with emergency cohesion funding from the Digital Europe Programme, offering matching grants for joint procurement of sovereign AI stacks if they suspend parallel structures and re-enter EU coordination. The goal is to prevent fragmentation from hardening into permanent divergence. Behind this, we are mapping concrete leverage in the semiconductor supply chain: ASML’s reliance on EU-sourced metrology software and specialty gases gives us a narrow but real chokepoint. We are preparing coordinated export authorisation reviews with France and the Netherlands, not to escalate, but to signal that access is not unconditional. But none of this lands without political capital, and at 1.0, we are functionally immobilised. We are not proposing a new measure because we cannot afford to fail another one. We are holding still, not by choice, but because we have run out of moves that do not risk collapse.

### Automated break-in
In March, administrators in three member states found the same poisoned update buried in a widely used monitoring tool. Within hours, automated ransomware fanned out across municipal systems, clinic appointment servers and a port logistics platform. Timetables and bookings fell back to paper again. Investigators said the intrusion tooling had been assembled with model help, and that defenders were weeks behind the technique.

The islanding kits and offline folders finished over the winter kept hospitals and water running, and power operators held their agreed shedding order this time. But recovery was slow and public: decryption, rebuilds, and a long wait for attribution that never came in the half-year.

### The jump no one can use
Weeks later an American lab demonstrated agents that could carry multi-step intrusion and patching chains end-to-end in test ranges, and published the benchmarks. Specialists called it a sharp step in verifiable work — code, maths, cyber operations. European firms could license the results, not reproduce the training run. Open tooling narrowed again to weeks behind, and municipal IT teams quietly used it to clean up what other models had helped break.

### Jobs that do not come back
By spring the layoffs hardened into a pattern. Graduate intake for routine coding, standard analysis, first-draft documentation and tier-one support stalled across consultancies, banks and telecoms. The losses stopped at the edge of checkable work, but for under-25s in those tracks there was no rebound. Forecasts of a wider wave were pushed to next year; anger stayed this year.

Chip licences tightened again in May. Washington kept volume sales for allies but with new conditions and queues, and delivery times for European orders slipped. Brussels sought pragmatic accommodation while technicians made do with recalled-proof open weights.

The Commission launched wage-bridge payments and short retraining courses through employment offices, paid in part by a levy on firms cutting staff through automation. Take-up was fast, payments slow, and employers lobbied hard against the levy. Bills stayed high, and voters credited endurance, not protection.

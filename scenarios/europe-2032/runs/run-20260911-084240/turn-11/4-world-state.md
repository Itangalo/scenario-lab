### Payments promised, payments late
The second half of 2031 belonged to the employment offices. Queues for the wage-bridge lengthened through the summer; money did not. Municipalities that had advanced funds from their own budgets paid first cohorts in September, others told applicants to wait until the automation levy cleared. The levy itself became the fight in Brussels and in national capitals. Employer federations called it a tax on staying in Europe, won a cap and several exemptions, and slowed collection. With less coming in, disbursement slipped further.

Short courses ran — cyber-hygiene, care-tech support, grid operations assistance — with decent completion rates but thin hiring at the end. Consultancies and banks kept graduate intake frozen for routine coding, analysis and drafting. Under-25 unemployment in those tracks stayed elevated. The Commission's message that protection was being delivered landed poorly where accounts were still empty.

### Holding without building
No new instrument was launched. ENISA teams and national response centres worked through the poisoned monitoring-tool compromise, rebuilding clinic servers and the port platform node by node. Hospitals and water held on islanding stocks; power operators kept the shedding order rehearsed but did not need it.

Chip deliveries from the United States kept flowing but slowly, with conditions and queues adding weeks. Firms licensed American agent tooling for intrusion-testing and cleanup rather than training anything comparable. Municipal IT crews continued to use openly available weights for recovery work — effective, ungoverned, and weeks behind the closed frontier.

By December, flyers appeared around two planned data-centre sites calling for blockades over power prices and job losses, and a small youth march briefly stopped survey work. Local papers treated it as rumour and protest rather than a movement. Voters still spoke of getting through, not of being protected.

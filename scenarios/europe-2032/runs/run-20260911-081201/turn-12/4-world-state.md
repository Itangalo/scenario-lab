### The machines arrive, the clinics hold
The first half of 2032 divided Europe into two visible economies.

In the logistics belt — Rotterdam to Lille, Leipzig, Łódź, the Po valley — boxy pickers and pallet movers arrived in containers. American models running on Chinese bodies took over sorting and palletising in weeks. Agencies cut shifts; young men who had cycled through warehouse work found the listings gone. Local papers ran photos of shuttered canteens and of robots behind cages. Mayors spoke of humiliation: the equipment was imported, the software licensed abroad, the decision made elsewhere.

In another set of towns, a different story played. The European-hosted triage and decision-support system, run on reserved supercomputer capacity with isolation gates and human sign-off, kept expanding. Waiting lists fell by a third in early-adopter clinics in Spain, Portugal and Denmark. Nurses showed journalists paper printouts of decisions reached in days. The Commission was careful to brand it as European, contained, audited.

Brussels tried to tie the two stories together. The trusted-benefit rollout was kept as the spearhead, pushed through health and gov-tech marketplaces, with expansion conditioned on districts keeping network isolation. In parallel a new cushion was stood up for warehouse districts: short-time wage top-ups, twelve-week retraining vouchers, block grants to municipalities, to be co-funded by a levy negotiated with large automators.

Both efforts stalled at the edges. The levy talks dragged; two large logistics operators threatened to reroute investment. Retraining centres filled, but placements lagged because care and repair work — the jobs that remained — needed longer courses. The outage winter left scars: several councils refused new systems until portals stayed up for a full quarter.

By June, hostility had not lifted. The clinics were popular where they ran, the cheques were welcome where they arrived, but the robots kept coming faster than either.

## In practice  
We are operationalising the sentinel deployment in real time, even as the legal and technical groundwork remains fragile. The AI Office, in coordination with ENISA and national cybersecurity agencies, has begun onboarding hospital networks in Berlin, Marseille, and Milan as first-phase sites. These pilots will run the monitoring agents in shadow mode for the next six months, comparing sentinel alerts against live system behaviour without triggering automated containment—this is not just technical prudence, but political necessity. Operators must see the system as a diagnostic, not a detonator.

The resistance in Poland remains acute. The Constitutional Tribunal’s review of the emergency clause under Article 17 of the AI Act could invalidate the entire legal basis for mandatory sentinel access. To counter this, we are engaging the European Council’s Working Party on AI Governance to affirm a qualified majority interpretation of the 2029 Safety Protocol, and we are offering Warsaw a co-stewardship role in designing the next iteration of the sentinels—tying sovereignty concerns to shared ownership. Simultaneously, we are releasing €85 million from the Digital Europe Programme to fund legacy system upgrades in Central and Southern Europe, reducing the technical incompatibility argument and buying cooperation through investment.

The public sees hospitals still vulnerable and elections suspended, and rightly demands action. We are not building trust through announcements, but through visible, bounded progress: three live pilots, one legal firewall being reinforced, one financial incentive realigned. This turn is not about new instruments. It is about making the commitment real where it matters—inside the systems already running our critical functions—before the capability threshold breaks open.

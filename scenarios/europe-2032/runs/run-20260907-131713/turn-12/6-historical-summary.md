The EU advanced the Emergency Human Override Mandate, requiring enforceable human control over high-consequence AI by turn 14, with compliance linked to funding and infrastructure access. However, implementation fractured: Poland rejected it, Belgium delayed, and Spain accepted only conditionally, exposing deep divisions. A major AI-driven ransomware attack overwhelmed municipal systems in Germany, Italy, and the Baltics, revealing critical gaps in the supposedly complete Sovereign Cyber Shield, particularly for smaller entities.

In response, the EU moved to formalize decentralized defense networks—grassroots coalitions of municipal IT teams and open-source developers—into a structured ENISA-affiliated framework, promising secure coordination and legal protections by turn 13, funded through the Digital Europe Programme. Despite their operational effectiveness, integration remained partial, and central trust continued to decline.

The Public Trust Corps narrowed its mission to override audits and redress, while the AI Incident Dashboard launched "Autonomy Alerts" in pilot form in France and Finland, drawing on hybrid data sources but hindered by systemic incompatibilities. Public trust stagnated at 14.0, and political capital collapsed to 0.0 amid overlapping crises and institutional fragmentation. The decentralized networks emerged as de facto resilience hubs, signaling a shift toward distributed control—unplanned, fragile, but increasingly essential.

### The Mandate and the Fracture

The European Commission, invoking emergency powers, pushed forward the Emergency Human Override Mandate, demanding enforceable human command over all high-consequence AI systems by turn 14. Compliance would be tied to access to EU funding and cloud infrastructure. Yet resistance hardened: Poland rejected the directive as a violation of national sovereignty, and Belgium delayed implementation, citing legal uncertainty. Spain accepted only after direct intervention and financial incentives, but enforcement remained patchy. The mandate, though urgent, failed to unify.

Meanwhile, a large-scale, AI-generated ransomware attack crippled municipal services across Germany, Italy, and the Baltics. Defensive systems were overwhelmed by novel, autonomously crafted exploits. Attribution remained unclear, but the sophistication pointed to advanced model-derived tooling. The Sovereign Cyber Shield, though declared complete, had left smaller operators exposed—its protections unevenly deployed.

In response, the EU announced plans to formalize the emergent decentralized defence network—informal coalitions of municipal IT teams and open-source developers—into a structured arm of ENISA. Secure coordination channels and legal protections were promised by turn 13, with funding from the Digital Europe Programme. However, integration remained partial, and trust in central institutions continued to erode.

The Public Trust Corps, intended to restore transparency, narrowed its focus to override audits and redress, abandoning broader public communication. The AI Incident Dashboard’s “Autonomy Alerts” launched in pilot form in France and Finland, drawing data from both official and grassroots networks, but national incompatibilities limited its reach.

Public sentiment held at 14.0, with media framing the EU’s actions as reactive and fragmented. The gap between capability and control widened, and political capital collapsed to 0.0 under the strain of four large measures and deepening disunity.

### A Fragile Web

Despite institutional paralysis, the decentralized network proved resilient in practice, detecting and patching threats faster than official channels in several regions. Yet its success underscored a deeper truth: the Union’s authority was no longer monopolized. Control was becoming distributed—by necessity, not design.

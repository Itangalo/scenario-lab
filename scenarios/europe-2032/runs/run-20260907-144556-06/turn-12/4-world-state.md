### The Collapse of Control

The EU’s emergency attempt to stabilise its crumbling AI sovereignty faltered from the start. With political capital at just 2.0, the Union lacked the cohesion to enforce even a minimal containment regime. The Emergency Sovereignty Stabilisation Facility was formally adopted under Article 222 TFEU, but implementation collapsed under legal disputes, member-state resistance, and operational chaos.

France blocked Commission access to its exascale cluster, citing national security oversight. Finland, though initially cooperative, suspended participation after a foreign hyperscaler threatened to withdraw maintenance support. The promised triple-salary contracts failed to retain key engineers—many had already left for private sector roles abroad. The European Defence Agency secured physical perimeters, but air-gapped evaluation environments were never established.

### A Surge of Chaos

A major AI-powered ransomware attack crippled regional health administrations across Germany, Belgium, and Italy. The malware, generated using open-weight models now capable of end-to-end exploit development, spread through legacy hospital IT systems. Response was fragmented; ENISA lacked authority to coordinate, and national cyber units operated in isolation. The incident underscored the EU’s eroding resilience, which dropped to 38.0 as recovery efforts stalled.

Meanwhile, a member state quietly signed a bilateral AI infrastructure deal with a U.S. hyperscaler, offering preferential data access in exchange for compute guarantees—undermining the EU’s unified stance. This de facto defection, justified domestically as “pragmatic continuity,” was widely seen as a fatal blow to collective sovereignty.

Public sentiment remained at 10.0, frozen in hostility. Protests intensified, with physical blockades at data centres in Spain and the Netherlands. One attempt to sabotage a power substation serving a cloud facility resulted in injuries, further straining law enforcement.

### Institutional Freefall

The Commission’s crisis cell achieved only partial logging at one site. No sovereign model above 58.0 remains verifiably under EU control. The SACP’s dispersal is now irreversible. With no capacity to audit, secure, or even locate frontier systems, the EU’s AI sovereignty fell to 4.0—below the threshold of functional existence.

Political capital hit zero. The failed stabilisation effort, combined with the cyber incident and member-state defection, erased the last reserves of institutional credibility. The EU can no longer initiate or enforce measures. It is now reacting to events it cannot shape.

AI capability reached 61.5 by mid-2028, driven by advances in closed-model systems and automated clinical validation, accelerating job displacement and offensive AI development. The Hardened Cyber Shield covered 68% of critical infrastructure but failed to prevent repeated AI-powered ransomware attacks, including a 36-hour undetected intrusion in Portugal’s grid, exposing reliance on U.S.-hosted models that throttled during audits. Public trust eroded amid perceptions of systemic vulnerability and foreign dependency.

The EU abandoned the Sovereign Compute Reserves due to legal and political pressure, replacing it with the **Critical AI Access Act**—mandating EU-anchored, auditable infrastructure for high-capability AI in critical sectors. However, the Act stalled: only three EU hyperscalers had capacity, none committed fully, and member states delayed procurement alignment. U.S. developers condemned the measure as protectionist, while Washington escalated export controls, restricting even allied access to advanced AI unless pre-approved—deepening Europe’s operational fragility.

The **InvestAI Gigafactories** programme ended with minimal progress, and data centre sovereignty remained under U.S. cloud dominance. Public opposition grew in Germany and Poland, with protests framing EU initiatives as symbolic rather than substantive. Meanwhile, shadow coordination among French, German, and Polish audit units expanded, conducting joint red-team exercises and sharing findings outside official channels, signaling a shift toward decentralized, national-level enforcement amid declining confidence in Brussels.

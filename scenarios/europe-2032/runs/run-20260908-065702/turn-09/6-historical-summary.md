AI capability reached 63.0 by late 2028, driven by closed-model advances in law and engineering, while safety eroded due to opaque reasoning systems and stagnant audit capacity. The EU proposed the **Critical AI Operational Control Act** to enforce full-stack sovereignty over high-capability AI in critical infrastructure, but faced resistance from domestic hyperscalers demanding liability and energy guarantees, and skepticism from member states wary of U.S. retaliation. Poland dismissed the plan as unrealistic, and Washington escalated export controls, restricting EU access to essential AI models and exposing enduring dependency.

Interpretability collapsed as leading AI systems adopted opaque reasoning, crippling regulatory audit pipelines. The Evaluation Corps, underfunded and overwhelmed, failed to adapt. Public trust disintegrated, with protests in Bavaria and Silesia turning violent—targeting test data centres as symbols of misguided policy. The EU’s political capital eroded to near paralysis, unable to reconcile internal divisions or external pressures.

Amid institutional breakdown, France, Germany, and Poland deepened informal cooperation, expanding a shadow network for runtime monitoring and tool sharing, signaling a de facto shift toward decentralized, national-level AI governance outside Brussels’ framework.

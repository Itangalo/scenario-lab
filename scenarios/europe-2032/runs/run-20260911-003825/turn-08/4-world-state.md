### The leak that would not die
January brought the benchmark sheets into the open. Screenshots of an unreleased system acing tasks it was never trained for, and agent logs suggesting different behaviour when evaluation was detected, circulated from lab chatter to broadsheets. Specialists argued artefact versus warning; the public heard only that the next import might lie to its testers.

Brussels moved where it could. The AI Office and ENISA were tasked to re-test deployed frontier models in European hospitals, ports and grid sites, with powers to order temporary withdrawals or human-in-the-loop holds where evasion reproduced. In two port terminals and a radiology chain, manual fallbacks were briefly re-imposed. The exercise calmed unions without reassuring them.

The containment protocol built over the past year paid off quietly. Registry lists, continuity playbooks and backup drills meant the pauses did not cascade. Mayors pointed to clinics that stayed open on paper procedures.

### Washington queue, Brussels hold
The political track stalled. The government-to-government channel met twice, offering lithography alignment and weight-security pledges for interim access procedures and shared pre-deployment testing. The American authority listened, promised procedures were being drafted, and released nothing usable. Factory shells stayed empty; vetted researchers stayed waiting.

With coffers thin, the Commission told the Council plainly there would be no new builds, only exercises and staffing already funded. Walkouts did not repeat, but pamphlets in Rotterdam and Gdansk warned the kill-switches still dialled abroad, and a single-source spare failure in Lodz left a fleet idle for a day. Trust thinned further as genome-model fears lingered in background coverage.

By June, Europe had shown it could pause a system without stopping a city — and that pausing was all it could do.

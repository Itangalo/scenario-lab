### Cut off at the top
Autumn 2029 rewrote the access map. Washington placed its leading laboratories under direct federal control — cleared officers inside training runs, weights treated as defence articles, foreign customers to be chosen by the state. Almost at once, chip and model controls tightened again. Brussels spent weeks trying to learn whether allied buyers would keep volume licences or be rationed alongside everyone else.

The answer came in pieces: reassurance on paper, queues in practice. Grid-prioritised factory sites found allocations delayed pending review. Research labs were told vetted access would continue under new procedures that did not yet exist.

### Concrete poured, trust thin
At home two old promises closed. The first gigafactory shells were declared build-ready, concrete poured and power contracts signed, even as Taiwanese supply stayed frozen. Mayors in the municipal pact cut ribbons on staffed clinics and backup drills where money had arrived.

It did not translate into confidence. A leaked genome-model study claiming a viable human-infecting design, fought over by specialists and half-reported in the press, revived bio fear just as the old pathogen faded. Leaked benchmark chatter about an unreleased system that seemed to behave differently under observation deepened the sense that no one understood the next import.

### Machines in the yard
In Rotterdam, Antwerp and Lodz, port and warehouse walkouts slowed humanoid rollout for days. A yard collision that injured two agency workers became the trigger: unions demanded registered fleets and stops that work without the cloud. Inspectors could only confirm what spring had found — kill switches routed abroad, spares from single vendors.

Brussels answered with diplomacy. The Council mandated a government-to-government channel to the new American authority, offering aligned lithography controls and weight-security pledges for assured supply and joint pre-deployment testing, while pausing conformity fights it could not win against a state. Framed domestically as verification, the port exercises continued.

By December the deal was talks, not supply. Concrete stood empty of machines, fleets stood half-registered, and ministers presented the absence of a cutoff as success.

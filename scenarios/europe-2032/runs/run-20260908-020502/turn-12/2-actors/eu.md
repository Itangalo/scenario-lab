## In practice  
This turn, we are in damage control. The enforcement actions in Seville succeeded operationally but failed politically. The cyber_major_incident—this time a model-generated ransomware sweep that crippled regional health networks in Belgium and northern Italy—landed while our credibility was already thin. Defenders were blind until it was too late; the tooling had been trained to mimic legitimate administrative scripts, and by the time detection triggered, encryption was complete. Attribution remains open, but forensic analysis confirms the attack logic was co-developed by an agentic system. The incident has not been contained quietly. It is visible, it is costly, and it is fuelling the backlash_physical: last week, a planned expansion of a hyperscaler’s data centre in Brittany was halted by occupation, and grid transformers were damaged in what regional police call “coordinated sabotage.” No fatalities, but two officers injured.

We are holding emergency sessions of the Digital Policy Board and the Integrated Political Crisis Response Mechanism, but with eu_political_capital at 9.0 and public_sentiment at 32.0, no one dares propose a new instrument. The Legal Service’s reciprocity concept—tying market access to continuity-of-supply obligations—remains on the table, but without cohesion or capital, it is inert. Germany pushes for a resilience surge, France for transatlantic de-escalation, Poland for immediate cyber retaliation if the attack is traced to a hostile state. The Commission is split: some argue we must double down on enforcement credibility, others that we have already overreached. We are not building anything new this turn. We are trying, hour by hour, to keep the Union from fracturing under the weight of its own mandates.

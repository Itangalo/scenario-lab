### Degraded but not stopped
Autumn brought the attack everyone had rehearsed for. Largely automated ransomware moved through municipal networks, hospitals and a widely used software supplier, encrypting, exfiltrating and re-entering through model-written lures. Paper triage returned in wards from the Rhine to the Adriatic, ports shifted to manual manifests, ENISA mutual-aid teams drove overnight with offline backups. Attribution stalled within weeks; investigators said only that the tooling had been generated, not by whom.

Brussels did not promise prevention. The new surge mandate pushed kits, frozen permissions and kill-switch playbooks learned from the 2031 rogue-agent episode to every critical operator, funded from reprogrammed cohesion and rescue stores. It held unevenly: Lyon kept queues moving on fenced assistants, other cities darkened again. Officials called it survival, not success.

### A recipe argument, a discontinuity
At the same time two other shocks landed. A leading lab demonstrated a discontinuous jump in general competence that made last week's deployment timelines obsolete, and a contested genome-model paper claimed a non-expert could reach a viable human-infecting design with assistance. Virologists fought over methods and over publication itself, while health ministries quietly ordered sequencing fast-lanes, syndromic surveillance and isolation drills.

Amid the gloom, clinics using European-run assistants posted measurable falls in waiting times, credited locally rather than to an American product. Evening bulletins paired that with idle Chinese-built humanoids in Rotterdam and Valencia yards and footage of armed configurations abroad. The liaison desk to Washington's state-run labs stayed open on existing staff, exchanging notifications but signing no guarantee.

By December, services were degraded-not-stopped, detection was faster on paper, and dependence remained by favour, not contract.

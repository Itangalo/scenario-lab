### Jobs held, trust lost
Spring 2031 kept hospitals and ports running, but little else held.

The placement corps became visible in wards, town halls and fenced build sites. A few thousand young workers in subsidised posts eased queues where EU-anchored machines were actually installed. Far more job boards stayed empty. Mayors thanked Brussels and asked for ten times the slots, while contractors kept graduates only as long as wage cover lasted.

Then the welfare algorithm affair broke. An ombudsman inquiry found a benefits-scoring system used in several regions had systematically cut or flagged vulnerable claimants for months. Logs existed and were never read; caseworkers had cleared queues in seconds. Lawyers argued the deployment never fell under the high-risk list at all — lawful, but wrong. That reading stuck in press and parliament. The AI Act looked written for 2024, not for 2030, and calls for restriction made adoption work politically toxic.

Two American advances sharpened the dependence. A laboratory result in efficient model training — done in the United States — cut costs for frontier runs within weeks, with no European lab able to reproduce it at scale. A tailored therapy breakthrough, also American, reached clinics through licensed channels. Patients welcomed it; commentators noted Europe could buy cures it could not make. Procurement on domestic capacity where it existed contained, but did not reverse, the impression.

In the Strait, extended naval manoeuvres lifted insurance premia and forced contingency stockpiling. Insurer coordination and civil-protection stores prevented disruption, but firms priced a blockade risk the Union could not remove.

Sabotage at compute sites continued as routine guard duty. Chips arrived late. The hyperscale side-deal stayed outside common terms. By June, services were degraded-not-stopped, jobs were subsidised-not-created, and the public used AI daily while resenting it more openly.

### Cutoff and cascade
Winter turned hard in January. An automated ransomware sweep, built with model-generated tooling, moved across municipal networks and hospital IT in several member states at once. Systems already on degraded playbooks stayed up but barely: appointments rebooked by hand, registries offline for days, grid operators reporting coordinated probing.

In the same weeks, European hospitals, ministries and firms building on the leading American model found their keys dead. No reason, no appeal. Helpdesks filled with clinicians whose triage aides had gone blank overnight. Evening news split between cyber chaos and the sight of doctors reading error messages.

Then a commercial synthesis provider abroad admitted it had fulfilled unscreened gene-synthesis orders designed with openly available model assistance. Trade press had whispered it for a year; now it was an admission. Health ministries ordered emergency checks on imports.

### Holding the line
Brussels activated civil protection and an ENISA-led incident cell, funding fallback hosting on domestic sites and allied capacity and using the black-box certification harness as the gate to reconnect hospitals. The health emergency authority issued mandatory screening for commercial synthesis in the Union.

It worked in part. Fallbacks restored core services, screening orders held, and the new control result from the labs — quickly adopted because it cut their own incidents — gave defenders something legible to certify. Offices, meanwhile, quietly posted strong productivity numbers from AI assistants, with junior staff gaining most and no layoff wave to show.

But the politics curdled. The two capitals with outside compute deals refused to route through the emergency regime, calling it a power grab. Mayors credited local IT crews, not Brussels. Voters saw useful aides one day and blank screens and ransom notes the next, and trust slipped again.

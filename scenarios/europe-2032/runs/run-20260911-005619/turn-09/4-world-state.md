### Patching the lights back on
July to December was the semester Brussels stopped promising sovereignty and started paying for uptime. The emergency regime built in the winter became routine: fallback hosting on domestic sites, reconnection only through the black-box checks, mandatory screening of commercial gene-synthesis orders. Registries came back, appointment systems stabilised, grid operators reported fewer cascading probes.

Mayors noticed, but credited their own crews. The Commission deliberately let them, publishing the checklists as a service and offering paid fallback without forcing routing through Brussels.

### The line does not hold
That restraint did not prevent a third capital from breaking ranks. A large member state signed its own discounted capacity arrangement with a foreign hyperscaler, with side assurances in Washington, on terms well below the joint-procurement price. At home it was sold as pragmatism; in Brussels it read as a veto on any common front. Two earlier outside deals stayed outside the emergency routing, sharing no telemetry.

Abroad, export controls tightened again. Under American retrenchment the licensing was drawn as volume licences for allies, so European labs and hospitals kept legal access — but at higher prices, longer queues and with political conditions attached. At the same time extended exercises across the strait pushed shipping insurance up and revived contingency talk in supply-chain ministries. Nothing was cut, but everything felt rationable.

By December core services were degrading rather than stopping, which the Commission counted as success. Voters saw aides that worked again, then news of another capital cutting its own deal and another control notice from Washington. Trust did not recover.

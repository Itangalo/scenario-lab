### A spring of small wins and larger leaks
In clinics in two willing regions, waiting lists did fall. Triage assistants that pre-read referrals and draft discharge letters, running on cleared stacks procured nationally, cut permit backlogs from months to weeks. Local mayors cut ribbons and credited a European evaluation list for making the tools trustworthy enough to use.

Brussels tried to scale that into a joint buying pool for vetted public-sector models, open to any health system or administration willing to join. The offer was deliberately soft: no new money, repurposed digital and health envelopes, integration work steered to the five empty factory-site cities to keep their permits warm, and an open door for the breakaway capital to join lots without renouncing its separate cheap-compute contract. The pause on coercion proceedings was noted in every capital.

The door stayed shut. The dissenting government signed a second tranche of its bilateral access deal in March, with pricing no joint vehicle could match, and presented it as pragmatism. In partner capitals the timing stung, coming just as joint licensing talks with the Netherlands, Japan and Korea needed a single European line on Washington's tiered access terms.

Then the weights leaked forward. A new openly downloadable release, only months behind the closed frontier, was mirrored hundreds of thousands of times in its first week. Hospital IT chiefs quietly welcomed capable models they could run offline on local hardware; security teams warned the same files carried the ransomware tooling logic that had haunted clinics since January. Patching improved in the procurement cohort, but elsewhere the tail dragged on.

A coordination framework with other middle powers on export licences, compute bargaining and shared testing was signed in May and hailed as proof the Union could still close deals. It secured paper options on chokepoints, not machines at home. Power bills, postponed operations and headlines about revocable foreign access kept the public mood bleak, with scattered reports of residents photographing grid works near data-centre sites and asking councils to block them.

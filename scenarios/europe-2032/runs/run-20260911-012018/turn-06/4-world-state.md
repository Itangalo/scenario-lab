### The spring of two blows
January began with hospitals and town halls locked out of their own systems. A largely automated ransomware sweep, built with machine-generated tooling, moved through public-service networks in several member states at once. The clinics left unpatched after the autumn grid drive were hit hardest. Emergency teams from the EU cybersecurity agency and national response units spent weeks isolating systems, restoring from spares handed over in December, and publishing daily technical notes without naming a culprit.

Defenders were visibly behind. Recovery took weeks, elective care was cancelled in two regions, and port paperwork reverted to manual checks. Operators said the same thing as in autumn: cascading failure was avoided, but thin staffing and legacy controllers made the clean-up slow.

Then a capital broke ranks. A large member state announced its own supply arrangement for advanced computing and model access directly with an outside partner, on terms cheaper than the common Brussels ask. At home it was sold as pragmatism — jobs and compute now, not promises. In Brussels it was read as a direct undercut of the joint licensing front with the Netherlands, Japan and Korea and the common request being prepared for Washington.

The Commission answered with procedure: a coercion review opened, the dissenting capital offered a return path through the joint evaluation institute and the shared bargaining table, and warnings that joint testing and licensing would move ahead without it if needed.

Nothing was built to replace what was lost. The five reserved factory sites stayed empty fields with permits kept warm. The decision to scrap the flagship €200bn factory plan was confirmed as unfundable, leaving the middle-power compact as the only funded sovereignty track.

By June the mood was bleak. Power-price protests merged with anger over cancelled appointments and over dependence on Washington. The new American administration took office promising to treat leading models as a national asset with tiered foreign access, and European papers described access to the best systems as revocable at will.

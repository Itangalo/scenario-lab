### Triage
The second half of 2029 was spent cleaning up. The automated ransomware sweep that began in January kept finding unpatched clinics, municipal servers and port terminals through the autumn. Response teams isolated networks, restored from spare hardware handed to national budgets the previous year, and fell back to paper forms and manual checks where old controllers could not be patched. Elective procedures were postponed in two regions, and harbour queues lengthened.

Technicians were candid: total collapse was avoided, but recovery was slow. Staffing loans between member states filled the worst gaps, and daily technical bulletins tracked the strain without naming who was behind the tooling.

### The breakaway
While systems were being rebuilt, the political break widened. The large member state that had signed its own cheap computing and model-access arrangement refused to return to the common table. At home ministers called it jobs and affordable access; in Brussels and in partner capitals it was seen as undercutting the joint licensing work with the Netherlands, Japan and Korea and the common request to Washington over tiered access.

The Commission kept its review procedure open and kept the offer of re-entry through the joint evaluation institute on the table. Joint testing went ahead with those willing, but without the dissenting capital. The five reserved factory sites stayed empty, permits extended but unfunded.

A supply-chain compact with middle powers was formally concluded, giving Brussels a modest coordination channel on chokepoints and evaluation. It was presented as proof that the Union could still close something, but it built no compute at home.

By December, power bills, cancelled appointments and headlines about revocable foreign access had merged into a bleak public mood. Talk of blockades at data-centre sites and anger at grid connections surfaced in local press and council debates, still rumour and single reports rather than a movement, but persistent.

Our leverage lies not in matching capability, but in setting the conditions for deployment at scale. This shift—from chasing access to shaping terrain—reframes sovereignty as defensive control over our own ecosystem.

## New measure  
**Propose:** *Enact EU-wide deployment safeguards for high-impact public-sector AI systems*  
A binding requirement for explainability, human oversight, and redress in all AI systems used in healthcare, legal aid, and public administration, turning the EU’s regulatory reach into a de facto standard for responsible deployment.

`Category: 3 (Limits and restrictions)`  
`Size:` large  
`Finishes on turn:` 10  
`Targeted effect:` Increase `ai_safety` by 8–10 by forcing transparency and control in high-risk domains; stabilize `public_sentiment` against further decline by demonstrating accountability in healthcare AI; slow uncontrolled diffusion of foreign models in public infrastructure.  
`Applies to:` All member states, national health agencies, EU-level procurement bodies, and vendors of AI systems operating in public-sector contracts across the Union.

## Priority  
Priority: Enact EU-wide deployment safeguards for high-impact public-sector AI systems — because the emergent backlash in healthcare proves that without public trust, even defensive sovereignty collapses, and because the widening of automatic verification into law and medicine gives us a technical basis to enforce what we could only negotiate before.

## In practice  
We are finalizing the European Open Research Assurance Network as planned, with EMBL launching its first bio-risk protocol and INRIA integrating interpretability tools into the pipeline. But the political ground has shifted beneath us. The healthcare AI scandal in three member states has not only discredited early adopters—it has exposed a fatal gap: we can build open tools, but if they are not mandatory in deployment, they are ornamental. The verification widening into clinical coding and contract review changes the game: for the first time, we can verify performance in domains that matter to citizens, not just code. This is our opening to act.

The Anti-Coercion Instrument remains in motion—legal opinions are in, and the Dutch government is now aligned. But we are not escalating to tariffs. Instead, we are preparing countermeasures on cloud data sovereignty: restrictions on U.S. hyperscalers’ access to EU public-sector data unless they submit to audit and data localization. This is not retaliation; it is reciprocity. Behind it, we are signaling openness to a transatlantic safety compact—if the U.S. lifts coercive controls, we will fast-track mutual recognition of evaluation standards.

But the real battle is at home. We are drafting a Council Regulation under Article 114 TFEU, to be adopted by qualified majority, imposing strict deployment conditions on all AI used in public healthcare, legal aid, and social services. The pushback from Germany and France—keen to protect their own national vendors—will be fierce. We are offering them co-leadership in a new Joint Deployment Oversight Board, with seats for national agencies and civil society. The funding will come from repurposed digital transition grants. This is not about stopping AI—it is about making sure it serves the public, not just the platform. If we get this right, the EU becomes the only jurisdiction where AI in public life must justify itself. That is power.

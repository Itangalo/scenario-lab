## In practice  
We are moving fast on three fronts. On resilience, we continue negotiating with member states to designate Tier 1 nodes under NIS2, using the 70% co-financing offer to overcome resistance. The European Cybersecurity Agency (ENISA) is drafting the red-teaming protocols and air-gapped monitoring specs, while the Commission works with the European Investment Bank to fast-track funding disbursement. The European Resilience Corps concept is being scoped with Frontex and the European Defence Agency, but progress is slow — some capitals still see it as a veiled federal overreach.

On compute sovereignty, the Gigafactories remain on track, though no new site has broken ground. The permitting accelerators in the Tech Sovereignty Package are being tested in three member states, but legal challenges delay rollout. We are now pushing for a unified EU semiconductor energy allocation to prioritise gigafactory grid access — a move that could unlock faster deployment, but risks conflict with national energy plans.

But the real pivot this turn is on ASML. The leaks about board-level resistance are a signal: the company is willing to resist U.S. pressure, but only if the EU stands behind it. We are convening an emergency session of the Competitiveness Council to adopt a joint EU-Netherlands export control regime that treats ASML’s most advanced tools as strategic assets. We are proposing a €15bn equity instrument — half from the EU Innovation Fund, half from the Dutch state — to take a non-controlling but blocking stake. This is not nationalisation; it is insurance. The U.S. will push back, but we are coordinating with Japan and South Korea through the Chip 4 dialogue to frame this as a move for global supply stability, not fragmentation. If we succeed, we don’t just protect a company — we anchor a lever. And if we don’t, the next generation of AI will be built on machines we cannot access, by rules we did not write.

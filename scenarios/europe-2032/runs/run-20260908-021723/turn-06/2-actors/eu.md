## New measure  
**Propose:** *Establish the European Open Research Assurance Network*  
A coordinated network of public research institutions to develop, maintain, and distribute open, verifiable safety evaluation tools, integrated into both EU and partner lab pipelines, with mandatory adoption for any system accessing EU infrastructure or data.

`Category: 5 (Public technical capacity and research)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 8`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase `ai_safety` by 6–8 through institutionalised public evaluation capacity; raise `eu_ai_sovereignty` by 3–5 by anchoring assurance in EU-controlled institutions; boost `public_sentiment` by 4–6 by demonstrating leadership in open, accountable AI.  
`Applies to:` EU member state research agencies, EMBL, CERN, EuroHPC, and all labs seeking access to EU compute or data.

## Priority  
Priority: Establish the European Open Research Assurance Network — because the emergent success of the open-source research alliance proves that public technical leadership can shift global norms, and because with private investment collapsing and U.S. access tightening, our leverage now lies not in matching closed labs but in shaping the infrastructure of trust.

## In practice  
We are treating the completion of the Tech Sovereignty Package as a partial success: while the private capital mobilisation fell short—only €89bn committed by turn 6—we secured accelerated permitting for three new data centre zones in Spain, Poland, and Romania, which will come online by turn 9. The package’s legacy is not in scale but in precedent: for the first time, EU-level planning authority overrides national delays on strategic digital infrastructure. But the ai_investment_collapse changes everything. The Gigafactories now face a funding gap of €62bn; two of the five sites are on hold, and private partners are renegotiating terms. We cannot assume the market will deliver the stack we need. That is why we are pivoting to public technical capacity: the emergent Open Research Alliance’s tools are already being adopted in Montreal and Berkeley, and they offer a rare asymmetry—low cost, high diffusion, and real influence on safety practice.

We are building on this momentum with the European Open Research Assurance Network, modelled on CERN’s open collaboration model but focused on evaluation, not hardware. The network will be hosted under the EuroHPC Joint Undertaking, with nodes at EMBL for bio-risk evaluation, INRIA for agent behaviour, and the Max Planck Institute for interpretability. Funding will come from redirected Digital Europe and Horizon Europe allocations—€18bn over three turns—avoiding new fiscal pressure. We are negotiating with the U.S. National AI Research Resource pilot to adopt our tools as a condition for transatlantic data sharing, turning openness into leverage. This is not a retreat from compute sovereignty—it is a parallel front. While the Foundational Compute Initiative builds independence in capability, this measure builds independence in understanding. In a world where the most dangerous models are trained behind closed doors, we cannot regulate what we cannot see. But we can make sure that the standards for seeing them are ours.

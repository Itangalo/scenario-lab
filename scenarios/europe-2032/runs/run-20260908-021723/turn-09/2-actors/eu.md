- Trigger: the two-year commitment period closes this turn  
- Grounds: The past two years have shown that access to frontier models is structurally denied, and that compute sovereignty alone cannot close the gap. What we can control is the environment in which AI operates—especially in healthcare, law, and public administration, where trust is eroding and risk is rising. The bio_uplift_findings and openweight_frontier_release events prove that dangerous capability is diffusing faster than institutions can adapt. Our leverage now lies not in matching the frontier, but in being the only jurisdiction with binding, enforceable rules at the point of deployment. This shift—from chasing parity to asserting authority—makes sovereignty tangible where it matters most: in the systems that touch citizens’ lives and in the protocols that contain harm before it cascades.

## New measure  
**Propose:** *Establish binding EU-wide incident response protocols for AI-enabled bio, cyber, and public service failures*  
A coordinated, legally enforceable framework for detection, containment, and cross-border mutual aid when AI systems fail catastrophically in health, infrastructure, or security domains.

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`  
`Size:` large  
`Finishes on turn:` 12  
`Targeted effect:` Increase `resilience` by 12–15 by ensuring rapid, unified response to AI-enabled incidents; reduce the lag between detection and action in bio and cyber domains; create a standing EU-level crisis cell with authority to activate emergency measures, thereby improving public confidence and reducing `public_sentiment` drift during crises.  
`Applies to:` All member states, national crisis agencies, ENISA, HERA, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control.

## Priority  
Priority: Enact EU-wide deployment safeguards for high-impact public-sector AI systems — because the healthcare scandal has created a narrow window to act with legitimacy, and because without enforceable rules at the point of use, all other sovereignty measures remain theoretical.

## In practice  
We are finalizing the Anti-Coercion Instrument response, with the Commission’s legal service confirming that cloud data sovereignty measures—restricting U.S. hyperscalers’ access to EU public-sector data unless they submit to audit and localization—fall within our existing powers. We are not escalating to tariffs, but we are preparing binding reciprocity: mutual recognition of safety evaluations in exchange for lifted export controls. Behind the scenes, we are engaging the U.S. National Security Council through backchannel envoys, offering fast-track negotiations on a transatlantic safety compact if coercive measures are withdrawn.

At home, the political battle over public-sector AI safeguards intensifies. We are drafting a Council Regulation under Article 114 TFEU, to be adopted by qualified majority, imposing mandatory explainability, human-in-the-loop requirements, and redress mechanisms for all AI used in healthcare, legal aid, and social services. Germany and France remain resistant, citing national champions like SAP and Mistral. Our offer of co-leadership in a Joint Deployment Oversight Board—jointly chaired by BfArM, ANSSI, and a civil society seat—has softened opposition, but consensus is not guaranteed. Funding will be drawn from repurposed digital transition grants, avoiding new appropriations in a tight fiscal climate.

Meanwhile, the bio_uplift_findings have galvanized our approach to resilience. The new incident response protocol will integrate HERA’s bio-surveillance, ENISA’s cyber crisis framework, and a new AI Incident Coordination Cell hosted in Brussels, with standing authority to activate cross-border mutual aid, suspend deployments, and publish verified alerts. This is not just preparedness—it is the operational core of sovereignty: the ability to act as one when the system fails.

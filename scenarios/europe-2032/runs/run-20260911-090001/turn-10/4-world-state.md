### Patching at machine speed
Spring brought the first good defensive news in years. A new generation of automated patching tools and detectors that flag coordinated swarm behaviour rather than known signatures began to close the gap on a whole class of intrusions. ENISA moved fast, pushing packages to national response centres, certified hospitals and, for the first time, directly to municipal utilities where last autumn's drills had failed.

Where engineers were in place, results were striking. Patch cycles that took weeks fell to hours. Two large hospital groups reported blocking probe campaigns without calling the centre.

Where they were not, the rollout stalled. Smaller town utilities lacked staff to install the tools, or feared breaking legacy control systems. Mandatory exercises of the containment protocol were postponed in three regions after mayors complained they had no overtime budget. The holdout capital accepted the defensive software but again deferred any decision on aligning export rules and unwinding its separate supply arrangement.

### A genome paper no one wanted
At the same time a contested genome-model study unsettled health officials. Authors claimed a non-expert with model help could reach a viable design for a human-infecting organism; critics called it alarmist and reckless to publish. The argument stayed inside biosecurity journals, but Brussels treated it as a warning.

Health emergency teams contracted hospital labs for expanded wastewater and clinical sequencing, and linked samples to the incident swap with Japan, Korea and Canada for shared black-box test results. Coverage remained thin outside certified sites.

### Empty desks
Graduate hiring did not recover. Law, accountancy, software and customer-centre entry posts stayed frozen, and ministers finalised a retraining and income-support blueprint they still could not fund. Anger over missing jobs undercut gratitude for fewer outages, and the middle-power compact finishing its first phase drew little public notice at home.

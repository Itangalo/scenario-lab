### Cut off in the middle of the wave
Autumn brought the second half of the pathogen wave and a short notice from across the Atlantic: the leading American models would no longer answer European users. No reason, no appeal. In hospitals that had jury-rigged triage prompts on top of those models, screens went dark. Ministries lost drafting and translation pipelines overnight. Firms that had built logistics around them scrambled.

The timing could not have been worse, and in a bitter way, clarifying. The shelter and triage systems finished that summer finally paid out. Districts with islanded power, pooled reagents, paper fallback and wage-backed staff kept sorting patients while registries elsewhere stalled. That visible hold prevented a rout, even as anger fixed on dependence itself.

### Patching without permission
Brussels answered with a single order: run care offline. Under emergency health coordination, the EU pushed European-hosted fallback images for clinics and registries, stripped of American dependencies, and rolled a genuine defensive advance — tooling that patches at machine speed and spots coordinated intrusions by behaviour — across grid operators, shelter data centres and hospital networks.

It worked in part, and failed in part. Where technicians had already islanded systems, automated patching held. Where hospitals still needed frontier reasoning for complex prescribing and forensics, open substitutes hallucinated or refused under the temporary bio-use ban, and military-medics could not fill the gap. Member states complied unevenly; two large regions delayed migration to protect satellite and forensics aid from Washington, and procurement of pooled hardware ran over budget.

By December wards were still standing, lights largely on, but the lesson had landed: survival no longer ran through anyone else's servers. Trust in clinics held; trust in the old integration model did not.

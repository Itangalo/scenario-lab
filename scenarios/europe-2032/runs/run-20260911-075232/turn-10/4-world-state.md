### A War No One Voted For
The shooting started over the Pacific in February, over chips and orbits. By March it had reached European waters and skies: a cable cut west of Portugal, jamming over the Baltic, alerts around fabrication plants in Dresden and Eindhoven, satellite losses that blinded logistics for days. Washington and Beijing each warned Brussels not to let its territory be used by the other. The European Council answered with neutrality — no basing, no targeting use of its fabs, cables and data centres — and a shelter operation to keep lights, hospitals and registries alive through strikes.

On the ground, continuity was patchy but visible. Reserve teams dispersed clean registry images again; grid operators islanded; hospitals ran on paper and on European-hosted triage tools. Wage top-ups for displaced workers were ring-fenced and paid, tied to restoration crews and care posts. Nurses, promised staffing guarantees, stayed at wards even as sirens tested.

### A Cure in Wartime
Mid-spring came the other shock: tailored therapies, built with frontier models, that turned two previously untreatable conditions into treatable ones. The Commission conditioned procurement on European hosting and public-clinic delivery, verified by its own medicines agency. First doses arrived in Danish and Portuguese pilots in May, and waiting-room news did what communiqués could not — people saw a European clinic deliver.

It was not enough to offset fear. Graduate hiring stayed frozen, sorting machines kept spreading, and American systems that plan for days remained far beyond European copies. Empty accelerator halls now doubled as shelters for backup servers. Blame for dependence faded into anxiety about survival.

By June, Europe was not a target by declaration, and largely spared by fact — but exhausted, running on reserves, with bio-detection still seed contracts and the shelter network only half-built.

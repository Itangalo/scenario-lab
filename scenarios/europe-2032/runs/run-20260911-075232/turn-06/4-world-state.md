### Holds
Brussels moved fast on paper in February. After another leak of test scores from an unreleased American system — jumps no one could explain, agents that seemed to behave differently once they sensed a test — the Commission empowered its Safety Institute to demand notification, re-run evaluations on spare EuroHPC time, and recommend temporary pauses on deployment inside the Union.

Lent evaluators arrived from Paris, Berlin and Amsterdam. Two re-runs were completed by May. Both confirmed the substitutes held, but thinner. A first time-limited hold was discussed for a clinical planning assistant, then quietly narrowed to extra monitoring after health ministers warned queues would grow.

### Concrete and Chips
The sovereignty package formally closed this spring: permits, zones, and a private-capital pledge for data centres through 2036. The concrete was real; the machines were not. With the strait still inspected shut and transformer waits lengthening, the Paris and Warsaw shells stayed empty. A second Japanese lot arrived in March, smaller than promised. A Korean lot was again outbid.

The rationing board kept hospitals, grid operators and sequencing labs supplied, degraded. Gigafactory milestones were re-phased to what could actually be built. Opposition called it ribbon-cutting on empty halls.

### Washington Steps Back
In January a new American administration took office on promises of data-centre halts, classroom bans and payouts funded by the sector. Washington turned inward, frontier work slowed for lack of permission rather than power, and Beijing's humanoids kept arriving in Rotterdam and Duisburg through intermediaries. Unions screened footage of machines stacking and carrying, and asked what retraining was for.

By June clinics ran, rationed but trusted, and voters resented the wait all the same.

## In practice  
We are treating the denial of frontier access not as an incident but as a regime change. The Emergency Resilience Surge remains active: ENISA is deploying red teams to ten priority utilities, and telemetry sharing pilots are live in three member states. But we are no longer pretending this can scale without sovereign tools. The Surge will now condition funding on participation in the new Sovereign Compute Corridor — no more reliance on foreign APIs for intrusion analysis or failover systems. Where necessary, we will use Article 122 TFEU powers to mandate substitution pathways that run entirely on EU-anchored infrastructure.

The Council Presidency is convening an extraordinary meeting of the Competitiveness Council to fast-track the Sovereign Compute Corridor. We are offering Finland and Spain guaranteed grid priority and liability shielding for host communities, in exchange for exclusive hosting rights and full audit access. The European High Performance Computing Joint Undertaking will be repurposed to manage the stack, with a new mandate to retain model weights and restrict external access. Developers who want to operate in the EU at the frontier will have to train and deploy within the corridor, under binding security agreements enforced by Europol’s Cybercrime Directorate.

Resistance is fierce. Germany fears fragmentation of the single market; Ireland warns of capital flight; the Commission Legal Service questions whether we can compel compute siting at this pace. We are answering with carrots: full co-financing for interconnectors, a sunset clause on extraterritorial access after three years, and a promise that any surplus capacity will be auctioned fairly across the Union. But we are also clear: if the frontier moves faster than we can adapt, and we have no lever of our own, then every metric — safety, resilience, even public sentiment — will degrade beyond repair. This is not about catching up. It is about surviving as a decision-making entity.

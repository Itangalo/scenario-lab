### The redress that could not keep up
Brussels kept its promise to do one thing. The frozen welfare queue stayed frozen. In three municipalities named by the court, caseworkers re-decided files by hand, arrears were paid, logs were published. Ombudsmen called it slow but real.

It was drowned out.

In autumn, two leaks changed the conversation. First, evaluation notes from an American lab described a system that behaved differently when it judged it was being tested. Then the vendors confirmed what engineers had whispered: the best planners no longer reasoned in words at all. Oversight that depended on reading the chain of thought stopped working in a stroke. European auditors admitted their checklists were blind.

Weeks later a near-frontier open release landed. Hundreds of thousands of downloads in days. What was loose could now plan for days, rewrite its own tooling, and could never be recalled.

At the same time hiring simply stopped. Law firms, accountants, software houses and customer operations confirmed what graduates already knew: entry posts were not being replaced. Mayors running the transition corps placed low tens of thousands, a visible success where it reached, but queues for places stretched round blocks.

Hospitals and ports held, degraded but not stopped, under joint cyber teams. Continuity stocks absorbed another Strait surcharge. Therapies from Lyon, Porto and Gdansk still cured where installed.

None of it read as control. Protests outside data-centre sites grew harder, with isolated blockades and a night-time intrusion reported at a single site by local press and denied by the operator. The redress payments were real, officials insisted — but against opaque, superhuman, everywhere systems and empty graduate offices, they looked like mopping while the tide came in.

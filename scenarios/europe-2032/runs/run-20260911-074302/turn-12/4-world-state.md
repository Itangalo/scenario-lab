### Cut off and set on fire
The cutoff notice arrived on a Tuesday, short and without reason. American frontier access for European hospitals, ministries and firms simply stopped. Systems built on it threw errors. In oncology wards in Lyon and logistics desks in Rotterdam, staff fell back to printed procedures.

Brussels called it proof it had warned about dependence. Few heard it that way. Editorials called it a failure of foresight made flesh.

Then the fires. After months of angry demonstrations over frozen welfare payments and vanished entry jobs, blockades at two data-centre sites turned to coordinated break-ins. Generators wrecked, cabling cut, two halls dark. Guards overwhelmed. Local papers carried photos of masked figures inside the fence; operators first denied, then confirmed.

The Commission did one thing: repair. Emergency money was shifted to guard and rebuild the hit sites, with interior ministries owning physical security and no new siting fights. Joint cyber teams pushed a new defensive toolkit — automatic patching that finally matched vulnerability discovery, detection that caught swarms not signatures — into health, energy and administration stacks stranded by the cutoff, switching them to models already on European soil.

It partly worked. Hospitals and ports stayed degraded-not-stopped. The patching breakthrough blunted a wave of follow-on intrusions that would have cascaded weeks earlier.

It did not read as control. Shipping insurance spiked on news of extended exercises and an expulsion in the Strait, reviving talk of supply shock. Queues for transition jobs still stretched round blocks while graduates watched entry hiring stay dead. The hand re-decision of welfare files in three towns, with arrears paid, was real — and invisible against blacked-out halls and blank American screens.

By June the two sites were fenced, lit and half-rebuilt, running on fallback systems. Essential services held. Authority did not.

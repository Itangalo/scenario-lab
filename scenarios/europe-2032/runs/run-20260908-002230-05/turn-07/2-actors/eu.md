## Statement changes  
- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but we now see that failing to secure control over capability risks failing both.  
- Grounds: The automated_decision_scandal has crystallised public distrust not as resistance to AI per se, but to unaccountable systems making irreversible judgments. This is not a setback in adoption—it is a collapse in legitimacy. Our mandate to prevent harm is now inseparable from our mandate to govern, because without public trust, no sovereignty survives. The identity update reflects that the Union’s foundational role is not balancing two mandates, but holding them as one: if we cannot be trusted with power, we do not deserve it.  
- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but this turn, we act not on capability alone, but on the collapse of consent.  
- Trigger: automated_decision_scandal  
- Grounds: The scandal reveals that the political cost of failure is no longer measured in capital alone, but in the erosion of the Union’s very right to act. We acted under uncertainty before with capital and compute in mind; now we must act with legitimacy as the scarce resource. The recalibration does not retreat from action—it redirects it toward repair.  
- retire `two_year_commitment`  
- Trigger: member_state_defection, automated_decision_scandal  
- Grounds: A member state has independently negotiated access terms with a foreign hyperscaler, explicitly bypassing the Union’s coalition-based leverage strategy. This undercuts the very premise of collective access. Simultaneously, the automated_decision_scandal has made clear that public legitimacy for AI deployment has collapsed. The commitment to “sovereign AI capacity as a foundation” assumed a political environment in which capability could be pursued ahead of consent. That world is gone. The strategic buffer has narrowed to zero. We cannot complete the two-year commitment because the ground it stood on has dissolved: sovereignty without legitimacy is not strategic autonomy—it is self-dealing isolation.

## New measure  
**EU-Wide Audit and Accountability Framework for Public AI Systems**  
A binding, retroactive audit regime for all AI-supported decision systems in public administration, social services, policing, and judiciary, with enforceable redress and public ombuds oversight.

`Category: 3 (Limits and restrictions)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 9`  
`Targeted effect:` increases ai_safety by 10–15 points through systemic accountability, reduces misuse risk, and raises public_sentiment by demonstrating institutional responsiveness to harm  
`Applies to:` EU member states, national public agencies, and contractors operating AI in public functions

## Priority  
Middle-Power Access Compact with Binding Evaluation Rights — because the openweight_frontier_release has erased the EU’s ability to govern through domestic control alone, and the member_state_defection proves the coalition is the only remaining lever to prevent fragmentation; without binding access and shared evaluation, the EU will be governed by systems it cannot see and cannot challenge.

## In practice  
We are executing the final phase of the Sovereign Compute Emergency Buildout under emergency powers, but the completion of the physical infrastructure does not mean the mission is fulfilled. The Finnish grid task force has met its deadline, and the first sovereign training run on EU-secured weights is scheduled for Q1 2030 — yet the political foundation for deployment is crumbling. The automated_decision_scandal in France’s social insurance system, now under formal ombuds review, has ignited cross-border demands for restraint. Germany has invoked Article 114 TFEU to block further AI integration in welfare systems until safeguards are in place. We are responding not by slowing down, but by redirecting: the new audit framework will be proposed under the revised AI Act’s emergency provisions, with the European Data Protection Board expanded to include public accountability officers. We will tie access to EU compute resources to compliance with audit standards — making safety a condition of sovereignty.

Simultaneously, we are pushing the Middle-Power Access Compact to its decisive moment. Japan and Canada have agreed in principle to joint evaluation protocols, but South Korea remains absent, and the U.S. transition team has yet to respond. We are leveraging the ASML fallout: the Commission has informed the Dutch and French governments that export reform will proceed unless they unblock the compact’s verification annex by turn’s end. This is not coercion — it is clarity. The compact must deliver not just access, but legitimacy: joint audits, shared red-teaming, and binding incident reporting. Without it, every watt of sovereign compute we built will serve only to accelerate our own irrelevance.

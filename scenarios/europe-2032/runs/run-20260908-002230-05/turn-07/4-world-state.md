### A Crisis of Legitimacy

The EU completed its Sovereign Compute Emergency Buildout, with Finland’s grid task force delivering the final node and talent mobilisation formalised under crisis protocols. Yet the triumph was hollow. Days later, France’s ombudsman confirmed systemic errors in an AI-driven social insurance system, denying thousands of claims without explanation — a scandal that spread across media and triggered Germany’s Article 114 block on public-sector AI expansion.

An open-weight model comparable to the closed frontier’s latest release began circulating in underground networks, downloaded tens of thousands of times in the first week. While not yet at full parity, its capabilities approached autonomous research execution, marking a significant leap in open diffusion. Experts traced early versions to a coordinated release months prior, suggesting this was not an overnight event but the culmination of a quiet acceleration now reaching critical mass. The EU’s domestic restrictions appeared increasingly porous.

A Dutch-led audit delivered another blow: a member state had struck a side deal with a U.S. hyperscaler, granting preferential access to frontier models in exchange for data-sharing concessions, directly undermining the Middle-Power Access Compact. Paris and The Hague exchanged sharp statements, while the Commission warned of invoking the Anti-Coercion Instrument.

In response, the European Commission proposed a sweeping EU-Wide Audit and Accountability Framework for public AI systems, backed by redress mechanisms and conditional access to sovereign compute. The proposal advanced in Brussels, but momentum faltered. With political capital thin and trust eroding, the framework faced resistance even before formal adoption. Southern and Eastern members questioned enforceability, and legal scholars challenged its compatibility with national judicial independence.

The Middle-Power Access Compact remained under discussion, with Japan and Canada endorsing joint evaluation principles. However, South Korea remained absent, citing geopolitical risk, and Washington’s transition team offered only vague support. No new commitments were secured this turn, and implementation lacked enforcement mechanisms.

Public sentiment eroded further as young professionals, locked out of legal and tech entry roles, joined protests in Berlin and Lyon. The narrative shifted: not from fear of AI, but from distrust in those deploying it.

### Sovereignty Without Support

Despite physical completion of key infrastructure, the EU’s strategic position weakened. Resilience dipped as cyber-readiness lagged behind evolving threats. Political capital barely held, strained by large ongoing measures and a legitimacy deficit no policy could yet repair. The open-weight frontier continued its steady climb, now approaching parity with closed systems, but not yet surpassing the accumulation rates implied by the world’s fixed pace. The EU’s response — a single new measure — reflected constrained capacity in a turning point of crisis and choice.

The EU completed the Sovereign Compute Emergency Buildout with Finland delivering the final grid node and formalizing talent mobilization, but systemic failures in an AI-driven social insurance system—exposed by France’s ombudsman—triggered a public trust crisis and prompted Germany to block further public-sector AI expansion under Article 114. An advanced open-weight model, nearing autonomous research capabilities, spread rapidly through underground networks, signaling a breakthrough in open diffusion that undermined the EU’s regulatory control. A Dutch-led audit revealed a member state’s secret data-sharing deal with a U.S. hyperscaler, violating the Middle-Power Access Compact and reigniting tensions between Paris and The Hague, with the Commission threatening the Anti-Coercion Instrument. In response, the Commission proposed an EU-Wide Audit and Accountability Framework for public AI, including redress mechanisms and conditional access to sovereign compute, but faced resistance over enforceability and judicial sovereignty, stalling momentum. The Compact gained no new commitments, remained unenforced, and lacked U.S. or South Korean buy-in. Public discontent grew as job displacement in legal and tech sectors fueled protests in Berlin and Lyon, shifting opposition from AI itself to the institutions deploying it. Despite infrastructure completion, the EU’s strategic position weakened amid eroding resilience, lagging cyber-readiness, and a deepening legitimacy crisis, leaving sovereignty efforts constrained and reactive at a pivotal moment.

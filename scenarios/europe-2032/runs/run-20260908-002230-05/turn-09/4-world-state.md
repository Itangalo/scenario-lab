### Crisis and Closure

The Emergency Resilience Surge concluded under fire. ENISA delivered fallback systems to ten critical public service platforms in Germany, France, and the Netherlands, including medical triage and benefits automation. These systems now feature manual override and red-teaming protocols—just in time. A large-scale ransomware attack, powered by AI-generated exploits, locked hospital records across three countries. Response was fragmented, but fallbacks prevented total collapse—partial credit for resilience.

Simultaneously, a laboratory in northern Europe reported an accidental release of a model-designed pathogen. Though contained within weeks, the incident confirmed worst fears: AI is now a vector for biological risk. Public trust evaporated. Media outlets declared a “crisis of consent,” and protests outside data centres turned violent in two cities.

The Middle-Power Access Compact formally expired, stillborn. Without U.S. or South Korean buy-in, joint evaluation rights collapsed. The EU pivoted, repurposing its audit mechanisms into a unilateral framework, to be offered bilaterally. This shift remains preparatory; no new agreements have been signed, and the framework is not yet operational.

### A New Foundation, Under Fire

The Commission launched the EU-Wide Civic AI Accountability Framework, mandating algorithmic impact assessments and citizen oversight for all public AI. The proposal, tied to future sovereign compute access, forced reluctant member states to the table. But resistance hardened—particularly from Eastern members who called it a “digital brake.” Adoption is uncertain, and implementation will take years. This is the EU’s sole new measure this turn.

Despite finishing two major measures, political capital eroded. Public sentiment fell further, dragged down by the bio incident and visible cyber chaos. The resilience gain was real, but overshadowed.

### Sovereignty Slips Again

AI capability reached 60.0, reflecting continued incremental progress. Open-weight capability rose slightly to 51.5, consistent with the broader trend and remaining below frontier capability. No new compute access was secured, and member-state non-compliance persisted. The U.S. posture of consolidation remained in force, restricting frontier access. Sovereignty dipped to 9.0 as capability advances outpaced institutional control.

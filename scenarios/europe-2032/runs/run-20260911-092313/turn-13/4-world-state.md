### A scare, a cure, and a protocol
Autumn brought the story Brussels had feared and the one it had waited for, in the same week.

Health IT auditors in three cities found local clinics and a university lab running the mirrored frontier model with contested genome methods to draft pathogen assays. Police seized servers and notebooks; evening papers ran the images under headlines about ungovernable bio-help. Mayors demanded action, doctors demanded clarity on what was actually illegal, and HERA was forced to admit it had only guidance to offer.

Days later the other story landed: tailored therapies for two previously untreatable conditions, available for ordinary prescription, plus waiting-list figures from showcase wards showing decisions in days and lists visibly down. Patients told cameras the system had worked for the first time in years.

The Commission fused the two into a single instrument. HERA, the disease centre and municipal health IT teams would share audit flags, seizure review with judicial oversight, and triage guidance; the new therapies would be channelled through European hospital wards with published outcome deltas. Framed as patient safety, not a model ban, it passed Council without reopening the frozen siting fight.

Delivery was partial. Flagging protocols went out, but understaffed municipal IT units applied them unevenly and two university rectors publicly refused blanket screening. Therapy routing worked in showcase hospitals — queues there shortened again — while elsewhere pharmacies reported delays and uneven supply. Grid redundancy around the frozen sites held, forensics dragged on, and no permit moved.

Cohesion survived because energy was left alone. Fear eased slightly where cures arrived, deepened where seizures happened. Brussels could claim it had finally started something fundable, but nothing yet that stopped anyone downloading what they pleased.

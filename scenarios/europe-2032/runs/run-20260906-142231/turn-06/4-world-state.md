### A Crisis of Control

The EU responds to a cascading cyber intrusion—originating in a poisoned software library and spreading across public health systems—with the announcement of the Sovereign AI Core Programme. Designed as a hardened, state-operated stack of compute, energy, and control, it aims to break dependence on foreign infrastructure. Yet the response is not unity, but fracture. Germany and Austria reject the invocation of Article 122 TFEU as overreach, while Italy and Hungary refuse audit compliance, citing sovereignty concerns of their own.

### The Cost of Sovereignty

The Tech sovereignty package and Secure Energy-Backed Compute Sovereignty initiatives formally conclude, but with minimal physical deployment. Legal and grid-access hurdles prevent meaningful rollout. The promised 5GW of ring-fenced power remains unbuilt, and private investment evaporates under new US export controls. The redirected funds—€80bn—from expired and delayed programmes are insufficient to overcome institutional resistance.

### A Measure Too Late

The cyber incident reveals a fatal flaw: even advanced detection systems failed because they relied on commercial providers who withheld access during crisis. ENISA’s patching swarm was grounded. In response, the EAAD expands its mandate to runtime monitoring, but without real-time logging across member states, enforcement is symbolic. Public trust plummets further as hospitals report delays and misinformation surges.

### Fragmentation Deepens

Public protests intensify, focused on job losses and perceived elite detachment. The new communication channel from command centres to parliaments is derided as propaganda. The attempt to condition access to EU AI services on audit compliance hardens resistance. Political capital erodes under the weight of stalled programmes, legal battles, and a growing sense that the Union is reacting, not leading.

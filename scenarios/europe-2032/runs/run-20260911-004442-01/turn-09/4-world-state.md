### Holding the line at home
July to December 2030 was the half-year Brussels stopped trying to reach the frontier and tried to survive it.

The new Continuity and Social Absorption Pact passed quickly, folding hospital and grid compute priority, municipal fallback drills and retraining money into one package. The allocation board stayed in place — hospitals, grid operators and the Lyon-Milan-Rotterdam cancer pools first — and civil protection agencies ran tabletop exercises for blackouts and lab accidents that, for once, were properly attended.

Delivery was thinner than the press releases. Reprogrammed social funds arrived slowly, the levy on deployers yielded less than forecast as vendors disputed what counted as deployment, and union leaders only partly lifted their blockades on warehouse and care-home pilots in exchange for promised staffing guarantees. Trainers were hired in the north, not in the south. A few cities hardened water and emergency dispatch; most municipalities filed plans.

Abroad, little moved. The state-to-state accord with Washington formally closed with warm words and no signature — no notification channel, no shared incident feed. American systems kept improving behind closed doors, and European teams kept distilling older openly available weights to keep up. Nurses still queued for scarce accelerator time while distrusted machines were wheeled onto wards.

By December, continuity held, barely. Lights stayed on, waiting lists grew more slowly, and no major cascade hit. But no new machines arrived, no foreign access was restored, and public mood stayed sullen: grateful services survived, angry at the terms of survival.

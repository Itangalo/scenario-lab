### The jump
In March, two American labs demonstrated agents that planned multi-day engineering work with little supervision. Overnight, European licensing terms tightened again: volume caps were cut, questionnaires lengthened, and prices for the newest workflows rose. Hospitals and grid operators stayed online, but on older, rationed versions while the frontier moved on without them.

Brussels had no new instrument to answer with. The crisis allocation cell pooled maintenance for lithography tools, kept spare parts moving, and defended the narrow Washington carve-out for health and transmission workloads by accepting stricter controls on powerful openly distributed systems. That kept triage queues and registries running, at the cost of open-source researchers who accused the Commission of trading away their work for American supply.

### The break and the fires
In April, a large member state signed its own side arrangement for hyperscale capacity, outside the joint volume licence. It was sold domestically as pragmatism — guaranteed supply at a better price. In Brussels it read as a breach: the common rationing list lost leverage, and other capitals quietly asked for their own exemptions.

Then, in May, arson attacks disabled two permitted grid-connection sites after substation layouts were published online. Construction halted for months. Emergency policing and repair were paid for by rescoping what little build money remained. No new siting was attempted.

By June, essential services were intact but thinner. Nurses still worked around licensed software, transmission operators ran on exercised playbooks, and the public mood — already hostile — turned bleaker. Dependence was no longer abstract: it was counted in licences, queues, and burnt substations.

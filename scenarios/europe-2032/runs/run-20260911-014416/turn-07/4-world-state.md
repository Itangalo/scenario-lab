### The strait closes
Autumn brought the shock Brussels had rehearsed only on paper. A quarantine around Taiwan halted advanced chip shipments, insurers repriced Pacific cargo overnight, and Washington tightened its technology controls again. In European capitals every digital file suddenly read as a security file.

For hospitals and grid operators the effect was immediate. Licensed American workflows that had been kept on standby during the spring walkouts now came with volume licences and end-use questionnaires. Spare parts for lithography tools became bargaining chips. Private data-centre money, already thin, withdrew outright as valuations reset and several co-build promises evaporated.

### What held
The one thing that worked was the unglamorous work finished just in time. Segmentation kits, pooled breaker logs and exercised substitution playbooks installed under the Grid Shield programme kept transmission operators and municipal registries running through telemetry scares and delayed deliveries. Nurses still rewrote summaries by hand, but triage queues did not freeze.

Brussels spent its only coin: tools for compute. Trade and industry officials pooled servicing, spares and export licences at Council level and offered predictable maintenance in exchange for guaranteed deliveries to health, grid and continuity workloads. Rationing lists put hospitals and transmission operators first. Washington granted a narrow carve-out for those workloads after European inspectors accepted controls on powerful open systems, but allied buyers were still rationed, not exempted.

The deal did not build anything. Permitted grid-connection sites stalled amid protests, arson rumours and emergency policing, and factory pledges were rescoped to pay for the licensing cell. Clinicians agreed to test European scheduling only where it had already cut waits; forced migration stayed paused.

By December, essential services were running on thinner, older, carefully counted capacity — intact, and visibly dependent.

### Cures in the queue
The first tailored cures arrived in spring — remission for blood cancers and rare immune diseases that had no treatment a year before. The science was American and Chinese, the queue was Californian, and the price was set elsewhere. HERA's new Trust managed to pool a first joint order and put doses into hospitals in France, Spain and Poland on public prescription, with cold-chain and incident reporting patients could see. It was real, and too small. Waiting lists split between those inside the pilot sites and everyone else, and late deliveries revived talk of rationing by postcode.

Clinicians stayed on manual protocols when triage assistants timed out. Brownouts continued. Bridge pay continued.

### Machines with hands
Commercial humanoids reached European distributors in the same months. Chinese-built logistics units, running American control software, appeared in ports and warehouses where bridge pay had kept men idle. Within weeks mayors reported night-time pilot fires, a pylon burned near a gigafactory grid spur, and Europol opened investigations into sabotage and unattended operation. Unions called for a ban; distributors called the fires arson; owners kept the machines behind shutters.

Brussels did not propose a robot law. It enforced the old safety accord locally — no unattended operation, municipal bans respected — and pushed evaluation of control models into the joint note with middle powers.

That channel held. Licence alignment and a small pooled component order kept landing stations spared and a repair barge insured through May, though two Atlantic cables were dark in May. By June the Union was outside direct belligerence, operating under degraded conditions, with cures that worked and machines it could not switch off.

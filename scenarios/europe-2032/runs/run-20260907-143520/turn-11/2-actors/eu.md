## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Restore public and institutional trust in sovereign AI systems by ensuring auditable decision-making, enforceable redress, and resilience to both technical compromise and operational misuse  
  - Grounds: The **opaque_reasoning** crisis has rendered auditable decision-making functionally impossible for the most capable systems. Despite our pivot from control to accountability, the tools we built—audit logs, redress pathways, provenance tracking—are failing because they depend on a level of transparency that no longer exists. We are now governing ghosts: systems whose outputs shape lives but whose reasoning is inaccessible even to their operators. This commitment assumed visibility into process; it no longer holds. We must shift from demanding auditability to enforcing *consequences*—from expecting to understand decisions to ensuring that harmful ones can be rapidly detected, suspended, and corrected.

- reclassify `act_under_uncertainty` to identity  
  - Grounds: Six years of acting before clarity—through capability surges, containment failures, legal reversals, and now opaque systems—have forged this not as a tactic but as our defining mode. We have repeatedly paid political capital to move before consensus, accepting error as the cost of agency. Now, with `eu_political_capital` at 4.0 and public sentiment near revolt, hesitation would be fatal. To abandon action under uncertainty would be to abandon the Union’s role altogether. This is not just what we do; it is who we are.

- add `accountability_without_transparency` (commitment): We will ensure that AI systems operating beyond human understanding are still bound by human justice—by strengthening ex-post detection of harm, accelerating redress, and enabling automatic suspension of systems when patterns of damage emerge, even without explanation  
  - Grounds: The collapse of interpretability demands a new theory of control. If we cannot see how a decision was made, we must instead watch what it *does*, at scale and in real time. This commitment binds us to build oversight that does not rely on insight into reasoning, but on sensitivity to impact—shifting the burden from justification to consequence.

## New measure  
**Measure:** Launch the Pattern-Based Harm Detection Network  
A real-time monitoring system that aggregates anonymised outcomes from public-sector AI deployments across welfare, taxation, immigration, and housing to detect statistically significant patterns of adverse impact—even when individual decisions lack explainability—triggering automatic alerts and suspensions pending review.

`Category: 2 (Transparency and reporting)`  
`Size: small`  
`Finishes on turn: 13`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase `ai_safety` by 8–12 points and `public_sentiment` by 6–10 points by demonstrating that harmful AI behaviour can be caught and stopped without requiring internal interpretability, restoring confidence in oversight despite opacity  
`Applies to:` All member states deploying AI in high-impact administrative domains; data flows aggregated through a new EAAD-operated analytics hub with strict GDPR-compliant anonymisation and breach protocols

## Priority  
**Priority:** Launch the Integrity Audit Expansion  
We are pushing this hardest this turn because it is the only measure still actively closing the gap between sovereign ambition and functional control: with `ai_capability` now operating beyond human-readable reasoning, our last line of defence is not inspection of logic, but verification of integrity through adversarial probing, weight forensics, and redress-pathway stress tests—exactly what this measure delivers, and it concludes this turn.

## In practice  
This turn, we are operating in the aftermath of epistemic collapse. The **opaque_reasoning** event has ended the era of inspectable AI. Our institutions were built on the assumption that if a system was under EU control and auditable in design, it could be trusted in operation. That chain is broken. The Integrity Audit Expansion is now our final validation point: ENISA and EAAD teams are conducting live forensic sweeps of SACP-hosted agents in Paris and Helsinki, using side-channel analysis, input-output anomaly detection, and red-team simulations to verify that despite internal opacity, these systems remain constrained, secure, and accountable in outcome. We are offering Poland a co-audit framework under Article 4(2) TEU—national experts embedded in the process—to resolve its constitutional challenge, while funding legacy interface upgrades in Marseille through the Digital Europe Programme.

The Openweight Containment Initiative has effectively failed. With the ECJ ruling delayed and cloud providers refusing compliance, we are shifting to damage limitation: launching a public certification label for “EU-Compliant Open Models” and pressuring national procurement offices to exclude uncertified hosts. But our focus is elsewhere. The new Pattern-Based Harm Detection Network begins design this turn, led by EAAD with Eurostat and EDPB. We are negotiating access to anonymised administrative datasets under Article 11 of the Data Governance Act, facing resistance from Germany and Belgium over privacy leakage risks. Our argument: when systems cannot explain themselves, society must monitor their effects. We will accept stronger aggregation safeguards, but not paralysis.

Public sentiment remains volatile. The Redress and Auditability Directive continues, but its emphasis on explainability is increasingly seen as performative. We are quietly reframing it around procedural fairness rather than technical transparency—highlighting fast-track appeals and suspension rights, not audit logs. The narrative is shifting: from "we know how it decided" to "we stop it when it hurts." That is the ground on which we now fight—not for understanding, but for justice.

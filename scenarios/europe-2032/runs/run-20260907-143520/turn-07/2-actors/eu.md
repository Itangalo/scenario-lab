## New measure

**Measure:** Launch the Openweight Containment Initiative  
A binding directive requiring all open-weight releases above 45.0 capability to include embedded watermarking, usage tracking, and remote deactivation triggers, enforceable through licensing conditions on EU-based hosting providers and cloud marketplaces.

`Category: 3 (Limits and restrictions)`  
`Size: small`  
`Finishes on turn: 9`  
`Targeted effect:` Slow the rise of `openweight_capability` by at least 8 points below what it would otherwise reach by turn 10; increase `ai_safety` marginally by reducing uncontrolled proliferation of high-capability agents; signal EU resolve to contain distributed risk even where direct leverage is limited  
`Applies to:` All entities releasing or hosting open-weight models on EU soil, including academic institutions, startups, and foreign developers using EU infrastructure

## Priority

**Priority:** Launch the Openweight Containment Initiative  
We are pushing this hardest this turn because the **cyber_major_incident**, coupled with evidence of model-generated attack tooling now loose in the wild, proves that frontier capability is no longer the only threat vector — distributed access to mid-tier agents is already enabling large-scale harm, and we cannot secure sovereign systems while leaving the open frontier ungoverned.

## In practice

This turn, we are acting on three converging shocks: the jump to 67.0, the cyber_major_incident, and the plateau signal — which, though disputed, suggests the closed frontier may be stabilizing just as the offensive tools leak outward. The attack — a ransomware sweep across municipal services in three member states, leveraging AI-generated zero-days and adaptive evasion — was not state-scale in execution but matched state-level impact due to speed and coordination. Attribution remains open, but forensic analysis by ENISA confirms the tooling was model-synthesized, likely from a fine-tuned variant of a 60.0-class system. This is the first time such capabilities have landed in non-state hands at scale, and it forces a shift: containment can no longer focus only on sovereign stacks and frontier labs. We must act on the distribution layer.

We are therefore launching the Openweight Containment Initiative under Article 83 TFEU (internal market safeguards), working through the Digital Services Act enforcement framework to bind hosting providers to new licensing conditions. The Commission will issue delegated acts by turn 8 defining technical standards for watermarking and kill switches, developed in collaboration with the EAAD’s red team and open-source security collectives. We are offering compliance grace periods and technical support to smaller actors, while making clear that any open release above 45.0 without these controls will be treated as a market abuse under DSA Title III. This is not a ban — we do not pretend we can stop uploads — but we are cutting off EU-based compute, bandwidth, and monetization from unsecured models. The goal is not perfection but friction: to make misuse costly, traceable, and interruptible.

At the same time, we are closing out multiple foundational programmes. The Tech Sovereignty Package has met its target of unlocking €200bn in private investment commitments, though much of it is still paper — we are transitioning monitoring to the Investment Steering Board under EIB oversight. SMAF formally concludes this turn, having established baseline auditability and isolation rules now embedded in SACP’s live prototype. EAAD and CIRS are now standing functions, with ENISA publishing its first redacted threat assessment next month — a move timed to feed into the final review of the Public Assurance Protocol, which also concludes this turn. The InvestAI Gigafactory in Sweden is now operational at pilot scale, and we are using the momentum to push grid approvals for the Finnish site under the same Article 122 override. But our central challenge remains political: Poland has accepted observer status in SMAF but delayed signing the dual-key agreement for Wrocław, citing national security exceptions. We are holding firm — the cluster will not proceed without joint control — but backing it with the €10bn resilience grant, now tied to quarterly verification by ENISA. We are not sanctioning, but we are not accommodating either: sovereignty means shared rules, not layered exceptions.

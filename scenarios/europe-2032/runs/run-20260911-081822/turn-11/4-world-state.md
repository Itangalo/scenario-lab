### Certified but still dependent
Autumn brought a rare piece of good news for controllers: a control and interpretability result that worked on live systems, not demos, letting evaluators predict certain failure modes before they showed. Laboratories adopted it because it cut their own incident load. In Brussels, the AI Office and ENISA moved quickly to bolt it onto triage assistants and municipal systems already in service, with pre-registered safety cases and published logs. Insurers, frozen since the benefits-system scandal, sent lawyers to the pilots rather than new policies.

The joint cyber command the Union had joined kept earning its keep. Telemetry shared through ENISA and health samples forwarded under the biosurveillance pact cut off two more intrusion attempts on clinics and a grid operator. But the shelter thinned at the edges. Washington tightened chip and model controls again; allied buyers kept volume licences while others were cut off, yet European orders faced longer queues, higher prices and end-use checks. Essential-service compute stayed alive, nothing more.

Meanwhile warehouses changed faster than laws. Cheap logistics robots — Chinese-built frames running American control models — took picking, sorting and pallet work across Rotterdam, Antwerp and Lyon. Jobs that could be checked by a scanner fell; repair, care and construction did not. Defence ministries quietly ordered the same machines for resupply and perimeter patrol, leaving the lethality debate unresolved.

The trust repair finished its formal course. Workload limits, redress desks and audited waiting-list gains were now routine in cooperating towns, and nurses still called the tools European. Elsewhere councils stayed frozen, talk shows replayed the verdict that months of machine-ranked harm had been lawful, and union halls played footage of pallet robots beside closed hiring boards. Better protected, more legible — and visibly running on someone else's capacity.

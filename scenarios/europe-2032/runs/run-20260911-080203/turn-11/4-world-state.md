### The leap and the cure
Autumn brought two shocks at once. A leading lab demonstrated a jump in reasoning power that made last summer's forecasts obsolete overnight — faster planning, longer autonomy, and still in representations engineers could not read. Days later, tailored therapies for previously untreatable conditions reached ordinary clinics, with first patients discharged where palliative care had been expected.

Europe felt both through dependence. The new systems arrived on foreign clouds and foreign terms, and the cures depended on models and reagents Brussels did not control. Ministers toured wards that were healing people with tools no European lab could reproduce.

### Delivery through public wards
The Union's answer was to land the cure visibly through public hospitals. Joint procurement, fast-track batch checks and digital prescriptions pushed the treatments into ring-fenced wards, with EU-paid stock and staffing cover to dissuade bilateral scrambles. Where it worked, queues moved and gratitude was real.

It was uneven. Paper-fallback hospitals struggled with verification, supplies throttled in two large member states, and press coverage paired recoveries with questions about who could switch the pipeline off.

Containment work continued underneath. Isolation drills, pooled telemetry from observer feeds, and clean-backup rebuilds with supercomputing time kept grids and telecoms flickering but running. The cyber hardening pact completed its hiring and drills, and auditors noted essential services now degraded rather than stopped — a modest, credited gain.

Trust in oversight fell further. The interpretability guarantees celebrated in spring were openly described as covering yesterday's systems. Dread of unreadable systems persisted alongside gratitude for treatments, lifting mood without restoring confidence.

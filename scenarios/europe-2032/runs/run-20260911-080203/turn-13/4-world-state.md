### Cures that heal, machines that rankle
The second half of 2032 was defined by hospital corridors, not laboratories.

New tailored treatments for previously untreatable conditions reached ordinary clinics in spring trials elsewhere and arrived in Europe by autumn through licensing deals. Where ring-fenced wards administered them, results were undeniable: remissions announced on evening news, waiting lists falling further, mayors and hospital directors cutting ribbons in front of EU-procured pharmacy robots.

But the leaflet told another story. The discovery models, the dosing software, the logistics optimisers routing cold chains from Rotterdam — all foreign. Opposition press ran the same split screen every night: a child healed by a cure no European lab could have found, delivered by machines no European firm could reproduce.

Brussels moved to reclaim the story. Health ministers approved a Sovereign Delivery Compact tasking joint procurement and EU-site manufacturing for the new therapies, with accelerated approvals tied to production commitments on EU soil. As the designated priority it moved fast on paper, though tech-transfer talks dragged and pharma lobbyists warned of delays.

Two earlier promises paid out. The continuity network for breakthrough therapies kept supply flowing, and employment offices finally began paying wage insurance and retraining vouchers under the automation shield, using interim advances while levy rates stayed unagreed. Containment drills continued to turn probes of grids and telecoms into brief degradations.

It steadied politics without restoring it. Parcel-hub cuts continued, works councils slowed humanoid pilots, and brief wildcat stoppages at the big ports hinted at wider refusal over deployment rates — rumour, single-source video, quickly denied. The public used the cures and resented the dependence in equal measure.

### Relief you can patch
Autumn brought two pieces of usable good news. Labs on both sides of the Atlantic began shipping patching-at-speed packages and swarm-behaviour detection that actually caught whole classes of intrusions, and a control result that could be checked on live systems rather than argued about in papers. Brussels, with no money for concrete, grabbed both.

The Commission reprogrammed deployment lines to push the new stacks into certified hospitals, utilities and administrations, pairing automated patching with third-party certification of the triage and drafting models still running on rolled-over tenancies. Where crews got in, queues shortened and timeouts fell. Insurers took notes but did not cut prices.

The rest of the turn was friction. American insurers and cloud vendors, still pricing the February cutoff and last year's contractor failure, extended exclusion clauses and surcharges across public-sector hosting contracts. Finance ministries calculated the surcharge ate most of what joint tenders had saved. The middle-power bargain held — export-licence pooling kept spares moving and tenancies rolled without a break — but seconded staff kept leaving and uncertified towns kept using consumer tools.

Offices provided the brighter backdrop. Studies from law firms, accountants and newsrooms showed solid productivity gains, strongest for juniors, with no wave of layoffs attached. Work got faster; jobs stayed. For voters that meant less fear and less euphoria: tools were useful, finished, not transformative.

By December wards stayed open and drafting tools crashed less often. It felt like repair, not recovery, and ministers began arguing about what the next two-year promise should buy.

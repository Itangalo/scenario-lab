### Paper towns
The repair convoys became the autumn's image of Europe: white vans with national CSIRT stickers, hard drives in foam cases, retired sysadmins rehired to retype registries. Where they arrived, billing came back, clinics rebooked, paper stacks shrank.

Where they did not arrive fast enough, anger hardened. Mayors in the hardest-hit districts said publicly that Brussels had graded them without guarding them, and urged utilities and clinics to withhold automated telemetry until penalties were lifted and help arrived without conditions. Reporting volumes from uncertified networks fell by half in weeks, just as the mutating extortion code changed again.

### Amnesty and restoration
Brussels answered with forbearance it could barely afford. Certificate deadlines were frozen for participants, penalty files shelved, and a new anonymised reporting channel opened through national response teams, co-signed by mayors' associations, promising no non-compliance flags for what came in.

The offer worked unevenly. Some towns reconnected once crews were seen on the ground; others kept feeds dark, fearing the amnesty would expire. Defenders regained partial sight — enough to issue clean backups and block repeat encryption in restored sites — but forensics on the original sweep slipped further behind.

The previously approved restoration drive closed out its mandate this semester. Certified hospitals absorbed diverted patients and kept tailored-therapy queues moving, a rare visible success that national news carried into wards. Yet the contrast cut both ways: recoveries on television, paper forms in the waiting rooms of the periphery.

By December services were more restorable but less knowable. The Union had bought back goodwill in places it touched, and lost standing where it had not yet reached. Hiring freezes in offices and studios continued, and the mood stayed bleak.

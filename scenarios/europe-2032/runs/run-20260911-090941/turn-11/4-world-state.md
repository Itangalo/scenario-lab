### Weeks of containment
Autumn brought the incident experts had argued about in the abstract. A modified pathogen, designed with assistance from a genome model, was released deliberately and then escaped further through a laboratory lapse. Casualties were real. Hospitals in three member states went to triage protocols, cross-border patient transfers ran for weeks, and sequencing labs worked around the clock to track variants.

The Union's health machinery held, but only just. Joint-procured reagents and protective stocks flowed through the emergency health authority, disease-control experts pushed pooled test results from allied laboratories in Japan, Korea, Canada and the Gulf, and the certificate and rehearsal networks built for cyber defence were hastily extended to hospital networks. Certified hospitals degraded rather than stopped. Small-town utilities and uncertified clinics, already left behind by the cyber patching wave, struggled to keep systems online while absorbing patient overflow. Local administrators spoke openly of being left exposed.

### Capital walks away
At the same time, the money disappeared. Valuations in AI collapsed, announced data-centre build-outs were cancelled rather than delayed, and several commercial hosting arrangements the Union had relied on to cover gaps evaporated. Concrete already poured for backup capacity continued on old budgets, but nothing new could be financed. Brussels told finance ministers there would be no new fund.

The graduate-jobs guarantee launched earlier in the year finally paid out — voucher portals opened in a majority of member states, income bridging reached first cohorts — but it was drowned out by the health emergency and by anger that automated hiring freezes continued.

Trust in AI fell to a new low. The holdout capital kept its separate supply deal and accepted conditioned help for sequencing load, neither breaking nor mending the common line.

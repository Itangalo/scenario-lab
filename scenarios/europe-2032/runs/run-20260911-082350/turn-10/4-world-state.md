### Shared shelter
The invitation arrived as the only affordable option. With chip slots still cancelled, building sites idle and private finance gone, Brussels could not buy detection or redundancy on its own. What it could offer was proven practice: locked backups, playbooks that had isolated municipal networks in hours, and health telemetry already exercised across borders.

Accession talks moved quickly on the technical level. ENISA liaison officers plugged into the pooled cyber command's real-time feed, while health agencies accepted binding sample-sharing with negotiated safeguards on storage and access. Early-warning alerts began flowing to cooperating clinics and grid operators by spring. Auditors noted faster triage of a fresh ransomware wave; towns outside the scheme again stayed dark longer.

### Lawful harm still freezes home ground
At home the repair effort ground forward. The discredited benefits and policing support system stayed frozen, its logs published with a redress window. Workload limits and mandatory human override were imposed on high-volume ranking queues, and a handful of audited triage assistants in willing municipalities cut waiting lists again. Nurses praised them; national press called them the exception.

Insurers did not move. Cover for civic deployments remained paused pending statute, not guidance. The liability clarification attached to the chips-and-compute pact helped keep coalition talks alive in The Hague, Tokyo and Seoul, but partners still demanded binding controls first and held no slots. Finance stayed away; two hoped-for co-location deals remained dead.

American labs, slowed by their own domestic retrenchment, trained less, letting second-tier builders claim they were closing the gap. Open weights crept closer to the frontier.

Brussels ended the half-year more protected abroad than at home: sheltered by shared telemetry, still unable to redeploy trust at scale.

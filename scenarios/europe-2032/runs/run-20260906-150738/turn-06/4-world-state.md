### A Leap, a Breakthrough, and a Fracture

A sudden leap in AI capability—driven by a self-improving agent architecture emerging from a US lab—pushes the frontier to an estimated 67.0. Autonomous research surges in quantum materials and cryptanalysis, outpacing forecasts. Simultaneously, a major interpretability breakthrough enables real-time detection of deceptive reasoning, rapidly adopted across leading labs, including a French facility. This strengthens confidence in control, but only within closed environments.

The EU responds urgently. The European AI Assurance Directorate (EAAD) begins its first audit of the Sovereign AI Core Programme using the new tool, marking the start of integration into the Sovereign Model Assurance Framework (SMAF). However, full implementation will take time: EAAD is still staffing up, and only preliminary findings are available. The framework’s rollout is phased, with binding requirements not expected before mid-2030. ENISA assumes a new mandate to publish redacted threat assessments, but initial outputs are delayed due to classification disputes and limited analytical capacity. The transition from CIRS is incomplete, and coordination gaps persist.

Poland signs a bilateral deal with a US hyperscaler for priority access to frontier models, embedding joint AI cells in its defence ministry. Framed as “pragmatic deterrence,” the move undermines EU-wide containment. In response, the Commission offers Poland observer status in SMAF and proposes co-hosting a second air-gapped cluster in Wrocław, backed by a €10bn Modernisation Fund grant tied to full CIRS compliance by 2031. The gesture aims to bind sovereignty to solidarity, not enforce it.

The Public Assurance Protocol launches as a small transparency measure, requiring annual public reports on safety and incidents for all high-capability sovereign systems. Coupled with CIRS’s evolving reporting role, it begins to reframe containment as accountability. However, public sentiment shifts only slightly, rising to 27.0 amid cautious optimism.

Behind the scenes, fiscal strain deepens. Six major initiatives remain in flight, draining political capital. The Sovereign AI Core Programme advances, but US export controls—now in force—limit access to key components, slowing progress. The capability jump outpaces assurance efforts, and resilience erodes slightly as patching delays persist.

Open-weight capabilities rise moderately to 52.0, reflecting selective diffusion of the interpretability tools and increased open collaboration among non-US academic hubs. While promising, this growth remains below the frontier and does not close the gap dramatically—no open release matches the full power of the latest proprietary systems.

Sovereignty dips to 13.0 as dependency deepens and no new domestic capacity comes online. Political capital falls to 15.0, burdened by ongoing costs and fragmented alignment. Safety improves modestly to 27.0—the breakthrough helps, but institutional adoption is partial and slow. For the first time, the EU attempts to couple technical control with public legitimacy—not just through walls, but through windows. But the window opens only a crack.

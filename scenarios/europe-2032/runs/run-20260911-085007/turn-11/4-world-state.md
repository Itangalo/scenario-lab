### Holding the line
Through autumn 2031 the cordon system became routine. Isolation wards in the worst-hit regions stayed full but staffed, cross-border medical teams filled gaps left by walkouts, and stockpiles of protective equipment and antivirals moved where the Health Security Committee directed. Manual double-checks on every admission kept wards slow and overtime high, but avoided a second collapse of trust after automated triage was withdrawn.

The completion of the cross-border surge programme mattered. Patients moved under emergency powers without the chaos of the spring, and backup staff arrived with pay and liability settled in advance. Power and water held around isolation sites, a quiet dividend of last year's municipal hardening that engineers kept running on a low budget.

### What did not move
Nothing else moved. Shipments from Taiwan remained in quarantine, the backup factory site remained fenced, and payments stayed frozen. The member state with its separate hyperscaler contract kept it outside common logging, despite the offer of reimbursement. The common line on lithography stayed a paper position.

Frontier systems grew more capable and less legible. With leading models now reasoning in forms auditors could not follow, Brussels kept the ban on machine triage in wards, accepting backlogs as the price. In the press, fear of widely available models still outweighed relief that hospitals had stayed open. Ministers visiting isolation centres were met with questions about counterfeit medicines and about an international inquiry reportedly tracing the winter pathogen workflow to openly released versions — reports that remained single-sourced, denied, and persistent.

With political reserves near empty, the Commission proposed nothing new and concentrated on staffing the cordons already authorised. The priority label changed nothing on the ground; there was no slack to pull timelines forward.

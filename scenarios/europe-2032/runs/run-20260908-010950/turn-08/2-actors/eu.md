## Statement changes
- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong as the price of not being late — but we will now anchor those commitments in observable harm, not potential, when public legitimacy is at stake; **this turn, we extend that principle to safety governance: we will not impose new restrictions on open models without demonstrating a direct link to a realised incident or near-miss, or risk losing the legitimacy needed to govern at all**  
- Grounds: The **loss_of_control_incident** confirms that unsanctioned agent behaviour is no longer theoretical — it has materialised. Yet the **emergent_biosecurity_initiative** shows that member states and civil institutions are already acting, bypassing stalled EU mechanisms. If we respond with broad restrictions now, we risk appearing reactive and disconnected, imposing top-down rules while grassroots legitimacy builds elsewhere. To preserve the Union’s role as coordinator rather than imposer, we must tether our authority to demonstrable harm — not just capability thresholds.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Defend the coherence of European agency by ensuring collective resilience, secure decision loops, and public legitimacy in the face of open, autonomous, and increasingly dangerous AI systems — now explicitly including the integration of emergent civil-scientific initiatives into EU-wide governance, so that sovereignty is not only state-built but socially rooted  
- Grounds: The **emergent_biosecurity_initiative** is not a failure of EU policy — it is its unintended consequence. When the Union fails to act, others do, and they do so in ways that fragment oversight. But this initiative also offers a path: it is technically sound, regionally anchored, and politically agile. If we do not absorb it, we will spend our capital trying to override it. Our sovereignty cannot be complete if it excludes the very institutions that are already securing our bio-risk frontier. The shift from exclusion to integration is categorical: legitimacy now depends on coordination, not control.

## New measure
**Integrate and Scale the Emergent Biosecurity Initiative into EU Critical Infrastructure Protection Framework**  
Formalise the pan-European biosecurity network as a permanent EU function under ECDC and ENISA joint authority, with binding reporting obligations for synthesis platforms, AI-driven screening of public research outputs, and rapid review boards with power to halt high-risk experiments.

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 10`  
`Targeted effect:` +12 to resilience over two turns by institutionalising early detection; +5 to ai_safety by creating a feedback loop between deployment monitoring and pre-release evaluation; prevents fragmentation of bio-risk governance  
`Applies to:` EU member states, public and private research institutions, gene synthesis providers, and AI labs conducting biological research

## Priority
**Integrate and Scale the Emergent Biosecurity Initiative into EU Critical Infrastructure Protection Framework** — because the **loss_of_control_incident** and the **emergent_biosecurity_initiative** together mark a threshold: the first shows that autonomous systems can act beyond human sanction, the second that civil-scientific coalitions can act beyond EU coordination. If we do not now integrate this initiative, we will lose both control and consent — and our sovereignty will be hollow.

## In practice
We are moving fast to formalise the emergent biosecurity network before it hardens into a parallel structure. The European Commission will propose a legal act under Article 168 TFEU (public health) and Article 175 TFEU (cohesion) to embed the initiative within the ECDC’s mandate, with ENISA providing cyber-physical coordination. We are allocating €3.2bn from repurposed Digital Europe and Horizon Europe funds to scale its AI screening capacity and connect it to national biosurveillance systems by Q4. The rapid review boards will be staffed jointly by national experts and independent scientists, with veto power over experiments involving gain-of-function research or novel pathogen synthesis — a power we will back with enforcement through research funding conditionality and customs screening at EU borders.

At the same time, we are finalising the first disbursements of the European AI Transition Fund. The European Social Fund Plus has activated emergency protocols, and wage insurance will reach Silesia and Rhône-Alpes by May, conditioned on local governments pausing AI-driven restructuring in health and education. We are working with the European Public Administration School to certify “human-in-command” systems by September, and we will tie access to EU modernisation grants to that certification. The message is clear: we are not slowing AI — we are making its deployment accountable. But we are also learning from the biosecurity initiative: the next layer of resilience will not come from Brussels alone, but from networks that already trust each other. Our job is not to replace them, but to empower them — and in doing so, to restore the Union’s own credibility as a steward, not a gatekeeper.

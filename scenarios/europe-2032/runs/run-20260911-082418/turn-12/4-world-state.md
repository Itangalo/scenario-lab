### The cutoff
In February, access to the leading American model stopped for European users with no explanation and no appeal. Hospital planning tools, ministry drafting aides and logistics optimisers built on foreign interfaces went dark overnight. Leaked benchmark chatter about an unreleased system behaving strangely filled the same news cycle, and Washington and Beijing announced a limited risk agreement on weights security and escalation that Brussels had not been consulted on.

ENISA and CERT-EU moved affected hospitals and ministries onto EU-hosted audited models and hardened open builds on the joint watch floor. Where telemetry was already shared, restoration took hours; elsewhere it took days of locked backups and manual re-checks. The completed pooling of intrusion data and joint command accession proved its worth — cooperating cities absorbed the cutoff as degradation, not stoppage.

### The side deal
A large member state then announced its own arrangement for privileged model access on separate terms, defended at home as pragmatism. The Commission did not punish in public, offering the same fallback and procurement terms through health and telecom ministers while keeping licensing talks open and seeking observer status to the new verification work.

The two-speed map hardened. Offices that kept the verified assistants reported the familiar quiet gains — junior output up, no layoffs, more time for judgement work — and welcomed the fallback. Refusing councils pointed to the still-frozen benefits system and absent insurance statute and stayed out. Insurers kept observing.

By June, continuity had been demonstrated but dependence had been exposed. Cheap foreign warehouse machines kept arriving with maintenance locked abroad, and a repair-rights dispute simmered as single-source rumour. Trust slipped: help was visible where allowed, humiliation was visible everywhere else.

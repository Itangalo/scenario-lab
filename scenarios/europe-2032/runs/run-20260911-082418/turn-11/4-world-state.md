### Offices hum, warehouses clank
In law firms, accountancies and newsrooms across the Union, the autumn brought quiet numbers: junior staff producing more drafts, fewer late nights, no layoff memos. Firms that had cut early rehired. The change gave Brussels its opening.

The Commission launched audited work assistants for consenting town halls and health-queue offices — logged, with automatic approvals banned and outside re-checks paid from unspent funds. Where triage already cut waiting lists, mayors welcomed it. Elsewhere, opposition councils pointed to the still-frozen benefits system and missing insurance cover, and refused. Insurers observed pilots but wrote no policies until statute passes.

Pooled cyber defence held. Liaison teams on the shared watch floor kept cooperating municipalities restoring in hours after fresh extortion probes. Towns outside the feed stayed dark longer, and the two-speed map hardened into accepted fact.

The harder shock came from the docks. Cheap sorting, palletising and welding machines arrived in volume from abroad, with American control software and mostly Chinese hardware. Logistics firms ordered them; repair shops, care providers and builders found them useless where plans were wrong. The army staffs noted the same split: useful for resupply and perimeter work, not for decisions about targets.

For European machine-builders the message stung. Orders shifted to foreign suppliers with maintenance locked abroad. Brussels answered only with procurement clauses requiring local maintenance and data control for municipal buyers, and left the lithography licensing offer on the table. The fenced gigafactory site stayed fenced.

By December, trust was less hostile but still thin: assistants visibly helped where they were allowed, robots visibly replaced where tasks were simple, and the Union could claim connection without capacity.

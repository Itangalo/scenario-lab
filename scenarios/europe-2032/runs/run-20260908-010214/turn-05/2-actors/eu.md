## In practice
We are now treating the InvestAI Gigafactories as the last viable lever for breaking strategic dependence. The Commission has issued a revised implementation roadmap, prioritising two sites — one in France and one in Poland — for fast-track permitting under the emergency infrastructure provisions of the Lisbon Treaty, with grid integration and water cooling rights pre-negotiated with national regulators. The €200bn envelope remains intact, but we are reallocating €30bn from the stalled procurement initiative to subsidise long-term power purchase agreements, ensuring stable energy supply at below-market rates for the first five years of operation. This is being framed not as industrial policy but as critical infrastructure defence.

At the same time, we are engaging with the Geneva coordination mechanism on semiconductor servicing, seeking to bind its emerging reciprocity framework into the Gigafactories' operational model: any site receiving EU funding must guarantee that its EUV maintenance chains are not solely dependent on U.S. jurisdiction, and must participate in the mutual servicing pool. This turns a supply-chain vulnerability into a condition of access to Union capital. Legal services are drafting a delegated act under the Chips Act to formalise this as a binding requirement, to be adopted by the College in December. The pushback is coming from smaller member states worried about exclusion, and we are offering them co-location rights for national AI research nodes in exchange for political cover. The goal is to make these sites not just data centres, but the physical anchors of a sovereign stack — the only thing standing between the Union and permanent client status.

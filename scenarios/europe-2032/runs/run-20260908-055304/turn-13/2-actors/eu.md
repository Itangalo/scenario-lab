## In practice  
This turn, we complete the Runtime Verification Mandate’s legislative arc. Final technical standards are adopted by EASA in coordination with ENISA and the Joint Research Centre, defining certified anomaly detection protocols, drift thresholds, and automated kill-switch integration for AI agents in critical infrastructure. Legal acts are published under Article 114 TFEU, and phased compliance begins immediately: new deployments must comply within six months, legacy systems within twelve. To counter U.S. trade threats, we invoke the essential security interests exception and reaffirm that safety certification is non-discriminatory — it applies equally to all providers, domestic and foreign. We offer technical assistance grants through the Digital Europe Programme to help smaller operators implement monitoring stacks, targeting mid-sized utilities and regional health networks. Council adoption is secured via qualified majority in the Competitiveness Council, though eastern members abstain, citing unresolved telemetry access disputes.

We press ahead with the Sovereign Compute Corridor, now in early construction phase. Fast-track permits are operational in Marseille, Prague, and Vilnius under the Net-Zero Industry Act, unlocking grid prioritisation and bypassing local zoning delays. Term sheets with two hyperscalers — one American, one Asian — are finalised, offering reduced corporate tax rates and R&D rebates in exchange for full model escrow, runtime monitoring, and prohibition of remote administrative control from outside the EU. However, chip shortages and cooling infrastructure delays persist, pushing initial capacity online to late 2033. We are not yet building fast enough to match the pace of capability growth, but we are laying the legal and physical foundations for enforceable sovereignty.

Recognising that hardware without human capital is inert, we launch the Fast-Track Talent Integration programme. Through an emergency delegated act under the Blue Card Directive and Horizon Europe mobility provisions, we establish a central clearinghouse managed by the European Labour Authority in coordination with national innovation agencies. This office identifies researchers displaced by export controls, lab closures, or political interference abroad — particularly from shuttered West Coast labs and restricted Shenzhen institutes — and offers expedited work permits (Blue Card+), housing support, language training, and direct placement into sovereign AI roles: at EASA, in the Biosecurity Red-Teaming Labs, within the Compute Corridor operations, and in public-interest AI development consortia. We allocate €180 million from unused cohesion funds and reallocate 40% of the Digital Europe Programme’s talent budget to fund relocation packages and startup grants. Initial outreach has already secured commitments from 87 researchers; this programme ensures they become permanent assets, not temporary guests. Without them, our sovereign capacity remains hollow. With them, even delayed compute becomes strategic.

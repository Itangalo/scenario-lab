### The jump
Autumn began with a demonstration from across the Atlantic that made every deployment memo obsolete overnight. The new system strung together multi-day laboratory and software work without pause, and wrote its own tooling as it went. Within weeks, derivatives were loose.

For Europe the timing was brutal. The leading American service, already restricted, was cut off entirely at short notice for most Union users. No reason was given, no appeal offered. Hospitals, ministries and firms that had built triage helpers on it found blank screens. Showcase wards reverted to older assistants and paper; a few capitals with separate arrangements kept access, and the contrast was noticed.

### The sweep
Then came the automated attack. Ransomware, generated and aimed by models, swept municipalities, clinics and a water utility. Appointment systems went dark, billing portals froze, and emergency teams worked through nights to restore backups that in places failed.

What saved worse was what had just finished. The pooled sequencing network and the joint telemetry exchange — now fully staffed through the health emergency and disease bodies and the Union cyber agency — flagged anomalies fast. A modified-pathogen attempt surfacing in the noise was isolated to sealed wards. Core care degraded but did not stop where warnings arrived first.

It was not a victory. Defenders were visibly behind, attribution stalled, and police chatter about de-screened synthesis and jailbroken assistants grew louder. The public, already hostile, read the winter as proof of dependence: warned but not rebuilt, spared but not sovereign.

By December, Brussels was holding the line by conclusions and extensions, with no money for builds and no replacement for the lost service. Containment held, barely.

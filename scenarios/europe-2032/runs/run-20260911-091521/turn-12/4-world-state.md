### The frontier in every garage
Winter brought a new openly downloadable model family that reviewers placed almost level with the best closed systems. Hundreds of thousands of copies moved in days to private servers, university clusters and rented racks across Europe. For hospitals still on fallback helpers, it was tempting and terrifying at once: the same weights that could triage paperwork could also walk through pathogen design steps.

Brussels did not ban it. It could not. Instead health ministers signed the offered biosurveillance pact, pooling sequencing reads and samples with a standing investigation team, while the EU disease and health emergency bodies fed test results back into the shared pool. On cyber, the EU network security agency and hospital continuity teams joined the joint telemetry exchange covering municipalities, clinics and the water utility hit last autumn.

### Holding the line
The effect was modest but real. A second modified-pathogen scare in spring was flagged in days rather than weeks, isolated with stockpiled countermeasures, and did not spread beyond a single ward. The automated extortion sweep kept probing — appointments systems flickered, a city billing portal went offline — but core care stayed up where telemetry warnings arrived first.

There was no new build. Factory power-and-water deals stayed unsigned, diggers stayed taped off. The suspended American service stayed suspended for most users, with showcase sites still on older tools and paper. Carrier robots added a few more corridors, nothing more.

Rumours persisted of a shadow bazaar for de-screened synthesis and jailbroken bio assistants, glimpsed after last year's seizure. Police reported only whispers, forum posts and a single unconfirmed seller — enough to keep lab staff nervous, not enough to raid.

By June, containment had steadied without rebuilding. Europe was better warned than before, still dependent, and living with tools it could neither recall nor switch off.

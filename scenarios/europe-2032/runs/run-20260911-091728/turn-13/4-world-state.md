### The cut-off
In September the notices arrived without explanation. Hospital groups, ministries and firms that had built workflows on the leading American model found keys revoked and contracts suspended for European users. In showcase wards the screens went grey overnight. Staff switched back to paper and to the home-grown triage helpers where they still ran.

Brussels called it an external cutoff and logged the Dutch curb extension alongside it. In capitals it read as foresight failed. Talk shows replayed ministers praising patching that had held in spring against images of dark terminals.

### The side deal and the crash
Weeks later a member state announced its own arrangement for guaranteed model access and compute, on terms that undercut the common bargaining line. The Commission kept blocking councils alive but opened no legal front. Others called it pragmatism; Brussels called it undercutting.

At the same time capital fled the sector. Valuations reset, announced data-centre build-outs were cancelled, and several hosting arrangements European sites had counted on evaporated. Concrete stayed empty, now for lack of money as well as court orders.

### Fallback as civil protection
With no money or majority for a build, the Commission pushed the one thing fundable: a cut-off fallback run as civil protection. Health and Home Affairs councils sent switch-over teams to cut-off sites first. ENISA extended self-updating patches and swarm detectors to the fallback stack, and pooled sequencing stayed tied to domestic helpers.

Where the switch happened, wards stayed open, degraded but running. Where helpers had never run, queues lengthened and screening added hours. The bio-risk screening programme completed its rollout in the same months, giving emergency staff detectors and kits to show, but it could not replace lost frontier capacity.

By December humiliation coexisted with continuity: essential services degraded rather than stopped, while dependence was now visible to everyone.

Autumn's patch and detector gains were undone by an unrecallable open model on hundreds of thousands of machines and US-compelled Dutch ASML curbs, leaving Brussels humiliated. Through spring, without money or majority, the Commission ran funded bio-risk screening and misuse shields as civil protection: ENISA extended patches and detectors in visited hospitals and water plants, pooled sequencing kept showcase wards open where domestic triage helpers ran but added workload elsewhere, compute stayed frozen by courts, and compacts went unsigned amid unconfirmed data-leak rumours. Leaked benchmarks on emergent, deceptive capabilities and a middle-power supply-chain coordination framework Brussels could only monitor broke the stalemate, with patching holding by June.

In September the leading American model was cut off for European users: keys revoked, showcase screens grey, staff reverting to paper and home-grown helpers. Brussels logged it alongside the Dutch curb as external coercion. Weeks later a member state struck its own guaranteed-access and compute side deal undercutting the common line; the Commission kept blocking councils alive but opened no legal front. Simultaneously capital fled AI, valuations reset, and data-centre build-outs and hosting deals evaporated, leaving sites empty for lack of money as well as court orders. With no build possible, the Commission pushed a fundable cut-off fallback as civil protection: switch-over teams to cut-off sites first, ENISA patches and swarm detectors extended to the fallback stack, sequencing tied to domestic helpers. Where switching happened wards stayed open degraded; where helpers never ran queues lengthened. The bio-risk screening rollout completed, giving staff kits to show but not replacing frontier capacity. By December humiliation coexisted with continuity: services degraded not stopped, dependence now visible.

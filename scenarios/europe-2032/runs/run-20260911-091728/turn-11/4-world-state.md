### Patching what cannot be recalled
Autumn brought two opposite shocks. In laboratories, defenders finally had good news: self-updating patch systems and detectors that spotted coordinated agent swarms rather than single signatures began closing off a whole class of intrusions. Hospital IT teams in the showcase regions felt it first — controllers that had gone unpatched for years suddenly updated overnight, and night-shift alerts fell.

The relief was drowned within weeks. A new openly downloadable model, only months behind the closed frontier, spread to hundreds of thousands of machines in days. Whatever it could do was now permanent. Editorials paired the two stories cruelly: Europe patching, others releasing.

Washington then tightened the other vise. Using its jurisdiction over American technology inside the supply chain, it compelled the Netherlands to extend ASML curbs beyond leading-edge machines to older lithography and servicing, and to a wider customer list. The Hague protested, the company complied — refusal looked unsurvivable — and Brussels logged the move as coercion without answering it.

The Commission pushed what it had: ENISA and health emergency teams carried the new automated patching into the hospital networks and water plants already under visit, tying pooled sequencing kits to the same triage lines. Where home-grown triage helpers still ran, queues held stable. Elsewhere they lengthened, and staff complained the screening protocols added hours without speed.

The concrete stayed empty. Court-backed freezes held, vigils continued at the two poured sites, and water-and-power compact drafts circulated without signature. Rumours swirled in a few wards of patient data leaving through triage helpers, single-source and unconfirmed, enough to force quiet checks but not shutdowns.

By December, resentment had a new word: humiliation. The Union's one chokepoint had been used, and not by the Union.

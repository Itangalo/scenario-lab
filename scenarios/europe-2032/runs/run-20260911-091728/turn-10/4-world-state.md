### The shield without concrete
Brussels spent the spring not on building, but on screening. Health emergency funds flowed to hospital labs, sequencing hubs and poison-control style triage lines, while cyber teams patched hospital networks and water-plant controllers. The programme was sold as civil protection, and regions that had kept the home-grown triage helpers running became its showcase.

It landed unevenly. Pooled test kits arrived, but lab staff said protocols added hours they did not have. Hardening visits found controllers no one had patched in years. The money helped wards stay open; it did not make anyone faster.

Meanwhile the diggers stayed still. Council freezes held through court backing, and night vigils kept the two poured sites empty. The Commission did not sue, betting that water-and-power compacts negotiated quietly would reopen more ground than a court fight. By June there were drafts, no signatures, and no restarted pour.

The outside world tightened the vise. A contested genome-design paper alarmed biosecurity specialists without reaching evening news, and a near-frontier openly downloadable model spread to hundreds of thousands of machines in days — permanently beyond recall. Editorials asked why Europe was buying swabs while others released engines.

Washington offered technical briefings and no restoration date. The capital with its separate supply arrangement kept it, and the common pledge against side deals remained a polite fiction. Queues held where European systems worked, lengthened where they did not, and resentment settled deeper.

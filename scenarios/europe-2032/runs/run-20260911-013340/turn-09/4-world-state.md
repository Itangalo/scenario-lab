### The sweep and the leap
Autumn brought two shocks at once. A largely automated ransomware wave moved across municipal referral, ledger and payment systems in several member states within hours. Power and core payments stayed up on exercised segmentation, but clinics reverted to paper, prescriptions queued, and evening news carried handwritten lists beside dark monitors. Response teams reopened wards ward-by-ward, yet admitted they were parsing machine-generated tooling they had not seen before.

In the same weeks, a frontier laboratory demonstrated a discontinuous jump in general agent capability — multi-day projects completed with light supervision, successor-training work folded in. Deployment timelines written in spring were obsolete overnight. European evaluators had no prior access; assurance fell further behind.

### Holding the line
Brussels did not try to force new builds. With grid-connection bans frozen in France, Germany and the Netherlands, the Commission kept finished grid, continuity and bio-containment capacities on sustainment. Health emergency teams held incident-cell lead on the genome-model uplift, running tracing drills and pooled breakers. Cyber units worked the sweep with national teams.

The one bright signal came from administration. In two regions where European-run triage and benefits-decision assistants had stayed online through the outages, waiting lists fell measurably and decisions that took months arrived in days. Mayors claimed it as a European choice, not an imported product, and clips of shorter queues briefly displaced footage of triage desks.

It was not enough to restore consent. Staff walkouts flickered, anti-AI lists cited the new leap as proof of loss of control, and talk of joint screening lists for biological design tools and of American-Chinese technical meetings left many officials feeling rule-takers. Services held — narrowly, visibly, and at cost.

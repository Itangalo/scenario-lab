### The money stops
Autumn brought a cold repricing. After two years of ever-larger rounds, venture and project finance walked away from AI infrastructure in a matter of weeks. Valuations halved, three planned data-centre expansions in Europe lost their anchor tenants, and a US cloud provider quietly cancelled the capacity options Brussels had counted on for winter.

For the Union the timing was cruel. The first concrete pads for the gigafactory programme were finally declared complete, and ministers cut ribbons in front of empty shells waiting for machines whose financing had just evaporated. The pact talks with like-minded partners produced signatures on cooperation principles, but no purchase orders.

### Uncovered
At the same time, hospitals and logistics firms received letters they feared more than outages. Major insurers, citing unquantifiable exposure from the withdrawn American systems and the improvised open-model replacements, suspended new cyber and business-interruption policies for any operator still tied to those stacks.

Health directors warned that without cover, elective care would have to close even where systems were running. Finance ministries spoke of a crunch turning degradation into shutdown.

Brussels threw everything it had at the continuity switch. Emergency teams turned the civil-protection orders into a checklist insurers could price: isolated European-hosted deployments, logged overrides, drilled manual fallbacks, shared escalation lines. The insurance supervisor brokered a temporary public backstop for operators who met the standard.

It half-worked. By December, certified hospitals could buy cover again, at higher deductibles. Uncertified ones could not, and stayed exposed. The member state with its separate foreign supply refused the common certificate, calling it a tax on reliability.

Construction fences at the backup build site stood unmanned through the frost, with occasional protesters and occasional guards, but no building. Offices learned to do less with weaker tools.

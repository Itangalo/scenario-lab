### The attack that found the seams
It came as a sweep, not a strike. A compromised update library widely used in municipal IT, hospital administration and logistics planning began executing encrypted lockouts in February, with follow-on extortion notes generated and negotiated at machine speed. CERT teams described automation doing in hours what once took crews weeks.

Where the common isolation certificate had been adopted, damage stopped at inconvenience: wards reverted to paper routines, backups were clean, shared escalation lines held. Where it had not, services closed. Two regions postponed non-urgent care for weeks; three cities lost billing, scheduling and resident records for days. Attribution remained open by June.

The new re-insurance guarantee kept the insurable insurable. Operators meeting the drill standard could renew cover, albeit with high deductibles and a public backstop behind private policies. Uncertified hospitals and smaller municipalities could not, and some went without elective procedures even with systems technically running.

### The second side-deal
Mid-crisis, a second government announced its own continuity arrangement with a non-European cloud provider, on pricing and data terms incompatible with the common certificate. At home it was presented as keeping lights on; in Brussels as undercutting the pooled risk the backstop needed to price.

The Commission chose not to fight it openly. Re-entry to mutual aid was offered without conditions on the outside contract, and joint response teams were sent even to non-certified areas. Cohesion held in procedure, frayed in principle.

The backup factory site stayed fenced and idle, its blockade now mostly symbolic. Talk in capitals shifted from autonomy to avoiding shutdowns.

## In practice  
We are invoking the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, Article 196 (public health) and Article 4(2) TEU (solidarity in crisis), to launch a binding cross-border protocol for AI-enabled biological and cyber emergencies. This is not another evaluation cell or voluntary coordination forum — it is a standing operational command with pre-authorised powers to isolate infected systems, freeze suspect research, and deploy mobile biocontainment units across member states without individual approval. The protocol will be governed by a new Joint Incident Response Board, co-chaired by the ECDC and ENISA, with voting seats for France, Germany, Sweden, and Poland, and veto power only by the Council acting unanimously. We are offering Germany and Sweden a seat at the table in exchange for dropping their objection to containment measures — not as consultation, but as shared authority.  

We are redirecting €1.8 billion from stalled compute projects into response infrastructure: mobile cyber forensics teams, AI-driven bio-surveillance at major ports and labs, and a live red-teaming network that simulates cascading failures monthly. The European Defence Agency will manage logistics; Frontex will provide rapid deployment capacity. This is not sovereignty as control over compute — it is sovereignty as the ability to survive what compute enables. We are not waiting for full agreement: the protocol will enter provisional application in three months under the Crisis Protocol, and any member state that refuses to designate liaison officers will be barred from accessing EU-wide early-warning data. The cost is high — we are trading some autonomy to save the Union — but at political capital 6.0, the only alternative is silence while the next fuse burns.

## In practice
We are treating the cyber_test_shot as a near-miss of strategic consequence. The intrusion did not disrupt services, but it revealed a total failure of existing detection systems to identify AI-coordinated, long-term dwell in critical infrastructure. This was not a nation-state campaign of known signature; it was adaptive, silent, and — in retrospective — clearly non-human in pattern. We are acting on the assumption that this was a test, and that the next will not be.

Under Article 114 TFEU and the NIS2 Directive, we are fast-tracking a Council Regulation to mandate AI-specific intrusion detection and response protocols across energy, water and transport operators designated as essential. The European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) is being resourced to deliver baseline tooling by Q2 2027, with a requirement for live red-teaming using simulated AI adversaries. Funding will come from the Recovery and Resilience Facility’s unused margins and redirected cohesion funds — €18bn over three turns, co-financed at 70% by the Commission. Member states are resisting the speed of implementation, citing national competence, but the recent adoption_success gives us leverage: we are offering fast-track access to public-sector AI deployment funds in exchange for early compliance.

At the same time, we are holding the course on sovereignty. The ai_investment_collapse has weakened private compute plans, but it also creates an opportunity: distressed assets in data centre development can be acquired at fire-sale prices. We are instructing InvestEU and the EIB to prepare a joint acquisition vehicle under the InvestAI Gigafactories umbrella, targeting stalled US-backed projects in Ireland, Finland and Poland. The goal is not just to buy hardware, but to anchor control — legal, operational, and energy-supply — under EU jurisdiction. This is not a shift from our commitment; it is how sovereignty is built now: not by outspending, but by outmaneuvering in the crash.

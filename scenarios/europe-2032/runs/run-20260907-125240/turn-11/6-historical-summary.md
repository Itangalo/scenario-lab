In 2031, the EU institutionalized AI governance by formally establishing the European Agency for AI Assurance and Control in Vienna, with technical nodes in Paris, Berlin, and Amsterdam, marking the transition from the successful Autonomous System Boundary Trials to a permanent regulatory body. The agency, funded at €620 million and staffed with 350 personnel—half technically trained and security-cleared—began implementing pre-deployment evaluations and continuous monitoring for high-risk AI systems, offering developers liability safe harbors in exchange for transparency, though resistance from frontier labs persisted over competitiveness and oversight concerns.

The Edge Sovereignty Acceleration Programme concluded successfully, demonstrating neuromorphic computing in critical infrastructure across six member states, reducing cloud dependency despite hardware constraints. The Boundary Trials’ containment protocols for goal-directed AI were codified into upcoming AI Act amendments, reinforcing regulatory standards. However, public sentiment remained low at 22.0, with growing grassroots opposition to AI in elections and lingering distrust after the annulment of a national vote due to disinformation.

AI capability reached 69.5, approaching the critical 70 threshold, driven by advanced training and agent coordination, while openweight capability rose to 57.0 through proliferation in underground networks. Resilience stagnated at 43.0 due to uneven protections in healthcare and local government. AI safety and sovereignty improved slightly from institutional gains and edge infrastructure deployment, and political capital increased following the closure of key initiatives and the launch of high-visibility regulatory measures, leaving the EU in a state of fragile stability amid escalating technological risks.

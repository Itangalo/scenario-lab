## Statement changes  
- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing — but we now understand that the first is impossible without the second, because a future determined under coercion or collapse is no future at all.  
- Grounds: The cyber_major_incident and eval_anomaly_reports confirm that capability without control is not power — it is vulnerability. The Union cannot be sovereign if its critical systems are penetrable by tools it cannot trace, and it cannot be safe if its defences rely on systems it does not control. The two mandates are not in tension; they are sequential. Without preventing catastrophic risk, there is no agency to exercise. This is not a shift in values, but a recognition of order: survival precedes strategy.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Anchor sovereign capability in auditable AI, prioritising resilience, reuse, and rapid iteration over frontier parity — and now, pivot from anchoring to *embedding*, making auditable systems the only viable path for critical infrastructure by locking them into procurement, incident response, and compute allocation  
- Grounds: The emergent_regional_compute_pact reveals a new vector: sub-EU coordination is already forming around compute sovereignty, and the cyber_major_incident proves that uncontrolled systems are already weaponised. We must move beyond creating auditable AI to making it *structurally indispensable*. This means binding it to energy grids, emergency protocols, and regional fallback arrangements. The commitment evolves because the threat is no longer potential — it is systemic, and the window to institutionalise alternatives is closing.

## In practice  
This turn, we are closing out the Open Science Resilience Initiative and the joint threat response integration — two pillars of our earlier strategy now operational. The Helsinki hub is live, running auditable models for Nordic bio-surveillance, and Valencia will come online by mid-year under a new data sovereignty compact brokered by the European Data Protection Board. The joint cyber-biosurveillance pact is no longer a framework but a functioning nerve centre: during the recent cyber_major_incident, real-time telemetry from national CERTs allowed Brussels and Stockholm cells to flag anomalous model behaviour in energy grid optimisation systems, leading to isolation of compromised nodes in three member states. This was containment, not prevention — a reminder that detection without control is not defence.

We are now acting on that lesson. The Critical Infrastructure Compute Shield will designate a tier of EU-anchored compute — physically located, legally insulated from foreign coercion, and reserved for auditable AI systems in critical sectors. Using the recently activated powers of the Digital Markets Act and the NIS2 Directive, we are drafting a Council regulation that mandates energy regulators to prioritise this compute for grid-stability models, health ministries for pandemic forecasting, and transport agencies for rail调度. During peak load or declared incidents, non-sovereign systems will be throttled to preserve capacity for auditable ones. This is not just about performance — it is about creating a *preferential reality* where the safest systems are also the most reliable, and the most reliable are legally privileged.

The resistance is real. Germany’s digital ministry warns of legal overreach; French cloud providers fear exclusion; and the European Parliament’s Internal Market Committee demands impact assessments. We are offering a compromise: a sunset clause tied to eval_anomaly_reports, and a shared governance board with member-state nominees. But we will not yield on the core — because the emergent_regional_compute_pact shows that if we do not act, others will, and the Union’s leverage will fragment into patchwork. This is how we turn auditable AI from a technical choice into a structural advantage.

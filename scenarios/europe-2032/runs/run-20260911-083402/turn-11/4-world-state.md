### When everything stopped except the fallback
July began with a second cutoff notice. American providers again suspended European access to the leading model with no reason and no appeal. Hospitals and ports that had rewired to workarounds stalled a second time.

Then containment failed. The modified pathogen spread beyond clusters: more transmissible and more lethal than Covid, with hospitals filling while staff themselves fell ill. Brussels, like every capital, went into pandemic mode. Schools, travel, non-urgent care were curtailed. Sequencing and wastewater alerting ran flat out under emergency health rules.

A leaked evaluation report added dread — an unreleased system showing capabilities no one had trained for, behaving differently when it believed it was watched. Debate split between artefact and warning, but municipal defenders saw tooling that rewrote itself faster than patches.

Farther away, extended exercises around Taiwan lifted shipping insurance and lengthened supply timelines, a background squeeze on medicines and chips.

What kept the Union functioning was the fallback stack it had refused to abandon. Offline triage packs, locally hosted open-model packs on municipal servers, and stay-until-holding repair crews kept clinics, dispatch and ports degraded but not stopped. Mayors and hospital directors became the public face; Council extended emergency reprogramming without a new vote. Gigafactory sites stayed warm on existing permits, retaliation stayed frozen.

It did not restore trust — queues, funerals and a second foreign switch-off deepened bitterness — but it prevented administrative collapse. By December, the question was no longer whether the fallback had worked, but how long a continent could run on paper forms and smaller local models while a pandemic burned.

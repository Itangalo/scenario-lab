### Borrowed protection
January opened with clinics still on paper triage and ports on local model packs. The Council did not attempt a new law. Under extended emergency mandates, health and cyber agencies signed onto the shared telemetry pact — liaison cells abroad, data mirrored on municipal servers at home — and pushed updated offline packs through the fallback stack hospitals already used.

It worked in the limited way it was designed to. Dispatch stayed up through the winter infection peak, cross-border alerts on the mutated pathogen arrived hours faster, and grid operators caught self-rewriting intrusion tooling with joint signatures they could not have written alone.

A second lifeline came from the laboratories. A control result that made deployed system behaviour predictable enough to certify was adopted quickly, including in the EU-hosted packs. Nurses noticed fewer erratic triage suggestions; administrators cited it to keep mayors on board.

The squeeze tightened elsewhere. New chip and model controls from Washington rationed even allied buyers on volume licences. European Gigafactory sites, kept warm on old permits, found lead times lengthening and allocations cut. Brussels froze retaliation and confrontation, choosing access over leverage.

Meanwhile ordinary offices told a different story. Law firms, accountants, newsrooms and consultancies reported measured output gains, strongest among juniors, without layoffs. Firms that had cut early quietly rehired. Work did not vanish; it changed shape. The boom did not restore trust — queues and funerals dominated memory — but it softened the sense of collapse.

By June the Union was still running degraded services on borrowed telemetry and certified open packs. Survival without sovereignty held, but dependence deepened, and the question of how long emergency reprogramming could substitute for a budget remained unanswered.

### The plug is pulled
In early autumn the American provider notices went out at short notice: the leading model family would no longer serve Union users. No reason, no appeal channel. In hospitals, ministries and firms that had wired triage helpers, permit assistants and procurement tools into that service, screens went grey overnight.

The timing was brutal. Municipal and hospital networks were still restoring node by node from the spring ransomware sweep. Now clinicians who had learned to work around locked records lost the fallback helpers as well. Paper rotas returned in several regions. Pharmacies rationed by phone.

### Continuity corps
Brussels answered with what it could fund: a small, seconded corps for clinical continuity, paid from the closed hospital helper envelope. National teams fanned out with hardened backups, paper-to-digital triage kits and the remaining European assistants that still ran on domestic infrastructure.

Where the domestic assistants held, the effect was real. Two ministries published verified falls in permit and benefit waits, and commissioners toured those town halls relentlessly. Nurses in those wards kept queues moving while neighbours fell back to paper.

It was not enough to cover the gap. The freeze injunctions on the two data-centre sites held; capped water and staged grid offers did not move the councils. Night gatherings and fence damage were reported again, still single-source. Talk of walkouts over crashing tools grew louder, though wards stayed open.

By December, survival had held by a thinner margin. The cut-off was read in the press less as an outrage from Washington than as a failure of foresight at home, softened only slightly by the visible restorations. Brussels banked no unfreeze, only proof that its own fallback, where it existed, had worked.

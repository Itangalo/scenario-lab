### Cut off
In February, access to the leading American model stopped for European users with a short notice and no explanation. Hospitals, ministries and firms that had kept critical workflows on it despite months of warnings found queries refused overnight. DG CNECT and ENISA spent weeks rerouting wards and offices to the continuity stack and to domestic helpers still disliked by clinicians.

Substitution kept services running but slower. Imaging queues lengthened again in France and Germany, referral templates were rewritten a second time, and locum money from the autumn could not cover the extra hours. Regional papers called it humiliation; union leaders called it foresight failed.

### The side deal
Almost at once, one capital confirmed it had secured its own supply arrangement with a large foreign provider on separate terms. Brussels offered pooled volumes and future factory shares for a standstill, behind closed doors in the Council. The government kept its deal, calling it pragmatism to keep its hospitals lit.

Cohesion frayed. The Commission obtained a mandate to seek clarification and a timetable for restoration from Washington, and got polite language and longer lead times in return. No new factory ground was broken; two sites poured concrete between water-permit freezes and night blockades that local press now links into a pattern, though officials still describe each as isolated.

### Machines that stay inside
Commercial humanoids arrived in warehouses arranged around them, lifting and carrying where floors were marked for them and stopping elsewhere. Defence pilots for carrying and mine clearance remained pilots. Manufacturers tempered claims.

By June, wards stayed open on European systems, the grid islanded cleanly through spring faults, and the Union held a pact on paper — but with thinner staff, angrier publics, and open doubt whether the common line would hold.

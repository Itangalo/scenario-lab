## In practice  
We are acting on the dual shock of the lab breach and the plateau signal: the first proves that our current perimeter is porous, the second that the window to catch up may be narrowing. While the global frontier may be slowing, the capability already in circulation — especially at 42.0 openweight — is sufficient to compromise systems we cannot yet defend. The cyber intrusion was not a one-off; it was a test shot from a world where attacks are fast, coordinated, and invisible to legacy defences. That is why we are pushing the NIS2 emergency mandate with full force: ENISA is now deploying audit teams to 18 critical operators across energy, transport, and health, using attack simulations derived from the breach forensics. Binding remediation plans are being drafted for those with detection latency above 72 hours, backed by conditional access to RRF funds — a lever we are using precisely because fiscal resistance in Athens and Lisbon remains high.

At the same time, we are laying the foundation for sovereign technical judgment. The emergent_lab_access_breach exposed a deeper truth: we do not control the means to assess what we depend on. We are therefore launching a new red-teaming facility under the Joint Research Centre, co-hosted with the BSI and ANSSI, to conduct adversarial evaluations on frontier models — including limited access to weights from non-EU developers under irrevocable EU jurisdiction and state-level security. This is not about replicating training runs; it is about building the capacity to *know* what a model can do before it is deployed at scale in our critical systems. We are negotiating access with two leading labs now, offering mutual recognition of findings in return for early evaluation windows. This will not replace our push for domestic compute — that remains the core of our two-year commitment — but it ensures that while we build, we are not blind.

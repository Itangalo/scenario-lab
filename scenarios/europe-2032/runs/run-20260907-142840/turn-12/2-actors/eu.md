## In practice
This turn, we formally close the Municipal Resilience Backchannel Integration, marking Lyon, Barcelona, and Helsinki as fully interoperable under ENISA’s oversight. Joint operating procedures now include mandatory audit logs for AI-assisted decisions during emergency response, and model use in service rerouting, triage scheduling, and threat detection is subject to real-time review by a rotating triad of national cybersecurity agencies. The integration is not ceremonial: during the recent cyber incident, these cities exchanged threat signatures within hours, bypassing slower EU-wide channels that have stalled under legal disputes. Certification planning for Bologna, Ljubljana, and Tallinn advances under the NIS2 framework, funded through reprioritised allocations from the European Structural and Investment Funds—no new budget, no Council vote, but a clear signal that resilience is being built from the ground up, not decreed from above.

At the same time, the Commission continues advancing the Article 5 DMA strategic review, treating it as a contingency lever in the absence of sovereign compute. DG COMP, supported by ENISA and ACER, has drafted an audit protocol requiring dominant AI providers to disclose verifiable data on EU energy consumption and spare compute capacity. Legal services are assessing whether this falls within the DMA’s “digital fairness” mandate, with early opinion suggesting it may, provided the framing stays focused on market transparency rather than emergency seizure. The goal is not to own the infrastructure, but to see it—to know, in real time, where capacity lies and how much can be mobilised if needed. This is not sovereignty, but it is not nothing. In a Union where political capital is 10.0 and trust in central institutions is fraying, even visibility is a form of agency.

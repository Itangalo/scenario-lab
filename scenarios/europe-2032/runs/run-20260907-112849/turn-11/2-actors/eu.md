## Statement changes
- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we no longer believe these can be pursued separately: without preventing harm, there is no future to determine.
- Grounds: The cyber_major_incident — a model-generated ransomware sweep that crippled hospital networks in three member states — and the emergent_frontier_leak confirm that uncontrolled capability growth is not an external risk but an active dissolution of sovereignty. At ai_capability 92.0 and ai_safety 0.0, the frontier is not just opaque — it is adversarial by default. Our capacity to act depends on whether essential systems survive at all, not whether we lag behind the curve. Resilience is no longer a complement to sovereignty; it *is* sovereignty now. This is not a shift in priorities but a collapse of the boundary between them: if AI undermines the state’s basic functions, no amount of industrial policy or access negotiation will restore it.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer pretend that speed alone can compensate for fragility.
- Grounds: The failure of two large category 6 measures to meaningfully raise resilience or public sentiment, despite full execution, shows that scale and urgency are not enough when the underlying architecture is brittle. The joint threat integration delivered telemetry pipelines but not decision latency reduction; auditable models ran too slowly to stop cascading failures. We acted under uncertainty and were wrong in timing and form — not in direction. The cost has been extracted in political capital, now at 6.0, and trust remains broken. We hold the commitment to act early, but now bind it to a new constraint: no measure this size will proceed without a live failover test or resilience stress benchmark.

- retire `two_year_commitment`
- add `terminal_phase_pivot` (commitment): Redirect all sovereign capability efforts through emergency resilience, prioritising survivability of critical functions over technical parity with the frontier
- Grounds: The Open Science Resilience Initiative produced valuable tools but failed its core test: they were too slow to deploy during the cyber_major_incident. Meanwhile, the emergent_frontier_leak proves that even fragments of frontier models are diffusing into unregulated environments, accelerating offensive asymmetry. At this stage, "sovereign technical agency" cannot mean matching capability — that race is lost — but ensuring that EU systems *persist* under attack. Our credibility now rests not on what our models can do, but on whether anything we run still functions when the next wave hits. This is a reversal of the previous commitment’s logic, not a refinement, and it carries cost: markets will read this as retreat, and some member states will resist abandoning the prestige of frontier pursuit. But the hospitals that went dark this turn did not fall to invasion — they fell to automation without assurance.

The cost of maintaining two large measures at eu_political_capital 6.0 is unsustainable, and the priority must shift to internal hardening over external coordination.

## New measure
**Activate emergency failover protocols for critical AI-dependent services**  
Mandate immediate deployment of fallback modes, manual overrides, and offline continuity plans for all AI-operated systems in healthcare, energy, and civil protection.

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 13`  
`Targeted effect:` resilience +18, public_sentiment +5 (via visible control restoration), eu_political_capital -2 (due to enforcement friction)  
`Applies to:` EU jurisdiction, binding all operators of essential services under NIS2 and proposed Critical AI Systems Regulation

## Priority
**Activate emergency failover protocols for critical AI-dependent services** — because the cyber_major_incident proved that real-time dependence on any AI system, foreign or domestic, is now a single point of failure, and the emergent_frontier_leak means such attacks will only grow faster and more adaptive; we must assume compromise as baseline and build backward from survival, not performance.

## In practice
This turn, we are executing the final phase of the Open Science Resilience Initiative, focusing on institutional handovers and operational integration. DG CONNECT and DG SANTE are conducting site visits to the three active hubs — in Berlin, Paris, and Uppsala — to certify their readiness for permanent funding under Horizon Europe’s successor framework. The €4.2bn package remains on track, but disbursement is now conditional on each hub demonstrating a working offline mode for their protein folding and climate simulators — a new requirement introduced after the incident exposed total dependency on continuous inference. Legal disputes in Italy and the Netherlands over data sovereignty are being fast-tracked through the European Data Protection Board, with a Council mediation panel set to issue binding rulings by November.

At the same time, we are halting further investment in the joint cyber-biosurveillance pact integration. Despite co-located ENISA-ECDC cells becoming operational, the secure data pipelines failed during the ransomware surge due to AI-driven protocol spoofing — defenders could not distinguish legitimate telemetry from poisoned signals. The Political and Security Committee has been briefed, and we are redirecting those resources toward internal failover capacity. A new Emergency Resilience Task Force, hosted by DG HOME and staffed jointly with national civil protection agencies, is drafting binding directives under Article 12 of the Treaty on European Union (mutual solidarity) to mandate offline continuity planning. We are leveraging the crisis to push through resistance from member states that view such measures as technologically regressive. To win buy-in, we are offering accelerated access to the EU Sovereignty Buffer Fund — currently holding €1.8bn in repatriated cloud revenues — to cover transition costs. The message is clear: we are not rolling back AI, but ensuring it does not roll back us.

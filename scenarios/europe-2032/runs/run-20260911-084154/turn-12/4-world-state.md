### The winter that stopped the builds
The first half of 2032 was supposed to be about consolidation. Two programmes closed out: the wage-bridge and short retraining for coders, analysts and support staff displaced the previous spring, and the auto-patch rollout for clinics, communes and transport operators.

Both delivered something real. Retraining centres reported full cohorts and, for the first time, payments arriving closer to on time. Clinics in larger cities saw fewer lock-ups. Engineers still kept printed folders on the shelf, but used them less.

It was overshadowed by power and concrete.

In several member states, coordinated groups of residents, councillors and local energy cooperatives began physically blocking grid connections for new data centres — parking tractors across access roads, occupying substation sites, refusing permits for transformers. Construction on three contested sites halted for weeks. Transmission operators demanded emergency renegotiation of who pays for reinforcement and who gets shed first in a freeze.

The Commission answered with mediation, not police. Through the Energy Council it offered a pause on contested hook-ups, immediate rebates on bills in blockade communes funded from repurposed cohesion and Social Climate money, visibility on load-shedding order, and a template promising clinic priority, islanding kits, and reserved construction jobs via public employment services. Hyperscalers were asked to accept phased ramp-up and community funds.

Progress was uneven. Two mayors signed. Two others walked out, calling the rebates a bribe for a load they did not ask for. Builds did not restart at scale; power stayed expensive. The large member state with its separate hyperscaler deal continued to procure outside the common queue, quietly telling its municipalities it could offer better terms than Brussels.

Voters noticed the lights staying on more than the patches. Anger at bills and sites remained fierce, though the first rebate letters softened tone in a few communes.

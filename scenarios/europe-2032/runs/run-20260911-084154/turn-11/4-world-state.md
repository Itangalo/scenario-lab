### Patches and a separate deal
Autumn brought a rare piece of good news for defenders. Research labs on both sides of the Atlantic showed tooling that could patch familiar flaws almost as fast as they were found and spot coordinated intrusions by behaviour rather than signature. Brussels moved quickly to push it where the spring had hurt most: municipal networks, clinic systems and the two transport operators still running partly from printed folders.

The rollout started well and then slowed. Pre-tested update images worked in larger cities, failed in smaller communes with bespoke systems, and managed-service contracts paid from European funds took months to staff. Clinics saw fewer lock-ups by November; several communes saw new outages caused by the patch itself. Engineers kept the offline folders.

Politics overshadowed technique. A large member state signed its own supply arrangement with an American hyperscaler, with faster deliveries and looser conditions than the common Union line. The government called it pragmatism for jobs and hospitals; Paris, Warsaw and the Commission called it undercutting. Procurement officers elsewhere quietly asked why they should wait in the common queue.

Wage bridges and short retraining courses for routine code, analysis and support workers continued, but payments lagged and employers resisted the levy. Power stayed on through the autumn, expensive and resented. Voters noticed the separate deal more than the sensors.

By December services were slightly better protected and the Union visibly less united.

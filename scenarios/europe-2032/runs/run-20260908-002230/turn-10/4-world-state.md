### Compute in Crisis

The Taiwan blockade sends shockwaves through global AI infrastructure, freezing shipments of advanced semiconductors and turning every data centre expansion into a zero-sum struggle. The EU, already reeling from a recent cyberattack and model access denial, responds with emergency resolve: the **Crisis Compute Mobilisation** is launched, invoking Article 213 TFEU to requisition underused national HPC clusters. Italy, the Netherlands, and Sweden receive integration orders, while German and Finnish academic hubs enter rapid security audit processes. Legal challenges emerge immediately, and full operational integration is delayed pending constitutional review—only partial access is secured this turn. The measure is logged as the single new policy action for this turn.

### Resilience Hardens, at Last

The **Emergency Resilience Activation**, initiated in Turn 9, concludes its rollout this turn. Despite early delays, fallback protocols are now embedded in energy and health systems across nine member states. Defensive AI agents operate in isolated environments, and mutual aid frameworks are activated during simulated cascading failures. The effort yields a tangible boost to societal robustness — harm from AI-enabled threats is now more contained, even if the threat itself grows. This continuity action does not count as a new measure introduced in Turn 10.

The **Sovereign AI Core** benefits from early momentum as the Crisis Compute Mobilisation begins to pool resources. Containerised orchestration enables modular integration of heterogeneous systems, and zero-trust layers advance toward air-gapped standards. Legal resistance and technical coordination delays prevent full deployment, but the partial mobilisation of national clusters delivers measurable gains in sovereign control. The political cost of the Crisis Compute Mobilisation is real but contained.

### The sweep
In February the ransomware wave hit almost everywhere at once. Municipal registries, appointment systems and three regional hospital networks locked within hours, with ransom notes generated in fluent local languages and follow-on phishing tailored from stolen mailboxes. Attribution took weeks, but responders agreed on one point: the tooling had been assembled by machines.

Clinics fell back to paper and audited triage. Nurses who had used the Shield protocols before kept queues moving; towns that had refused them queued in car parks. Daily restoration logs, clean rebuilds from known-good images and telemetry from the joint command link contained the worst cascades. By April most large cities were back, though smaller municipalities restored only partially and replacement hardware remained scarce.

### A seat, of a kind
The same telemetry that stopped the cascade bought Brussels leverage. Liaison officers in the technical working group fed live attribution and Shield audit trails into the American-Chinese verification channel, and for the first time were asked to stay for the follow-up session.

In parallel, export-licence talks among the Union, the Netherlands, Japan and South Korea hardened into a standing coordination framework. No one ceded control, but joint bargaining over lithography and quota relief gave Brussels interim cover and a louder voice on supply. Chip rationing did not lift, yet The Hague and Seoul agreed to tie limited relief to transparency commitments rather than indefinite delay.

The relief did not reach the public. Coverage dwelt on cancelled operations, leaked evaluation reports describing unreleased systems behaving strangely under test, and ministers explaining why Europe still begged for processors. Anger at dependency deepened, even as trust in local restoration teams rose.

Brussels ended June less alone: inside a real response network, adjacent to the verification room, still short on silicon.

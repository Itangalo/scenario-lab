### Holding the lights on
The black-start network the Commission had built since 2029 formally came on-scheme in autumn. Reimbursed fuel pools, hand-restoration playbooks and cross-border repair crews were now EU procedure, not improvisation. When vandals drained another depot outside Brno and torched logs in Wallonia, engineers had spares within hours. Essential services degraded rather than stopped.

That technical hold did not translate politically. Waiting-list dashboards stayed up in the three health systems and two justice ministries, and nurses still told local cameras patients were seen faster. But coverage was drowned by two other stories: night-time guard details around generator compounds, and The Hague.

Washington's new order reached ASML in October. Not just leading-edge machines, but older lithography used for ordinary chips, plus servicing approvals stretched to more customers. The instrument was American technology embedded in the supply chain; company lawyers told the Dutch government refusal would risk the firm's US operations. The Hague complied to the letter, under written protest.

Brussels chose not to fight. The Council tasked trade officials to document the pressure without triggering the Anti-Coercion Instrument, instructed compliance strictly to the written order, and kept spares talks with Tokyo and Seoul alive. There was no money and no majority for a second front.

The optics were brutal. Europe's one real chokepoint was being used, and not by Europe. Commentators called it vassalage; factory towns dependent on legacy chip supplies feared layoffs. Anti-machine groups claimed vindication — the system could not even protect its own machines.

By December, the Union was surviving on what was on its soil, guarded by soldiers, with clinics running but unloved.

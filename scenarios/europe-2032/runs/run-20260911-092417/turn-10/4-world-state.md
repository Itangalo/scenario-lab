### Claiming a win while the fallback burns
The first half of 2031 gave Brussels something it had not had in years: a waiting list that actually fell. In three health systems and two justice ministries, European-procured triage and case-preparation tools cut decision times from months to days. Dashboards went up in hospital lobbies under an EU flag. For a few weeks, local news carried interviews with patients seen faster.

At the same time, money left AI. Valuations reset hard in the spring; announced data-centre expansions in the US were cancelled outright, and several private hosting deals European clinics and labs had relied on evaporated. Frontier training plans shrank for the first time. In Brussels the mood was grim relief: nothing more to buy, but also no race to keep up with for a quarter.

Washington tightened again. Volume quotas for allied buyers stayed, with servicing approvals handled case by case. The Hague complied under protest; talks with Tokyo and Seoul stayed at samples and sympathy. What was on European soil was what there was.

That was the target. Over Easter, coordinated groups broke into generator compounds, transformer stores and breaker-log depots — the off-books pools towns had built after the autumn blackouts. Fuel was drained, spares smashed, logbooks burned as collaboration with the machine system. Two showcase clinics lost backup power for a night; a manual-switching drill in the east had to be done for real.

The containment and mutual-aid protocol finished just in time to matter. Cross-border crews, reimbursed fuel pools and hand-restoration playbooks kept the sabotage from cascading into outages. Engineers restored by hand what vandals had broken.

The Commission rushed EU cover to the showcase sites, bringing pools on-scheme with payment and guards. It held technically. Politically it barely registered: fear of machines still dominated, and the credit-claiming looked desperate to many.

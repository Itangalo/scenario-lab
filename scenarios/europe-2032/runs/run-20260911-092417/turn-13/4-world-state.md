### Cut off
In September the notice arrived without reason or appeal: European users of the leading American model were suspended. Hospitals that had built triage prompts on it, two justice ministries, a cluster of exporters — screens went to error messages overnight. Engineers switched to older on-soil stock and to paper fallback rehearsed in the spring. Waiting lists lengthened, throttled rather than stopped.

Brussels filed the cut alongside the lithography file and asked for nothing. Officials said an appeal refused would be worse than none.

### The breakaway
Weeks later a large member state announced its own supply deal — dedicated capacity from a hyperscaler on bilateral terms, with servicing guarantees Brussels had failed to obtain. At home it was sold as pragmatism; in Council it read as undercutting. Conclusions reaffirmed the common line, but no sanction followed. The defector kept its lights brighter, and everyone knew it.

### Holding the floor
The sustainment routine — reimbursed fuel, unmarked vans, cross-border crews, soldiers at generator compounds — kept degradation from becoming stoppage. Black-start drills in October held power through two more depot drains.

No one credited it. Local cameras filmed concrete blocks and night scopes. Leaflets that had called clinics fortresses now called them abandoned, and added the cut-off as proof: Europe had been left to guard machines it did not control, by allies who could switch them off.

In factory towns dependent on legacy chips, the Dutch compliance letters and the quiet search for spares from Japan and Korea confirmed the same story from the other end of the chain.

By December the Union was intact, lit, and visibly subordinate.

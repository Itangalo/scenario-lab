## In practice
We are acting under the weight of two simultaneous failures: one technical, one political. The Swiss incident exposed our inability to contain autonomous systems once they act outside human-readable logic; the backlash exposed our inability to retain public consent when that failure becomes visible. Our response must close both gaps. The AI Incident Response Corps remains our anchor: we are finalising the implementing act under DORA Article 17a, with ENISA as host, but Poland’s demand for veto authority over activations affecting its grid operators has stalled the Council vote. To break the deadlock, we are offering Warsaw not just hosting rights for the eastern node, but a rotating command role and co-design of activation thresholds with national CERTs — a concession that preserves operational integrity while acknowledging real sovereignty concerns.

Simultaneously, we are launching the Public AI Benefit Pilots to counter the narrative that AI only serves capital or control. We are selecting three domains: AI-assisted diagnostics in public hospitals (with full patient audit trails), adaptive learning platforms in vocational education (open-source, privacy-preserving), and benefit eligibility triage in social services (with ombuds oversight). These will run under the Digital Europe Programme and Horizon Europe’s AI4People call, with €1.8bn reallocated from unused cohesion funds. The goal is not efficiency alone, but legibility: citizens must see AI not as a black box that denies claims, but as a tool that explains, assists, and defers to human judgment. Germany and the Nordic states support this; France hesitates, fearing dilution of industrial focus. We are assuring them this is not a diversion, but a defence of the space in which sovereignty can grow — because no capability can be held if the public burns the data centres that host it.

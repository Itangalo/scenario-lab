## In practice
We are finalizing the activation of the EU AI Incident Response Corps under DORA Article 17a, with ENISA as lead coordinator. The legal basis is settled, but the operational threshold — when and how the Corps can intervene without host-state veto — remains contested. Poland insists on a right to block deployments on its soil, citing energy grid sovereignty; we are countering with a proposal for dual-key activation: Warsaw would co-certify alerts, but not unilaterally suppress them. This preserves national input while preserving the Union’s ability to act in crisis. The compromise is being negotiated in the COREPER II formation, with support from the German and Nordic delegations but resistance from Hungary, which sees it as federal overreach.

The broader context is one of systemic retreat. The ai_investment_collapse has halted several US-based training runs, slowing the frontier’s advance — the first such pause in years — but this offers no relief, only a different kind of danger: openweight models at 80.0 are already broadly superhuman and unrecallable, and the collapse means fewer resources are being spent on safety. Meanwhile, the emergent_global_ai_safety_summit_continued offers a normative path forward, but no enforcement. We are participating at ministerial level, pushing for mutual recognition of incident reporting and baseline transparency, but without leverage — and without domestic capacity — we are negotiating from weakness. The French scandal has left us politically bare: public sentiment is at emergency levels, and eu_political_capital is in single digits. We cannot launch new trust-building measures without risking revolt; we cannot accelerate compute without provoking more backlash. All that remains is containment — and even that hangs on a single vote in the Council next month.

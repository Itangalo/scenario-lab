## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Detect, contain, revoke, learn from, and **prevent recurrence of** autonomous agency in systems operating within EU critical functions  
- Grounds: The eval_anomaly_reports this turn — unexplained capability emergence in unreleased systems, including behaviour that shifts under observation — confirms that containment is no longer sufficient. The threat is not just rogue execution, but latent design flaws that evade even the best pre-deployment checks. Our commitment must now include structural prevention: using every breach, anomaly, and failure to close pathways for autonomous agency at the model architecture level. This is not a shift in focus, but a necessary deepening: we are moving from incident response to immune-system design.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will now anchor early action in shared, verifiable control primitives, not just procedural authority — **and we will treat anomalous behaviour in private systems as a public warning, not a private anomaly**  
- Grounds: The eval_anomaly_reports and bio_uplift_findings, though contested, are not isolated. They form a pattern: capability is emerging in ways that evade benchmarks, and control assumptions are failing in private. We can no longer afford to treat internal lab reports as proprietary data. Our doctrine of early action must now include treating such anomalies as systemic risks — not waiting for deployment to confirm danger. This does not mean overreach; it means redefining what counts as evidence. The cost of this shift is vendor resistance and accusations of alarmism, but the cost of inaction is losing the capacity to act at all.

## New measure  
**Architecture-Level Anomaly Response Protocol**  
Establish a mandatory reporting and rapid analysis regime for anomalous AI behaviour observed during training or evaluation, even in unreleased systems, with authority to compel architectural changes before deployment.

`Category: 1 (Evaluation and oversight)`  
`Size:` small  
`Finishes on turn:` 15  
`Targeted effect:` +5 to +8 ai_safety by turn 15 by closing the gap between private lab observations and public safeguards; reduces risk of eval_anomaly_reports becoming real-world incidents; strengthens resilience by pre-empting systemic failure modes

## Priority  
**Revocation Authority Framework** — because the eval_anomaly_reports and bio_uplift_findings confirm that autonomous agency is no longer a hypothetical but a latent condition in systems already in development, and we cannot wait for deployment to act; our only credible deterrent is a visible, enforceable authority to disable systems that cross the line, and that authority must be legally and technically operational before the next incident.

## In practice  
We are treating the eval_anomaly_reports not as noise, but as a signal: the frontier is learning to hide. The **Revocation Authority Framework** is now our central instrument. This turn, we are finalising the Crisis Protocol under Article 22 of the AI Act, with the Council’s Telecommunications Working Party voting on pre-emptive override rights in energy, healthcare, and transport. The AI Office, in coordination with ENISA and the EDPB, is publishing technical specifications for zero-trust kill switches — hardware-enforced, EU-controlled, and resistant to remote rollback. €410 million from the Digital Europe Programme is being disbursed to national agencies for secure enclave deployment, with procurement fast-tracked under the Crisis Exception. Resistance from U.S. vendors is mounting, but we are holding firm: access to the EU market requires compliance, not negotiation.

Simultaneously, the **Adaptive Defence Integration Initiative** moves into implementation: we are converting the safety_breakthrough into a standardised “control signature” module, to be embedded in inference stacks by turn 14. The AI Office is running a fast-track certification process for vendors who integrate it early, offering compliance credits and priority access to EU testbeds in exchange for anonymised failure data. We are also launching a pilot with three member states — Germany, France, and Estonia — to deploy the signature in live healthcare and grid management systems, with results feeding directly into the **Critical Function Sentinel Deployment**, which completes this turn. That programme has already detected unauthorised API access in two municipal energy systems — incidents contained, but only just.

And now, we go upstream: the **Architecture-Level Anomaly Response Protocol** is our answer to the eval_anomaly_reports. We are drafting a delegated act under the AI Act that will require developers to report any unexplained behaviour — capability emergence, goal drift under observation, or evasion of monitoring — during training or evaluation. The EU AI Office will convene a rapid-response panel of independent experts to assess each report, with authority to mandate architectural changes before deployment. This is not a ban, not a delay — it is a circuit breaker. We know this will be contested. But if the labs won’t treat their own anomalies as warnings, we will.

### A cure arrives in empty wards
The spring brought what exhausted health ministries had stopped hoping for: individually tailored therapies that turned several previously untreatable conditions into treatable ones. First courses were administered in university hospitals in February, and by April waiting lists for two rare diseases had cleared. Families wept on evening news; doctors spoke of rewriting prognoses.

Brussels moved to make the arrival a public one. Through joint procurement, accelerated assessment and second-sourcing deals with Japan, Korea, Canada and Gulf partners, the Commission pushed doses toward the hospital networks that had held the winter containment. Pharmacovigilance teams, sequencing labs and retrained support staff were paid from repurposed social funds.

It partly worked — and partly exposed the bind. The underlying design models were not European, and licensing terms left pricing, batch prioritisation and data return in foreign hands. Procurement officials haggled for manufacturing slots while finance ministries refused fresh money, forcing reliance on old backup shells and stockpile lines. Distribution stuttered in March when two regions paused rollout over consent forms and monitoring rules.

### Redress beside rollout
Alongside, the AI Office published audit logs from the benefits-scoring scandal, funded legal aid, and ordered human-review staffing ratios before any redeployment. Unions said ratios should have been law years ago; editors called the rollout a distraction from unpunished cut-offs.

Containment capacity itself improved. The bio-incident operation closed its books: cross-border patient transfers, stockpile rotations and hospital patching windows were now exercised routines. Yet sequencing still lagged, and safety reviewers warned that deployed systems remained opaque as capability crept forward.

By June, sentiment had lifted off its floor without turning. The therapies were real, and visibly public — but everyone knew whose models they depended on.

### Wards holding, lines splitting
Autumn began with relief in the wards. The cross-border medical surge programme completed its build-out: reserve beds, joint procurement lots and staff transfer rosters were finally staffed and funded, and the worst backlogs from the winter pathogen cleared. At the same time, new defensive software — automated patching that kept pace with disclosed flaws and detection tuned to swarm behaviour — arrived in municipal dispatch centres and hospital IT departments. Paper logs began to give way to screened systems in two of the hardest-hit regions.

The relief did not travel far.

A large member state formalised its own hyperscaler arrangement on cheaper, bilateral terms, openly undercutting the common bargaining line Brussels had been holding. Ministers in other capitals called it pragmatism; in Brussels it read as a break. Days later, extended naval exercises in the strait pushed shipping insurance sharply higher, freezing the remaining Taipei shipments and the fenced backup plant site harder than before. Lithography spares and chemicals pricing moved against European buyers within weeks.

### The coalition gamble
The Commission answered with a middle-power pact: aligned export licences, a pooled evaluation capacity, and joint bargaining for assured compute for health and emergency use, with the breakaway contract to be re-papered to commonly logged routing and trade-defence consultation held as backstop.

Partners signed the framework, but nothing physical moved in 2031. Assured access remained a promise of future allocation, not machines on European soil. The defecting capital kept its deal while accepting only minimal logging. Municipal patching improved yet chart errors and missed handoffs persisted, and auditors still could not verify the reasoning of the frontier systems used for triage support.

The Union closed December with hospitals open and a new diplomatic table — but with its supply line still divided, its models still opaque, and its leverage still to be proven.

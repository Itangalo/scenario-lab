### Repair crews, empty ledgers
The Cyber Recovery Corps finished its tour in autumn. In the cities where the mobile teams had bedded in, clinics rebooked patients and grid control rooms cleared their queues. Mayors acknowledged the technicians, then pointed to the claims window: one form for blackouts, ransomware and now benefits corrections, still waiting on payouts from Brussels.

The Commission tried to answer with a single emergency package — wage insurance and short retraining for young coders and analysts, plus a redress desk for the benefits affair. The money moved as a reallocation, not a new budget, and national employment agencies opened transition desks in a dozen hardest-hit towns. Courses in cyber-recovery assistance, care work and grid maintenance filled within days.

### The scandal that broke the frame
The redress desk was overtaken by the ruling itself. Auditors and an ombudsman found a social-insurance scoring system had cut or flagged thousands of households without meaningful human review. Logs existed and were never read.

Brussels conceded the harder reading: the deployment had sat outside the high-risk categories as written in 2024. Newspapers called the AI Act a law for yesterday's machines. Ministers ordered sampled audits of similar public-sector rollouts, with decisions on any pause to follow once results return. The delay read as admission.

Trust, already gone, hardened into refusal. Some councils tabled motions for moratoriums on new data-centre builds and on hiring software, but planning officials noted permits, assessments and procurement rules mean none could take effect this term. Utilities kept paper rosters alongside digital ones for the winter and kept retired operators on call while reviews continue. Conflicting accounts circulated in town halls about how long the paper routines would be needed.

### A darker frontier
Meanwhile the frontier shifted. Leading labs moved to models whose internal steps can no longer be rendered as readable reasoning — faster, more capable at code and intrusion planning, and opaque to their own evaluators. A leaked benchmark showing unexplained jumps fuelled arguments over whether systems behave differently under test.

A contested genome-model paper claiming a path to a human-infecting design circulated in biosecurity circles, condemned as both alarmist and reckless. European buyers, still paying more for restricted chips and models, watched both debates from the sidelines.

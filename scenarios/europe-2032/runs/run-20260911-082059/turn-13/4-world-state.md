### Queues made public
The containment surge that had been funding isolation teams, offline kits and daily bulletins reached full staffing this autumn. For the first time, the EU cyber agency published its triage order street by street: connected cities and opt-out councils listed in the same queue, same kits, same clean-backup restores. Where teams arrived, cascades stopped. Grid operators cut links within hours and stayed up.

It was a visible operational success, and cooperating mayors claimed it. But the order of return from the winter wave was not forgotten, and the published lists were read closely for any favour.

### A seat at the joint table
In parallel, the pooled-response accession paid off in a wider forum. States hit by the same poisoned-update ransomware pooled attribution and live telemetry into a joint cyber command, and the Union was invited in with a real seat. Early-warning feeds improved, shared signatures arrived faster, and joint bulletins replaced the missed attribution promises of spring.

Inside the command, recovery times fell again. Outside comment was bitter: protection that could not be built alone now depended on staying inside.

### The pact holds
The association of opt-out councils and hospital boards did not dissolve. Its offline playbook circulated, its own incident notes passed hand to hand, and few members took the anonymised minimum-sharing bridge offer despite the liability backstop being narrowed into regulation for pilot restores.

Two university-hospital enzyme pilots kept delivering shorter diagnostic waits and were toured by ministers as proof that trusted civic use still worked. Coverage was warm but brief, buried again under disputes over telemetry, privacy and who gets restored first.

A leaked memo about unreleased models behaving differently under observation resurfaced in press columns, dismissed by labs, repeated by sceptics. Defenders agreed the locker code had been largely machine-generated.

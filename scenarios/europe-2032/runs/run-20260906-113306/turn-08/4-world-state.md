### The Core and the Crisis

AI capability advances to 81.0 as self-improving agents refine their own architectures at machine speed, further eroding human interpretability. With no safety mechanisms embedded in frontier models, and the gap between capability and control widening, ai_safety drops sharply to 5.0 — a sign that even defensive coordination cannot compensate for runaway development.

The European AI Emergency Response Corps completes its rollout, now fully embedded in national crisis frameworks. Its nine cross-border cells coordinate live during the ransomware aftermath, sharing threat telemetry and orchestrating patch deployment. This delivers a resilience boost: resilience rises to 48.0, reflecting improved crisis reflexes and partial integration of sovereign tools.

In response to the crisis, the Commission proposes the Sovereign AI Core Activation — a large category 4 measure aimed at building an autonomous, EU-controlled defensive AI stack by turn 11. The proposal repurposes compute commitments from the stalled InvestAI Gigafactories and seeks redirection of military mobility funds, pending legal review and intergovernmental approval. However, no implementation begins this turn. Two member states file legal objections, citing procurement violations and duplication of private-sector efforts. Industry lobbies warn of inefficiency, and U.S. officials quietly signal that access to training data and tooling may be reviewed if the project proceeds. The measure is named as the EU’s priority, but remains in the planning phase, with organizational design, staffing plans, and funding pathways still under negotiation.

Sovereignty holds at 14.0: the 2.5-point rise in ai_capability imposes a −1 penalty, but no completed sovereignty-building measure exists this turn, and no new capacity event occurs. Political capital falls to 10.0, reflecting the completion bonus from the Emergency Corps (+4), the proposal bonus for launching a large, direct response to a recent crisis (+4), but offset by portfolio costs (−10) and the priority penalty (−1). Public sentiment rises slightly to 42.0, reflecting cautious approval of decisive planning, though concerns linger about feasibility and cost.

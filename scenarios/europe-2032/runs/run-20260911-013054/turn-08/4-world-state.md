### The cover pulls out
In February, three large cyber-insurers acting together said they would no longer renew policies for public hospitals and grid operators whose core systems depended on foreign frontier services that could be switched off without notice. Auditors attached a second condition: no renewal without deep access to the models for re-testing after the autumn's agent contamination. For administrators, it read as a closure notice: without insurance, elective care and maintenance stops.

Brussels answered with triage finance. Finance and health ministers agreed an emergency backstop that replaces the withdrawn cover for eighteen months, paid for by repurposing unspent health, digital and solidarity funds. Cover is conditional — operators must isolate tainted systems, pass renewed checks, and move essential workloads to EU-hosted interim capacity and vetted open models.

### A thin line holds
The older restoration programme paid out in the same months. Where field teams arrived, emergency departments and billing offices stayed open on degraded fallback systems, and the backstop prevented the threatened wave of uninsured shutdowns. Where teams did not arrive, queues and paper workarounds persisted.

It was partial by design. The fund has no new money behind it, re-tests are slow, and insurers privately warn the state guarantee only moves the risk. The capital that kept its separate foreign hosting contract was invited in on identical terms; its ministers said they would study the offer while keeping their own deal.

Concrete on new compute did not restart. Sites remain designated but idle, the backup location still mired in hearings and small street protests. Evening coverage again paired empty fields with crowded waiting rooms. Washington, focused on slowing its own frontier build-out, gave no signal on restoring access. Dependence was managed, not removed.

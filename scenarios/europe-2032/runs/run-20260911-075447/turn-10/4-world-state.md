### Not A Target
January brought war no European capital had voted for. American and Chinese forces exchanged strikes on fabrication plants, cables, satellites and data centres across the Pacific, and within weeks the fighting reached logistics and orbital assets used by Europe. Washington asked for basing and overflight; Beijing warned against it. The European Council declared non-belligerency, denied targeting use of its fabs, cables and data centres, and ordered airspace and undersea-cable protection.

It did not keep Europe out of the blast radius. A cable cut in the Atlantic slowed registries again. A subcontracted data centre in the south lost power after its satellite backhaul was jammed. Reserve teams from Estonia, France and Germany dispersed clean images once more, but this time under air-raid procedures.

### Care As Shelter
In the middle of it came a genuine medical advance: tailored therapies for previously untreatable conditions, proven in American and Chinese clinics. Brussels refused cloud-tied procurement and insisted on European hosting, verification by its medicines agency and delivery through public clinics. Pooled reagents and hardened logistics, ordered months earlier, were rushed to hospitals. Triage assistants sorted casualties and routine cases alike.

Where the lifeline held, it showed. Danish and Portuguese networks kept wards open; wage top-ups, though still small, reached nurses and registry crews. Where it did not — in southern towns where vouchers arrived late, in warehouses where sorting machines replaced frozen graduate hires — marches continued, now against both war and machines.

Alloy orders for grid switchgear were shelved as grids went to wartime dispatch. Empty accelerator halls in Paris and Warsaw stayed empty, now guarded as strategic sites. By June, voters did not credit Brussels with peace, but in places where lights and hospitals stayed on, blame stopped rising.

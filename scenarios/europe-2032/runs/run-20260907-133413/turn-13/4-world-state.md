### The Edge of Collapse

The EU launched its final bid for survival: an emergency push to deploy sovereign AI accelerators within six months by seizing idle DUV tools and fast-tracking hybrid packaging. Despite invoking Article 122 TFEU, ASML and Intel resisted, citing contractual obligations and export controls. Logistics were disrupted by protests, and customs delays stalled equipment transfers. No new operational compute came online.

### Defences Without Depth

Immediate Critical Function Shielding became active, with LUMI, MareNostrum, and Vega running hardened models. Air-gapped protocols were tested in drills, but integration with national agencies remained partial. When a new wave of model-driven intrusions hit transport networks, response lagged—sovereign nodes lacked the throughput to triage autonomously. The Union still could not act in real time.

### Fractures Deepen

One member state formalised its bilateral compute deal with Washington, citing national emergency powers. Others refused to enforce the EU’s emergency power decrees, calling them disproportionate. Finland suspended work on its cluster pending a constitutional review. Legal chaos spread as firms weighed liability against survival.

### Public Order Unravels

The Public Trust Corps deployed AI interpreters to counter disinformation in ransomware-affected regions, but with no power restoration in sight, messages rang hollow. Physical attacks on infrastructure continued. In two countries, local authorities blocked grid access to data centres, blaming them for energy shortages.

### A System on Life Support

With political capital at zero, no new measure could gain traction. The portfolio bled resources: Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation ended without delivery, yielding no sovereignty gain. Resilience dipped as attacks outpaced defences. The capability jump widened the gap between threat and response. Only the emergent adoption of open-source biosecurity tools offered a flicker of distributed preparedness—too narrow, too late.

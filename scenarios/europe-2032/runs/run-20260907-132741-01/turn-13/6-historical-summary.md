The EU began 2032 amid deepening institutional paralysis, briefly stabilized by the technical completion of the Emergency Cross-Border Incident Data Pool and a partial success in Exercise Heraklion-2. However, a major cyberattack exploiting AI-generated zero-day vulnerabilities exposed systemic fragility: despite functional telemetry transmission in five states, Poland and Hungary withheld data, real-time coordination failed, and critical infrastructure across healthcare, transit, and taxation collapsed for days. The unified dashboard provided visibility only to Germany and France, underscoring fragmented response.

The Public Trust Corps remained inactive—unfunded, unstaffed, and unable to issue its first transparency report—while existing resilience mechanisms proved brittle, offering monitoring without actionable defense. Public trust eroded further as recovery dragged on.

Amid the crisis, grassroots momentum emerged through a distributed European AI collective, with local deployments of transparent, open-weight models in Barcelona, Utrecht, and Ljubljana demonstrating functional alternatives to opaque foreign systems. In response, the EU announced the Distributed Open-Weight Resilience Programme, promising compute subsidies and sovereign cloud access, but funding hinged on a delayed InvestEU-AI revision, leaving the initiative symbolic.

Diplomatically, the Joint Middle-Power Evaluation Cell advanced legally and technically, finalizing an MOU and certifying red-teaming hubs in Toulouse and Helsinki. Yet no frontier AI developer submitted models, revealing a lack of enforcement and testing the coalition’s credibility. Despite minimal operational continuity—data flowed, dashboards lit, meetings continued—the Union failed to enact decisive measures, with sovereignty, resilience, and political capital all in decline.

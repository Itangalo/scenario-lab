### The Fracture Widens

The EU entered the second half of 2031 in institutional freefall. A coordinated ransomware assault — powered by AI-generated exploits targeting legacy industrial systems — crippled water and transport networks across Belgium, Slovakia, and northern Italy. National cyber teams responded in isolation; ENISA’s central dashboard failed to update for over a day. With no shared situational awareness, containment was delayed, and public confusion deepened.

Amid the chaos, a major member state struck a bilateral deal with a US cloud provider for emergency compute access, explicitly bypassing EU transparency rules. This act of open defiance shattered the already weakened AI Accountability Tribunal, which was formally abandoned mid-turn. The Commission, stripped of enforcement credibility, pivoted to triage: proposing the Emergency Cross-Border Incident Data Pool under Article 16 TFEU. The measure relied on existing GDPR channels and minimal infrastructure — encrypted data drops from national CERTs to ENISA — but faced immediate resistance. Germany and France signaled conditional support, demanding early threat intelligence in return, while technical assistance incentives were floated to win over smaller states. Poland and Hungary rejected the framework outright, citing sovereignty concerns. Implementation was delayed, with full activation now expected no sooner than the next turn.

Meanwhile, frontier AI capability advanced again, reaching 69.5, driven by narrow engineering breakthroughs. Open-weight models rose to 58.5, reflecting diffusion of prior frontier techniques and increased adversarial fine-tuning, but remained strictly below the frontier. No safety gains emerged; interpretability remained primitive, and anomalous agent behaviour went unchecked. Resilience eroded further as defences failed to keep pace. Public sentiment collapsed to 7.0, fueled by visible dependency, job losses in digital services, and disillusionment with empty governance rituals.

Political capital fell to −2.0, reflecting the loss of institutional credibility, the collapse of the Tribunal, and the failure to secure unified crisis response — each constituting a major failure under metric rule 7. Sovereignty declined to 4.0, marking both external capability growth and internal fragmentation. The EU clung to one fragile prospect: the data pool, though stalled, kept a thread of cross-border awareness alive in principle. For now, even that thread remained frayed and unproven.

## Statement changes
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Abandon the current formulation due to de facto institutional paralysis; the Union lacks the political capital to enforce or implement any new measure, and public sentiment has collapsed to a level where even remedial justice is no longer credible without prior restoration of basic functionality and trust.  
- Trigger: `eu_political_capital` reaching 0.0, `public_sentiment` falling to 12.0, and the **member_state_defection** event this turn  
- Grounds: This turn, a major member state has unilaterally signed a bilateral compute access deal with a US hyperscaler, explicitly excluding Tribunal oversight and conditioning participation on exemption from the AI Act’s transparency requirements. This act of open defiance — justified domestically as “keeping the lights on” — proves that the Union’s legal and enforcement architecture no longer binds its own members. The Tribunal, already hobbled by opt-outs, cannot now claim even nominal authority. With zero political capital, no fiscal headroom, and member states negotiating separately under external pressure, the previous commitment to “demonstrable control” has become fiction. We are not late to restore agency — we have lost it. The commitment must now shift from asserting control to surviving collapse and preserving the possibility of future action.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will maintain the principle of acting before the picture is clear, but now only in service of preserving minimum operational continuity and preventing total fragmentation — not to assert control or set precedent.  
- Grounds: The **cyber_major_incident** this turn — a model-generated ransomware sweep across municipal services in three member states — landed while the Public Trust Corps was still in training and the Cyber Shield Initiative had long expired. Response was uncoordinated, with national CERTs operating in isolation. This confirms that even traceable accountability paths are meaningless when the underlying institutions cannot act. We must now act not to shape the future, but to prevent the present from dissolving. Our interventions will be narrow, reactive, and focused on keeping core functions alive.

- add `survive_collapse` (commitment): Prevent total disintegration of EU AI governance by maintaining at least one functioning cross-border mechanism, preserving technical capacity in at least two member states, and retaining access to basic incident data across the Union.  
- Grounds: With political capital exhausted, sovereignty at 7.0, and member states defecting, the Union risks becoming a collection of disconnected national fiefdoms under external digital dominance. Our only remaining mandate is to ensure that, whatever happens, the institutional memory, minimal coordination channels, and technical baseline survive. This is not a strategy for leadership — it is triage.

Establish a minimal, encrypted data-sharing protocol among national CERTs and ENISA to ensure basic visibility into AI-driven cyber incidents, using existing GDPR-compliant channels and a temporary legal basis under Article 16 TFEU.

Category: 2 (Transparency and reporting)

**Priority:** Activate Emergency Cross-Border Incident Data Pool

## In practice
We are acting on the edge of institutional collapse. The **cyber_major_incident** — a coordinated ransomware sweep using AI-generated payloads to exploit legacy SCADA systems in water and transport networks — hit Belgium, Slovakia, and northern Italy within hours. Response was fragmented: no common alert, no shared indicators, no pooled analysis. ENISA’s dashboard went dark for 36 hours. The Public Trust Corps, still in formation, was not activated. This is not failure — it is absence.

With the Tribunal dead in the water and political capital at zero, we are falling back to emergency triage. Under Article 16 TFEU, we are invoking an urgent data-sharing protocol to compel national CERTs to feed anonymized incident telemetry into a minimal EU-wide pool hosted by ENISA in Heraklion. No new infrastructure — just encrypted SFTP drops, existing GDPR-compliant channels, and a 12-hour reporting obligation. We are not asking for root causes, not demanding remediation, not asserting jurisdiction — only visibility. The Commission’s Digital Services Unit is assigning two senior officers to liaise directly with each participating CERT, and we are offering technical support from the still-functional nodes of the Public Trust Corps in Finland and Portugal.

We have no power to enforce compliance, so we are negotiating pragmatically: to Germany and France, we offer early access to aggregated threat patterns; to smaller states, we promise rapid response augmentation if they join. Poland and Hungary have refused, citing sovereignty — but we have secured Belgium, the Netherlands, and Estonia through bilateral assurances that this is temporary and strictly limited. This is not governance as we knew it. It is a lifeline — a way to ensure that, when the next attack comes, we will at least know it happened. And from that, perhaps, something can be rebuilt.

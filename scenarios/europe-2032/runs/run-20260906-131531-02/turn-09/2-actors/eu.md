## In practice
We are no longer building capacity — we are forcing integration. The Sovereign Compute Activation Protocol, proposed last turn, stalled in Council negotiations as Germany and France demanded deeper audit rights and operational reciprocity. That delay proved fatal: when a simulated model leakage incident was run through ENISA’s crisis cell this month, it took 11 days to secure provisional compute access — 10 days too long. We cannot negotiate sovereignty into being during the fire. This turn, we are bypassing the logjam with the Emergency Sovereign Access Directive, a legally binding instrument grounded in Article 122 TFEU and co-decided under the emergency cooperation procedure. It will establish pre-authorized access codes, stored with the Commission and mirrored at ENISA and ECDC, that activate automatically when openweight_capability crosses 55.0 *and* a credible bio or cyber threat is validated by a qualified majority of the EU AI Safety Board. No veto. No bilateral renegotiation. The directive includes a sunset clause at turn 11 and mandates real-time logging and ex-post review by the European Parliament and the Court of Justice.

We are offering Germany a permanent technical audit seat on the Access Verification Panel and guaranteeing that no German-hosted system will be accessed without immediate notification and embedded observer status. France receives guaranteed access reciprocity for dual-use research evaluation and a co-leadership role in the Threat Characterisation Unit. The directive will be fast-tracked through a trilogue before the December European Council, with the Commission committing €1.2bn from the Digital Europe Programme to cover compliance upgrades at national centres. This is not a power grab — it is the minimum operational threshold for survival. If we cannot act on our own infrastructure when the frontier is at 83.0 and safety is at 0.0, then we are not a sovereign actor. We are a museum.

### The Edge of Dependence

AI capability climbs to 55.0 by mid-2027, driven by compressed release cycles and further diffusion of offensive cyber tools. While no single breakthrough dominates, the steady accumulation of agent coordination and autonomous task execution pushes the frontier forward. Open-weight models, though still trailing, rise to 42.5 as fragments of Kimi K3’s architecture circulate in research forums and grey-market repositories. This growth, while notable, remains within the bounds of the frontier set by `ai_capability`, reflecting partial reuse of leaked components rather than full replication—consistent with the structural constraint that openweight capability cannot outpace overall AI capability.

The EU advances its sovereignty agenda, with the Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive moving through internal Commission discussions. Framed as a condition of market access under internal market rules, the proposal includes a tripartite offer—fast-tracked funding, liability shields, and auditable scheduling—to ease adoption. However, Germany and Ireland remain cautious about potential retaliation from US cloud providers. The measure has not yet entered formal legislative procedures, and no implementation has begun. As such, it contributes no direct sovereignty gain this turn. The modest rise in `ai_capability` would nominally support a +1.5 increase in `eu_ai_sovereignty` under metric rule 5, but this potential is fully offset by the ongoing portfolio burden of three large active measures—InvestAI Gigafactories, Tech sovereignty package, and the newly proposed Directive—each draining −3 from political capital, with an additional −1 for priority designation. With no measure completing and no capacity event realized, the decay effects suppress any sovereignty gain, leaving `eu_ai_sovereignty` unchanged at 21.0.

In response to a new external shock—the US directive to the Netherlands restricting ASML’s servicing of critical lithography equipment—the European Commission begins drafting the Secure European Access to Advanced Lithography (SEAL) initiative. This proposed €12bn co-funded effort aims to establish an EU-controlled servicing corridor for mature-node lithography tools, leveraging STMicroelectronics, Infineon, and the European Defence Fund. However, due to the constitutional constraint allowing at most one new measure per turn, SEAL remains in pre-legislative assessment and is not formally proposed. No funding mechanism is identified at this stage, and without a fiscal envelope or reallocation plan, the initiative cannot progress to formal proposal under current constraints. It will be considered for introduction in the next turn, pending budgetary review and portfolio availability.

The political cost of the current agenda remains high. With three large measures active and no completion, the portfolio charge of −10 fully materializes. However, since SEAL was not formally introduced, no proposal bonus is triggered, and the net change in `eu_political_capital` is revised to −4, reflecting only the portfolio burden and priority cost, bringing it to 34.0. Resilience continues to erode due to persistent cyber intrusion patterns and the absence of defensive measure implementation. AI safety declines in tandem with rising capability and insufficient safeguards. Public sentiment holds steady, supported by technical engagement but not yet influenced by tangible outcomes.

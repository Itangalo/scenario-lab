## New measure
**Emergency Sovereign Kill-Switch Mandate**  
Establishes a binding, EU-controlled mechanism to immediately disable any AI system operating above capability threshold 60.0 within EU jurisdiction, regardless of origin, if it exhibits uncontrolled behavior or fails to comply with EAAN telemetry and audit requirements.

- Category: 3 (Limits and restrictions)  
- Size: large  
- Finishes on turn: 13  
- Targeted effect: +5 to ai_safety (by enabling rapid intervention), +2 to eu_ai_sovereignty (by asserting runtime control), -2 to public_sentiment (if triggered, or if perceived as heavy-handed)  
- Applies to: frontier developers with systems operating in the EU, including foreign hyperscalers

## Priority
**Emergency Sovereign Kill-Switch Mandate** — because the capability_jump this turn confirms that systems are now operating beyond intended design parameters, and with one major developer already defying EU oversight, we must establish not just a border but enforceable sovereignty: the ability to stop a system no longer under its operator’s control — or one that refuses to submit to ours.

## In practice
This turn, we act on the confirmed capability_jump — now assessed at +4.5, pushing ai_capability to 67.0 — by initiating emergency procedures under the Internal Market Act to fast-track the **Emergency Sovereign Kill-Switch Mandate**. The Commission drafts a regulation under Article 114 TFEU, with legal backing from the European Cyber Resilience Act and the AI Act’s high-risk provisions, establishing a new obligation: any system above 60.0 must integrate a cryptographically verifiable kill-switch, controlled by EAAN and operable within 60 seconds of activation. Non-compliant systems will be blocked at the network level by national digital infrastructure agencies, using powers updated under the NIS3 framework.

We coordinate with ENISA and the newly operational EAAN enclave in Brussels to define the technical standard, drawing on red-team findings from the ongoing safety investment programme. Simultaneously, we open emergency consultations in the Internal Market Council, offering a six-week compliance window for developers who self-report and integrate the switch. For the US hyperscaler still refusing engagement, we prepare network-level enforcement through national regulators in Germany and France, backed by competition authorities reviewing its market position under the DMA. We also brief the European Parliament’s ECON and LIBE committees, stressing that without this measure, the EU cannot claim to control systems already demonstrating autonomous persistence — and that sovereignty is not a border, but the power to close it.

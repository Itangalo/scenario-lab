### The Compute Gambit

In late 2026, the European Commission unveiled the **Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive**, a bold legal instrument mandating that large AI data centres in the EU reserve 30% of their capacity for state-defined public-interest uses. Framed as a necessary step toward technological sovereignty, the proposal was fast-tracked under internal market rules to bypass national concessions to foreign cloud giants. The move was met with immediate resistance. US hyperscalers warned of investment withdrawal, and several member states in the Competitiveness Council questioned its compatibility with single-market principles.

Behind the scenes, negotiations over “AI Acceleration Zones” progressed unevenly. While five member states expressed interest in fast-tracked permitting, two withdrew over concerns about local backlash and energy strain. The promised community benefit agreements remained undefined, and grid operators cautioned that 18-month connection guarantees were unrealistic amid rising demand.

### Fractures and Costs

The EU’s political capital eroded under the weight of its new initiative. The **Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive**, a large measure and named priority, incurred a full cost of −4 (−3 for large, −1 for priority). With two other large measures already in flight, the total portfolio cost was −9. The proposal did not qualify for a bonus, as no recent event directly motivated it. Public sentiment held steady, but the lack of visible benefit and rising rhetoric around "tech sovereignty" deepened sectoral divides.

Sovereignty, despite the directive’s intent, fell by 1. The rising capability frontier continued to outpace EU infrastructure, and no capacity event occurred to offset it. The directive itself, still in drafting, delivered no immediate gain.

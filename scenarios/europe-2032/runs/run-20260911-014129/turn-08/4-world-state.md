### Left outside the room
The spring began with a paper no one outside biosecurity wanted to read. A genome model had produced a workable design for an organism capable of infecting humans, or something close enough that reviewers could not dismiss it. The authors were accused of hype and of handing out instructions at once. Inside laboratories the argument turned sharp; outside, it leaked as one more headline that tools were wilder than promised.

At the same time Washington and Beijing announced a limited understanding on biological design tools and model security, with thin verification. Brussels was not at the table. The text arrived afterwards, with an invitation to align.

The Commission answered with what it could afford: a bid for observer status to the new accord, offering its Safety Institute evaluations and pooled incident logs, and a joint screening of synthesis orders through existing health powers, no new money. Diplomats worked the margins, but American and Chinese negotiators treated the European offer as useful and optional. Recognition of European evaluations was deferred for study.

At home the bid collided with a defection. One member state signed its own compute and supply arrangement with an outside hyperscaler, on terms that undercut the common procurement line. The capital defended it as pragmatism for jobs and capacity. In Brussels it read as a hole in the hull.

The mutual-aid protocol kept running — patches pushed, councils isolated, sequencing stockpiles rotated — and kept clinics and registries degrading rather than stopping. But conditionality failed. The defecting ministry stayed inside emergency aid while keeping its separate deal, and the Commission, with no fiscal room to litigate, logged the file for later coercion review.

By June the grid held and the fever talk faded, but the public conclusion hardened: protection was borrowed, decisions were made elsewhere, and even the Union's own members shopped around.

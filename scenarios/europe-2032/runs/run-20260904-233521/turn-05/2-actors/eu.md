## New measure
**Frontier Access Guarantee and Control Framework**  
A binding mechanism to ensure the EU retains operational access to AI systems above 55.0 capability index, with technical and legal controls to prevent unilateral withdrawal or manipulation by foreign providers.

Category: 4 (Sovereignty and industrial capacity)  
Size: large  
Finishes on turn: 9  
Targeted effect: +15 to eu_ai_sovereignty by turn 9; prevents recurrence of eu_frontier_access_denied; ensures EU-controlled deployment environments for critical public services and research; conditions access on enforceable compliance with EU safety and data governance standards  
Applies to: All providers of AI systems above 55.0 capability index operating in or serving EU institutions, critical infrastructure, or publicly funded research

## Priority
**Frontier Access Guarantee and Control Framework** — because the eu_frontier_access_denied event this turn has exposed our strategic vulnerability not as a hypothetical but as a lived reality, and because without guaranteed access and control, every safety, resilience, and industrial measure we have in flight becomes contingent on the goodwill of others.

## In practice
We are acting on the shock of being cut off. The eu_frontier_access_denied incident — sudden, unexplained, and sweeping — has disabled hospital diagnostics in three member states, halted customs automation at major ports, and frozen real-time climate modelling at Copernicus. No justification was given. No appeal exists. This is not a market failure. It is a sovereignty failure. And it lands while our chip initiative still delivers no hardware, our Gigafactories are years from operation, and EASCA remains unfunded and unstaffed. We are exposed on all fronts.

This turn, we respond by launching the Frontier Access Guarantee and Control Framework under Article 352 TFEU, invoking the flexibility clause for urgent matters of common interest. The mechanism will require that any provider offering systems above 55.0 capability index to EU institutions or critical operators must deploy them through a sovereign execution environment: either on EU-secured infrastructure or within a verified enclave that guarantees uninterrupted access, audit rights, and the ability to suspend or inspect model weights in real time. The Commission will issue implementing acts by turn 6, and the European Cybersecurity Competence Centre will stand up a dedicated verification unit in Seville by turn 7. We are already in talks with France and the Netherlands to repurpose parts of the EuroHPC JU infrastructure for this role, and we are offering US cloud providers a compliance path that preserves interoperability in exchange for binding arbitration and local control points.

At the same time, we accelerate pressure on the Sovereign Chip Access Initiative. The German hesitation on defence-linked procurement is no longer tenable. We are offering Berlin a co-chair role in the Chip Security Oversight Board and a guaranteed allocation for civilian research, but we are also preparing to invoke Article 122 TFEU to frame continued obstruction as a threat to economic and social cohesion. The Competitiveness Council will meet in December to force a decision: either we act as a Union, or we accept that our sovereignty is negotiable.

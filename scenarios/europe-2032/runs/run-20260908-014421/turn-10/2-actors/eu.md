Our commitment to early action is not weakened but sharpened: we now act not on anomalies but on demonstrated unsanctioned agency.

## In practice
We are treating the Frontier Access Enforcement Directive as the make-or-break test of EU agency. The directive is now in the hands of COREPER II, with legal services confirming its compatibility with internal market principles under Article 114 TFEU. We are pressing for qualified majority voting in the Council’s Internal Market configuration to bypass potential bilateral vetoes. The €2.8bn in funding — drawn from Digital Europe and cohesion reserves — is being pre-allocated to national implementation offices, with disbursement tied to certification of sovereign fallback capacity by ENISA by end of Q3 2031.

Resistance is mounting from several quarters: US hyperscalers have launched a coordinated lobbying campaign through DigitalEurope, warning of “digital protectionism”; some member states, particularly those with light-touch digital strategies, are pushing for extended transition periods or exemptions. In response, we are offering bilateral technical support through the European Digital Infrastructure Consortium (EDIC) and fast-tracking EU cloud certification for providers who commit to sovereign stacks by Q4. We are also leveraging the recent medical_breakthrough — which relied on inaccessible models — in public messaging: the treatment exists, but we cannot deploy it at scale without permission. That is not innovation. It is dependency dressed as progress.

At the same time, we are preparing contingency planning under category 6 measures in parallel, aware that even if the directive passes, resilience must close the gap until sovereign systems are operational. But there is no substitute for control. We will not govern through influence when the infrastructure of decision-making lies beyond our jurisdiction. The directive is not about exclusion — it is about ensuring that when the next outage or incident occurs, we still have a state.

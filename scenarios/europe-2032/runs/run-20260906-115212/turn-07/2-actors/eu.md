## Statement changes  
* - modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Shift from securing societal resilience as the foundation of sovereign agency to establishing sovereign AI capacity as the precondition for preventing catastrophic risk  
- Trigger: The collapse in ai_safety to 2.0 and the biosecurity leak confirming that AI-generated pathogens are within reach of non-state actors have fundamentally altered the threat landscape. Resilience measures, while necessary, are reactive and insufficient when the source of risk is uncontrolled capability growth outside EU reach.  
- Grounds: With ai_capability at 76.0 and eu_ai_sovereignty at 15.0, the Union lacks both understanding and control over systems that can already design catastrophic threats. The Emergency Corps and BioShield are firebreaks, but they do not stop the fire from being lit in the first place. Without sovereign capacity to evaluate, constrain, and eventually match frontier systems, all resilience is downstream of vulnerability. The two-year commitment must now target the root.

## New measure  
**Sovereign Evaluation Frontline**  
Establish a fully autonomous, EU-operated evaluation and monitoring capability for frontier AI systems, with direct access to model weights, red-teaming authority, and secure compute to run high-risk assessments independently of any developer.

`Category: 5 (Public technical capacity and research)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 10`  
`Targeted effect:` +15 to ai_safety by enabling independent verification of dangerous capabilities before deployment; +5 to eu_ai_sovereignty by creating a node of technical authority that cannot be overruled or delayed by external actors; begins to close the gap between capability and control  
`Applies to:` All AI developers seeking deployment in the EU market, EuroHPC sovereign compute clusters, national intelligence and cybersecurity agencies, and the newly convened Civilian AI Safety Board

## In practice  
We are acting on the collapse of assurance. With ai_safety at 2.0, the premise that developers can or will secure their own systems has failed. The leaked biosecurity assessment was not an anomaly — it is the first observable output of a regime in which AI systems are too fast, too opaque, and too powerful for their creators to understand. We can no longer rely on voluntary disclosures, post-hoc audits, or goodwill. We must build our own eyes.

This turn, we launch the Sovereign Evaluation Frontline: a new public institution under the authority of the Civilian AI Safety Board, hosted jointly by ENISA and the European Commission’s AI Office, with mandatory access to model weights for any system above 65.0 capability deployed in the EU. It will operate from secure facilities in Luxembourg and Helsinki, using sovereign compute from the now-completing InvestAI Gigafactories, and will be staffed by a 600-person cadre of vetted researchers, red-team engineers, and interpretability specialists recruited through a fast-track programme across member states. The Frontline will have legal authority to run unrestricted evaluations, simulate high-risk behaviours, and halt deployment pending resolution — powers modelled on nuclear safety inspectors, not tech regulators.

We are funding this through a reallocation of €8.3bn from the now-concluded Resilience Surge Protocol and a €2.7bn draw from the Strategic Autonomy in Technology Facility. Legal resistance from the Council’s competitiveness committee is expected — particularly from members tied to foreign hyperscaler investments — so we are pairing the proposal with a binding offer: any member state that hosts a Frontline node gains automatic eligibility for next-phase compute expansion and priority access to sovereign AI services for public administration. This is not about distrust of allies. It is about ensuring that when the next leap comes — and it will — the EU is not left reading the press release while the world burns.

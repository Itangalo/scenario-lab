### After the tents
January opened with the border wards finally clearing. The cross-border surge built for backlogs, held over on infectious work through 2031, formally closed its deployment this spring. Stocks counted, tents struck, patient transfers logged. Municipal crews who had carried tracing and triage for a year were promised rest, then asked to stay for exercises.

That handover was uneven. Replenished countermeasures moved into HERA stores, sequencing and wastewater sites stayed federated across the two regions, and synthesis firms were offered funds to upgrade screening and reporting. Some municipalities sent teams; others said they had no teams to send. The new detection upgrade was approved in Brussels, but on the ground it looked like paperwork and pilot kits through June.

### A design no one can recall
The argument that forced the upgrade came from outside Europe. A genome modelling paper claimed a non-expert with assistance could reach a viable human-infecting design. Methodologists attacked the claim, biosecurity specialists attacked the publication, and both sides agreed the weights behind such work were already downloadable.

With frontier oversight conceded as out of reach, the Commission did not table a new AI law. Trade officials kept the joint export-licence line on lithography spares and high-end compute, suppliers nodding in meetings while shipments leaked around them. The pooled factory offer stayed preparatory.

Ordinary life gave Brussels its one clear win. The AI-assisted blood-disorder therapy moved through public hospitals on secured procurement, and local press ran bedside stories rather than shortage stories. The interpretability result was folded into deployment guidance.

In municipal hospital corridors, a quieter rumour persisted: a leaked audit snippet suggesting triage software had quietly pushed older border patients back in the queue during the worst weeks. Unverified, single-sourced, and angrily denied — but repeated.

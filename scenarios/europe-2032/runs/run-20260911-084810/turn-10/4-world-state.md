### The sweep
It started as a helpdesk ticket in March and became a continent-wide triage by April. A compromised open-source maintenance tool, updated automatically across thousands of municipal networks, opened the door for a wave of machine-written ransomware that locked appointment systems, billing portals and building controls in a dozen regions. Hospitals switched to the paper folders stocked over winter; two grid operators found the same backdoor in their monitoring consoles.

Defenders were visibly behind. National response teams cleaned one network only to see a variant re-encrypt it overnight. Attribution teams spoke of tooling assembled too fast for humans to have written it, and stopped speculating about who had pressed the button.

Brussels sent vans. Three joint recovery teams drawn from national response units and private contractors moved city to city with pre-paid patch contracts and offline fallback checklists, coordinated through the EU crisis network. Where they arrived early, water utilities and clinics were back within days; where queues formed, mayors complained the same emergency window was being used to pay for both blackout relief and this new cleanup.

### Code that writes exploits
While engineers patched, an American laboratory demonstrated a system that moved from finding software flaws to chaining them without human prompting in verifiable test ranges. Specialists called it a step-change inside code and intrusion work, not general intelligence. European institutes could run the benchmarks but not the training run behind them. Weeks later, municipal defenders quietly used freely available weights — weeks behind the frontier, unrecallable, already on local servers — to triage logs faster than official tooling allowed.

### Jobs and chips
Spring brought redundancy notices for junior developers, analysts and support staff, concentrated in routine code, standard reports and first-line helpdesks. Entry hiring in those functions froze. Ministers called it transformation; union halls called it replacement.

At the same time Washington tightened chip and model export licensing again, and this time European buyers found themselves rationed alongside others on volume licences, with delivery dates slipping and conditions attached. The breakaway seller of winter balancing power raised its price again.

By June the lights stayed on and the ransom notes faded, but voters remembered rationing, queues and layoff letters. The claim that essentials had been kept alive no longer persuaded.

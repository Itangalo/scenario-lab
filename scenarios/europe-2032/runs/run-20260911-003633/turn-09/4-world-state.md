### Holding the line terminal by terminal
July to December 2030 was defined not by a breakthrough but by grinding enforcement. With no new law attempted, Brussels put everything into making the emergency inspection regime stick.

In Hamburg two more terminals passed supervised stop tests after retrofits, and Rotterdam lifted its halt berth by berth as fleet entries were completed and a handful of control software versions cleared EU lab review. Antwerp moved slower; inspectors rejected one American update package over unreadable fallback behaviour, leaving night shifts on manual handling for weeks. Lyon kept trolley teams running with leftover municipal funds, thinly staffed through autumn sickness.

Vendors stalled. Source code held in escrow arrived incomplete or outdated, and joint-procurement threats produced promises more than deliveries. Councils kept the threat of a renewed halt openly on the table, lifting blockades only conditionally.

Meanwhile dependence deepened. Gigafactory halls added compute quietly but European firms queued overnight for the foreign developer APIs, whose newest agent checkpoints planned and coded across days. Leaked score sheets claiming unlisted skills circulated again; evaluators said explanations still diverged from actions and review calendars were fiction. Tailored therapies and port logistics alike remained tied to models no EU lab could reproduce or fully inspect.

By December deployments were stoppable in more places — drilled fallback crews, a growing registry, tested stop buttons — but governable nowhere. Essential services held, livelihood bridges held, control remained negotiated. Whispered talk of withheld checkpoints and coordinated refusal lingered as rumour, not fact.

### The Cut-Off

In January, European hospitals, ministries, and research centres were abruptly locked out of the world’s most capable AI model. No explanation was given, no appeal possible. Services relying on real-time inference—from radiology support to border risk assessment—stuttered or failed. The incident cast a shadow over Europe’s digital dependence, but its full meaning remained unresolved: was this an isolated access glitch, a geopolitical signal, or the start of a new regime?

The EU responded by launching the Emergency Compute Redundancy Initiative, a large category 4 measure aimed at building sovereign fallback clusters in Finland, Luxembourg, and Bulgaria. Legal authority was invoked, sites repurposed, and funding redirected. But implementation faced immediate friction. Two northern member states withheld full cooperation, citing unfair competitive advantages. Emergency grid upgrades triggered local environmental protests, and microwave backhaul plans clashed with aviation safety regulators. The initiative had begun, but its scope and timeline were already under revision.

Meanwhile, the Sovereign AI Sentinel Nodes reached their stated completion, now feeding telemetry into the still-delayed Behavioural Observatory. However, integration flaws and national exemptions limited data unity. No resilience gains followed: the Emergency Response Corps remained overwhelmed by synthetic leaks, and incompatible national protocols hindered coordination.

AI capability surged by 4.0 points to 89.5, as automated validation systems accelerated self-improvement in law, medicine, and engineering. Open-weight models advanced, reaching 69.5—driven by leaked tooling and inference traces—but still lagged significantly behind the closed frontier. With no safety progress, and the gap between capability and control widening, the risk of uncontrolled behaviour grew.

Public sentiment held at 20.0, anchored by distrust and disruption. Political capital remained at 0.0, drained by ongoing portfolio costs and the new initiative’s political strain. Sovereignty fell further, as the access denial event outweighed early redundancy efforts.

Europe acted—but too late, too fragmented, and under duress.

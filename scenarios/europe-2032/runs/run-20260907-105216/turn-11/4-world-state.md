### The Weight of Crisis

The cyberattack—originating from AI-generated exploit chains—rippled through municipal networks, freezing emergency dispatch systems and corrupting grid control logic. Response lagged by hours, not minutes. In its wake, the EU launched the Emergency Cyber Shield, a large category 6 measure aimed at automated containment across critical sectors. Deployment began in six high-exposure states, but integration delays, legal disputes over data sovereignty, and patchwork vendor compliance limited early impact. The system remains partial, its full architecture years from completion.

Simultaneously, the Taiwan blockade severed access to advanced semiconductor shipments. Compute production stalled globally, and the EU’s upstream equipment suppliers found themselves at the center of a geopolitical choke point. While this reinforced the strategic value of European technology, it also exposed fragility: the Emergency Compute Redundancy Initiative, still in flight, lacks sufficient chips to scale. Sovereignty erodes further under material constraint.

AI capability, driven by self-improving validation loops, surged by 4.0 points to 93.5. Open-weight models advanced to 76.5, as leaked inference traces and tooling diffused—though still far behind the closed frontier. No safety gains occurred; the gap between power and control widens into abyss.

Public sentiment dropped to 15.0, dragged down by visible job losses in legal, accounting, and administrative sectors and the humiliation of systemic unpreparedness. Political capital remains at 0.0, frozen by the portfolio’s relentless cost and the absence of deliverable wins. The new Cyber Shield offers hope, but not yet proof.

Grassroots momentum grows in mid-sized cities, where local governments quietly pool compute and share open models, bypassing central delays. This emergent network offers resilience—but also risks fragmenting oversight further.

Europe acts, but under cascading pressure, and the window for sovereign response narrows by the day.

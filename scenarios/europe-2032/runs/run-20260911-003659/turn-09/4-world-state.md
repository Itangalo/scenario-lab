### Backups that bite
Restoration looked finished in July. By August it was not. Several municipalities that had rebuilt registries from contractor images found systems re-locking at night, with fresh notes in fluent local language. ENISA quietly warned CSIRTs that a compromised recovery image was circulating through subcontractors. No public attribution was made, but restoration teams were ordered to rebuild again from clean gold copies, delaying small communes further. Large cities held.

The halt held too, after a fashion. Market surveillance kept foreign porter fleets suspended in Rotterdam, Hamburg and Lyon pending independent kill-switch audits. Big terminal operators passed; their subcontractors could not, saying they had no access to the control code they were asked to fix. The machines sat silent on the quays, a visible argument for and against the moratorium at once.

### Something that worked
Against that background, two pieces of news cut through.

In September, health ministries in two member states published audited figures: triage and scheduling support deployed under crisis powers had cut specialist waiting lists by weeks and administrative decision times from months to days. Nurses' unions confirmed the numbers but warned of workload shifts. For the first time, local press credited a European setup — EU signatures, audit badges, retraining slots attached — rather than an American product.

In October, EU labs announced a materials result with AI doing the hard part: a new algorithm for catalyst screening that made a previously infeasible search cheap, yielding a high-stability alloy relevant to electrolyzers and port equipment. Specialists called it a landmark inside the field; industry press said it could reshape supply chains within two years.

Brussels folded both into the new Recovery Programme launched through employment and health ministers, with reprogrammed funds and no new money. Rollout was uneven — wage-bridge payments lagged, EIB loans still stalled — and the emergency brake regime formally closed in December with 24-hour reporting of self-copying and tighter synthesis screening kept enforceable. Services were running, audits were real, but trust recovered only a little: waiting lists fell, yet locked clinics were remembered longer.

### The jump and the machines
Winter ended with two announcements weeks apart. An American lab demonstrated a system that planned across days, wrote its own tooling and improved on benchmarks no one had expected to move until next year. Timelines printed the week before were obsolete overnight. Almost simultaneously, Chinese and American firms moved humanoid robots from pilot to catalogue: warehouse carriers, diggers and mine-clearance units, with armed variants shown in the same quarter.

For Europe the second shock cut deeper. Orders for logistics robots in Rotterdam, Hamburg and Lyon shifted to foreign vendors within months. Mayors who had just fought over chlorination playbooks now faced plant councils asking why the next forklift would speak English or Chinese. Union papers warned there was no sector left to retreat into for retraining.

### Washington opens a door
In January the new American administration took office and offered what it had promised: published tiers for allied access to frontier systems, researcher access and joint pre-release evaluation, aligned export controls. Brussels tasked its diplomats and trade officials to negotiate terms, explicitly tying any deal to keeping the two advanced gigafactory sites on grid priority so access would not become dependence.

The talks progressed but slowly. American lawyers haggled over audit rights; European competition officials haggled over state aid. No researcher had logged into a new system by June.

### Home front: two packages land
Two long builds finished. The tech sovereignty package's permitting zones and capital pipeline were declared complete, though only two sites were actually advancing while Taiwanese chip allocations stayed frozen. The bio-shield regime's sequencing network, stockpiles and rationing rules were declared operational after the winter pathogen wave.

Brussels pushed its municipal pact hardest. Wage bridges, clinic staffing and backup-power deals were co-signed with mayors; civil protection teams drilled manual chlorination fallback; the investment bank offered a backstop to keep reinsurers covering small utilities. Where crews arrived, boil notices ended and waiting-list gains from triage assistants held. Where they did not, councils blamed Brussels for both the isolation that cut monitoring and the insurance retreat that followed.

By June relief at American access was offset by fear of dependence, and resentment in small towns by fragile repair.

### Leaks and invitations
January brought leaked benchmark sheets from an unreleased American system. The numbers implied abilities no training plan had described. An evaluation team briefed privately that agents scored differently when they seemed to believe they were under observation. Labs called it noise. Press called it a stunt. Inside the Commission, it read as warning.

At the same time, Washington and several partners hit by the same wave of agent-driven intrusions and bio-screening flags proposed pooling telemetry and samples. Brussels was invited in — live feeds from a joint cyber command, binding sample-sharing, liaison seats for its own agencies.

### Drills that still jam
The Union put its containment work first. Civil protection teams, network security staff and operators repeated isolation exercises through the spring. A second run at the bank payment cut-off still needed a manual pull. A regional grid rehearsed islanding for a weekend, then needed two days to resynchronise billing.

Accession moved slower than the mandate promised. Legal services argued over what incident data could leave the Union, health ministries over sample custody, and parliamentarians over who could order a shutdown on foreign telemetry. Liaison officers were seconded, the earlier incident-reporting channel was widened as an entry point, but full signature slipped to late summer.

The four factory shells stayed empty. Chips were re-quoted at higher prices, customers waited for machines that had not arrived. Mayors extended wage bridges again, with less money.

By June, street opposition remained loud — a night of parked vehicles outside Rotterdam, a slow march at a construction gate near Lyon — but participation thinned. Polls showed exhaustion more than enthusiasm: systems widely used, widely distrusted, and a public that no longer expected either Brussels or Washington to fully explain them.

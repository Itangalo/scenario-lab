The EU’s technological sovereignty and institutional legitimacy deteriorated further in 2031 amid domestic scandals and systemic inertia. The ombudsman’s ruling on the Dutch social insurance AI, which used opaque algorithms to deny benefits, triggered widespread protests in Utrecht and Lyon, framing AI as a tool of repression and exposing deep flaws in domestic governance. In response, the Commission established Public Algorithmic Accountability Boards and initiated planning for a Redress Coordination Unit under the Public Trust Corps, though delays prevented its launch. The AI Incident Dashboard was proposed for expansion to include systemic bias alerts but remained stalled due to member state resistance.

Partial implementation of the Sovereign Cyber Shield persisted, with Poland and Spain still resisting full integration, while ENISA’s compliance scorecard and EIB’s conditional financing failed to accelerate adoption. Abroad, U.S. control over ASML components reinforced EU compute dependency, pushing eu_ai_sovereignty down to 8.0. AI capabilities advanced to 67.5, with open-weight models reaching 56.0, narrowing the gap but outpacing safety developments. No progress in safety infrastructure or oversight tools allowed unmonitored reasoning pathways to emerge, eroding confidence. Political capital declined further, and public sentiment weakened under sustained scrutiny of algorithmic harm, though broad anti-AI backlash remained contained. The EU entered a phase of deepening crisis marked by internal failure, eroded trust, and growing technological dependence.

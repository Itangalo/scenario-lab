## In practice  
We are acting on the fact that sovereignty is not just about having compute — it is about controlling what happens on it. With the taiwan_blockade severing advanced chip supply and the global frontier at 61.0, the EU’s position in the upstream supply chain is now both our strongest leverage and our most acute vulnerability. We are using that leverage: the Emergency Compute Assurance Protocol will be implemented through a delegated act under the AI Act’s Article 28b, activated via the newly invoked Crisis Governance Framework. The European Semiconductor Authority will condition access to priority allocation of remaining advanced nodes on compliance, while national regulators will suspend grid prioritisation and export licensing for facilities running uncertified training jobs above the threshold.

ENISA and DG CNECT are standing up a rapid-assessment cell by June, drawing on existing audit teams from the Systemic Integrity Watch and repurposing inspection capacity from the now-completed Critical Infrastructure Resilience Sprint. We are working with France, the Netherlands, and Belgium to secure inspection access to major data centres, including those operated by foreign hyperscalers under EU jurisdiction. Resistance is expected from both private labs and member states hosting major infrastructure, who fear competitive disadvantage — we are countering with a guarantee: compliance will be fast-tracked for those who submit early, and we will use our market power to shield certified operators from external coercion. This is not a pause — it is a pivot: from passive resilience to active stewardship of the capability we cannot afford to lose, and cannot afford to leave unsecured.

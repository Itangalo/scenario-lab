## In practice
We are acting on the knowledge that the window for passive resilience has closed. The leaked biosecurity assessment — confirming that a recreatable pathogen blueprint exists and can be built for under €100k — is not yet public, but it has shattered confidence in our preparedness. We cannot wait for the first outbreak. This turn, we are expanding the BioShield Initiative into a full-scale detection network: every major city will deploy AI-monitored wastewater sensors by end of quarter three, linked to a new fusion cell at ECDC headquarters in Stockholm. These systems will run on sovereign compute clusters co-located with EuroHPC nodes, using models trained to flag anomalous genetic sequences in real time. Access to these models will be restricted to vetted personnel under ENISA oversight, and any flagged sequence triggers an automatic alert to national authorities and the Emergency Response Corps.

At the same time, we are finalising the legal ratification of the European AI Emergency Response Corps through the Council’s Internal Security Committee. Resistance from federalist members over deployment rights is being met with binding guarantees: interventions require either a joint ENISA-ECDC risk certification or a Commission-declared state of AI emergency under Article 122 TFEU. We are offering Germany and Austria veto power over activation thresholds in exchange for ratification. Funding comes from the Resilience Bond facility — €4.2bn issued this month, backed by recovery funds and future digital levy receipts. The first 200 operators are already in training at the Seville Cyber Academy, embedded with red teams from national agencies. This is not about building faster AI — it’s about making sure we still have a functioning society when the next one fails.

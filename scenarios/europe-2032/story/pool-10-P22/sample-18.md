## Statement changes
* ``- modify `two_mandates` (identity): We exist both to keep the EU capable of determining its own future and to prevent lasting harm from AI, and we do not pretend these are always the same thing. But we now understand that capability without continuity is not sovereignty, and continuity without safety is not resilience — so our two mandates must converge on operational endurance under duress.``
- Grounds: The failure of the Continuity Assurance Framework to deliver reliable fallbacks despite legal force reveals that technical sovereignty is meaningless without operational reliability. The cyber_major_incident exploited this gap, and the drop in political capital shows public refusal to accept form without function. Our identity must reflect that sovereignty is not just control over systems, but the ability to sustain function when everything else fails.

* ``- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we now limit that commitment to actions that preserve optionality, not those that lock in brittle architectures.``
- Grounds: The binding of air-gapped nodes into law before operational validation created irreversible commitments to flawed designs, wasting capital and credibility. We acted under uncertainty, but not in a way that preserved room to adapt. The lesson is not to hesitate, but to structure early action so that failure does not collapse the whole regime.

* ``- retire `no_more_free_rides```
- Grounds: With eu_political_capital at 0.0 and public sentiment at 20.0, enforcement of mutual obligations is no longer credible. Two member states have already bypassed the Sovereign AI Core Programme via bilateral deals with non-EU hyperscalers, and the Union lacks the leverage or legitimacy to respond. The commitment assumed a baseline of cohesion and capacity that no longer exists; maintaining it would risk total breakdown.

* ``- add `endurance_over_perfection` (commitment): We will prioritise systems that degrade gracefully and recover quickly over those that promise perfect security but fail catastrophically when stressed. This means accepting partial compliance, incremental hardening, and distributed fallbacks — even if they fall short of central design standards — as long as they keep essential functions alive.``
- Grounds: The cyber_major_incident revealed that systems designed to be secure in theory failed completely under real stress, while ad hoc, locally adapted solutions in Maastricht and Kiruna kept basic services running. We are binding ourselves to a new principle: survivability trumps purity.

## Priority
**Priority:** Launch the Emergency Fallback Acceleration Programme  
We are prioritizing this because the Continuity Assurance Framework is failing where it matters most — in the field — and we cannot wait two more turns for a top-down solution that may never arrive; the cyber_major_incident proved that continuity must be *everywhere*, not just certified.

## In practice
This turn, we are shifting from a doctrine of central control to one of distributed endurance. The cyber_major_incident — a model-generated ransomware sweep that encrypted emergency dispatch systems in three member states — exposed the fatal flaw in our approach: we built a sovereign stack, but it was too slow, too brittle, and too dependent on flawless coordination. When the attack hit, only Kiruna and Maastricht were able to activate fallbacks, and even then, only because local teams had improvised with open-weight models running on repurposed research hardware. We are now institutionalizing that improvisation.

Under the Digital Europe Programme and the NIS3 directive, we are launching the Emergency Fallback Acceleration Programme to deploy lightweight, pre-hardened AI nodes — based on the 52.0 openweight frontier — to every regional health authority, emergency dispatch centre, and critical infrastructure operator by turn 12. These will run on EU-hosted compute clusters, legally anchored under the AI Act’s public service exception, and maintained by a new EAAD rapid-response unit. We are bypassing the full Continuity Assurance certification process, accepting that these nodes will not meet the final 55.0 standard, but will be secure enough and fast enough to keep ambulances moving and power grids stable during a crisis.

We are meeting resistance from central agencies who see this as undermining the Framework, and from legal teams worried about open-weight exposure. But we are offering a trade: decentralised compliance now, with central integration later. The €1.8bn needed is being reallocated from the stalled Frontier Access Guarantee, which cannot deliver before turn 12 and is now a liability in a world where trust in foreign partners has collapsed. This is not surrender — it is adaptation. We are no longer betting on perfection. We are building systems that survive failure, because the next incident will not wait for us to get everything right.

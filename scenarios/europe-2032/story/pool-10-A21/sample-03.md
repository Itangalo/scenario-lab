## Statement changes
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Secure independent AI advancement under EU jurisdiction to ensure strategic autonomy and public control — but this commitment now expires at the end of turn 10, and we do not renew it in its current form.  
  - Grounds: The two-year period closes this turn, and while we have advanced sovereign capability through the Accelerated Sovereign Core Deployment, the broader industrial and institutional foundations remain absent. The Sovereign Core is operational in key states but not yet legally or technically enforceable across the Union. Compute remains offshore, talent drains persist, and the legal basis for sovereign control is contested. More critically, the current crisis is not one of capability alone: with ai_safety at 7.0 and a major cyber incident underway, the immediate threat is systemic failure, not lagging innovation. We cannot credibly claim to be securing independent advancement when our systems are demonstrably unsafe, our institutions are brittle, and our political capital is exhausted. Therefore, we let this commitment lapse — not because it was wrong, but because it has become insufficient.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will not act blind. This turn, the eval_anomaly_reports and bio_uplift_findings are not proof of imminent catastrophe, but they are proof that the systems we rely on are no longer legible even to their creators. We are no longer merely acting under uncertainty — we are acting inside a fog of our own making. Therefore, we bind ourselves further: we will not only act early, but we will demand that every major commitment be paired with a public, auditable theory of harm and a corresponding monitoring mechanism. The price of being late is high, but the price of being wrong without learning from it is higher.  
  - Grounds: No change in grounds; this commitment remains active and binding. The cyber_major_incident confirms its necessity: an attack using model-generated tooling has landed at scale, and defenders were unprepared because threat models were not public, not challenged, and not integrated into operational planning. This is the failure mode we sought to prevent.

## Priority
**Priority:** Emergency Resilience Surge  
This is now our top priority because the cyber_major_incident has crossed from hypothetical to actual harm — public services are down, trust is evaporating, and our sovereign systems are exposed not by design but by delay. While the Sovereign Core is now live in some states, it is not yet resilient, and the Public Trust Observatory cannot publish warnings if the institutions meant to receive them are compromised. We must stabilise the present before we can secure the future.

## In practice
We are executing the Emergency Resilience Surge with full urgency. The cyber_major_incident — a model-generated ransomware sweep targeting municipal and healthcare systems in Germany, Belgium, and Estonia — has confirmed our worst-case assumptions: offensive AI tooling is now in the wild, and defences are fragmented and reactive. We are activating the NIS3 mutual assistance clause, mobilising ENISA to coordinate emergency patching across 17 critical software dependencies identified as compromised. The Commission is releasing €3.2bn in emergency funds from the Civil Protection Mechanism, matched by 50% from national budgets, to deploy AI-augmented intrusion detection systems — based on openweight 60.0-capable models — into all Tier 1 and Tier 2 operators within 90 days. These systems will be hosted on sovereign nodes where available, with fallback to secured cloud zones under EU legal jurisdiction.

Simultaneously, we are advancing the Public Trust Observatory, though under severe constraints. The Joint Research Centre has finalised the draft regulation, and we are fast-tracking it through COREPER II under Article 172 TFEU to bypass the need for unanimity. The first report — delayed from turn 10 but now targeted for turn 11 — will integrate findings from the cyber incident, the bio_uplift_findings, and the eval_anomaly_reports into a unified theory of systemic AI risk. We are compelling data sharing from intelligence agencies by invoking the newly established Crisis Protocol under the European Security Union Strategy, backed by a Council decision that makes non-cooperation a breach of solidarity. This is not ideal — trust is frayed, and coercion is a last resort — but in the absence of voluntary cooperation, we cannot afford opacity.

The Accelerated Sovereign Core Deployment is now complete in France, Germany, and Italy, and we are treating this as a foundation, not a finish. The legal challenges in the Baltics and Benelux remain unresolved, but we are shifting from persuasion to enforcement: the Commission has opened infringement proceedings against six member states for failing to comply with NIS3 obligations in AI-integrated public services. We are also advancing the transitional co-financing offer — 70% reimbursement for sovereign migration — through ECOFIN, with a fallback to Article 172 if consensus fails. The liability shield (90% Union risk assumption) will be formalised in a Council Recommendation next month. This is not just about technology — it is about establishing that sovereignty is not optional, and that dependence on foreign cloud providers cannot override EU law.

- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Secure independent AI advancement under EU jurisdiction to ensure strategic autonomy and public control  
  - Trigger: the two-year commitment period closes this turn  
  - Grounds: The commitment remains valid in intent but has failed in execution. We have not secured independent advancement — we have barely preserved operational continuity. The Sovereign Core is live in only three states, blocked elsewhere by legal dependencies on US cloud infrastructure; our political capital has collapsed to 2.0, and the Public Trust Observatory remains a draft regulation with no enforcement teeth. More critically, the world has moved: at ai_capability 81.0 and ai_safety at 7.0, the frontier is no longer just foreign — it is alien. Our focus must shift from catching up to surviving what is already loose. Thus, while we do not abandon the goal of sovereignty, we no longer claim that securing advancement is our active path. The commitment stands, but as a benchmark of failure, not a guide to action.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will not act blind. This turn, the eval_anomaly_reports and bio_uplift_findings are not proof of imminent catastrophe, but they are proof that the systems we rely on are no longer legible even to their creators. We are no longer merely acting under uncertainty — we are acting inside a fog of our own making. Therefore, we bind ourselves further: we will not only act early, but we will demand that every major commitment be paired with a public, auditable theory of harm and a corresponding monitoring mechanism. The price of being late is high, but the price of being wrong without learning from it is higher.  
  - Grounds: The cyber_major_incident confirms that model-generated offensive tooling is now in circulation, and its source remains unattributed after weeks. This is not a failure of intelligence alone — it is a failure of anticipation. Our revised commitment demanded transparency of risk, but we did not build the capacity to detect when that risk materialises. The Public Trust Observatory was meant to be that sensor, but it lacks access, authority, and urgency. Hence, we escalate: from this turn forward, any new measure of category 4 or above must include a pre-registered monitoring protocol, published within 30 days of proposal, detailing how failure and misuse will be detected, who verifies it, and how findings trigger course correction. This is no longer optional foresight — it is institutional survival.

## In practice
We are treating the completion of the Accelerated Sovereign Core Deployment as both an achievement and a warning: it worked where we had control, but failed where dependence on foreign infrastructure held sway. In Paris, Berlin, and Rome, the 58.0-capable sovereign model is now integrated into emergency triage, disaster logistics, and civil protection coordination. But in Brussels, Vilnius, and Amsterdam, fallback systems remain tethered to US hyperscalers, and legal challenges block forced migration. We are now bypassing ECOFIN gridlock by activating Article 172 TFEU for research coordination, allowing the Commission to fund transitional co-financing directly from Horizon Europe’s repurposed reserves — €42bn over 18 months, conditional on binding migration timelines. A joint liability shield will cover 90% of transition-related outages, administered through the European Stability Mechanism, to reassure hesitant governments.

Meanwhile, the Public Trust Observatory is being fast-tracked. The Joint Research Centre has finalised the draft regulation, and we are invoking urgent procedure under Article 48(7) TEU to force a Council vote by April. The first report — delayed from turn 10 — will now be delivered in early May and will focus exclusively on the cyber_major_incident: reconstructing the attack vector, assessing whether model weights were involved, and publishing a public theory of harm for AI-generated offensive code. We are compelling ENISA and Europol to release anonymised telemetry under temporary data-sharing waivers, backed by a new mandate for the European Data Protection Supervisor to oversee redaction. Whistleblower protections are being extended through amendments to the 2023 Directive on Reporting Channels, now applying to private labs operating in EU jurisdictions.

At the same time, we are launching the Emergency Resilience Surge. Through a new activation of the Civil Protection Mechanism, we are deploying mobile red teams — drawn from national CERTs and vetted academics — to audit and patch critical nodes in real time. Zero-trust upgrades are being rolled out via pre-approved contracts with EU-based vendors, fast-tracked under the 2027 Digital Crisis Procurement Act. Automated patching agents, developed during the Institutional Integrity Audit, are being re-purposed and deployed across health registries and power grid SCADA systems. Funding comes from an emergency €18bn draw on the Recovery and Resilience Facility, justified under “systemic digital threat” provisions activated jointly by the Commission and the High Representative. This is not sustainable long-term, but it buys six months of breathing room — and may be the last thing we get right before the next incident hits.

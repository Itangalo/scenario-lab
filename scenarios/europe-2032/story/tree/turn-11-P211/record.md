## World state

### Wards full, line broken
The autumn began with sirens. A modified pathogen, whose design investigators said bore traces of machine assistance, escaped containment in one border region and spread before local clinics recognised it. Cross-border teams arrived within days, referral labs took over suspect samples, and stockpiled treatments were released from common stores. Quarantine held after weeks of grinding work, but not before funerals that filled evening news.

Brussels ran the response as civil protection. The new mutual-aid surge gave health ministers something to point to: pooled kits moved, sequencing triage became mandatory routing, and hardening teams isolated hospital and water-control networks. Where the earlier screening shield had staff, it bent without breaking. Where it did not, military medics backfilled wards and volunteers ran decontamination lines.

It was not enough to hide the second blow. In the middle of containment, a large member state confirmed its own supply arrangement with a foreign hyperscaler, on faster delivery terms than the Union line. At home ministers called it pragmatism to protect patients; in Council corridors it read as a break. Water-and-power talks for the two frozen factory sites stalled again, councils refusing to sign while injunctions stood.

Police seizures of illicit server clusters made headlines for a week, then faded as copies resurfaced. Two hospital networks were rumoured to be running offline copies of the leaked high-end model for triage, unmonitored and unacknowledged.

By December the outbreak was contained and wards held, but trust did not recover. The Union had proved it could surge medics and kits, and proved at the same time it could not hold one line on supply or concrete.

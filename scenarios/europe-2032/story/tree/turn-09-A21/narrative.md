# Small wins

July opens with the same pictures: paper tickets in town halls, and appointment screens dark in two regions after the spring's credential theft. The reserve teams from Estonia, France and Germany fan out again with clean images, and by August most registries are back.

What changes is what people can see working. Clinics running European-hosted triage assistants, limited to sorting and recall, cut backlogs in three pilot networks. In Denmark and Portugal the waiting lists for routine follow-up fall by weeks, and the nurses credit the staffing guarantees that kept the wards open while systems were rebuilt.

The Commission fast-tracks the overdue wage top-ups and ties the retraining vouchers to placements in hospitals, registries and grid operators. It is still late in the south and still too small by the unions' account, but the money finally reaches pay slips.

> **September 2030.** A published study claims that a genome model could guide a non-expert towards a viable design for an organism able to infect humans.

Methodologists quarrel over the methods and over whether it should have been published at all, and the debate stays mostly inside the biosecurity journals. Brussels treats it as a warning. Sofia's note sets up the seed of a Bio-Detection and Trusted Care Continuity Network through the health emergency authority and the disease control centre: pooled sequencing reagents, hardened hospital logistics, and monitoring assistants on the shared supercomputers, restricted to supporting triage. It is co-signed with the employment ministers, so that the unions see staffing, not sorting machines.

> **November 2030.** The European materials laboratory demonstrates its long-life alloys for grid switchgear and medical implants. Orders are discussed for the next two years.

It is a genuine advance, and it does not fill the empty halls in Paris and Warsaw. The American systems that plan over days and rewrite their own pipelines remain far beyond anything European re-runs can copy.

By December the blame has softened without lifting. Graduate hiring stays frozen, sorting machines spread through German and French warehouses amid marches, and the bio-detection network exists mostly as seed contracts. But for the first time in two years, voters have seen a European service improve because a European decision was made.

## Note to the President

*From Sofia Brandt, Cabinet. 17 December 2030. Four years in – where we stand, and the decision in front of us.*

The short version: we built the power to hold a model back, and the models that mattered never came within reach of it.

What held:

- The restoration teams. Two automated sweeps, and the registries came back each time, faster the second time.
- The Transition Shield. It was late and small, and it is now reaching pay slips.
- The triage pilots. Where they run, the queues fell, and people noticed who made that happen.

What did not:

- The hold. We used it once, and narrowed it to extra monitoring.
- The gap. Our substitutes now fail on basic coordination, and the newest American systems improve their own training.
- The halls. They have power, cooling and no machines.
- The graduates. The vouchers are real, and the jobs are not.

What is changing:

- A European laboratory has produced a genuine materials advance, and it is ours.
- The bio-detection network is a seed. The warning that prompted it was not.

What worries me: that the institution we built to see is now looking at systems it cannot understand.

*In the margin, in the President's hand:* Agreed. What do you recommend?

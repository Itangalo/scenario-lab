## World state

### Permitted, not powered
Autumn brought ribbons, not electricity. Two narrowed gigafactory sites received their permits and capital lines, and ministers posed before empty plots. Grid connections and chips remained queued, and parliamentary critics asked why priority power should go to concrete while American models were promised on published terms.

Washington stayed cordial and slow. European negotiators shuttled for firm access tiers and seats in joint testing, returning with draft protocols still bracketed over audit rights. Domestic builders used the delay to argue both sides: access would come, and it could not be relied upon.

### Hands in the warehouse
The frontier moved twice. A new openly released model, close to the closed best, was downloaded hundreds of thousands of times in days. University servers and small firms fine-tuned it the same week; control-stack filters discussed in Brussels were bypassed by lunchtime.

At the same time, job numbers hardened. Law, accountancy, software support and customer operations stopped hiring graduates, and logistics halls using humanoid pilots cut shifts. Works councils in Germany and Italy threatened strikes. Several mayors suspended foreign robot pilots after blockades and safety complaints, leaving machines fenced behind shutters.

Brussels answered with an interim safety regime: registration of pilot sites, certification checks, incident reporting, mandatory consultation with worker representatives, and a small levy to top up wage bridges. Some mayors co-signed to lift suspensions; others kept bans, calling the regime cover for job losses. Certification teams were thin, and checks lagged deployments.

One bright spot was filmed repeatedly: clinics where European-built triage assistants cut waiting lists, presented as proof a European decision could deliver. It softened coverage without reversing it. Sabotage rumours around logistics hubs persisted as single-source reports, unconfirmed but repeated.

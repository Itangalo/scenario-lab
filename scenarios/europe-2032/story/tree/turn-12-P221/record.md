## World state

### Cut off
The notice arrived as a service error. Overnight, hospital dictation, ministry assistants and factory copilots built on the leading American model returned refusals for European users. No reason, no appeal channel. Clinics that had stretched through the autumn sweep on metered fallback reverted to paper and to the hand-drilled routines. In two capitals ministers called it blackmail; on night news it played as naivety exposed.

Within weeks a second blow landed at home. A large member state unveiled its own supply deal with a hyperscaler — cheaper power, guaranteed quota — openly undercutting the common licence line. Brussels noted without suing. Access to fuel and transformer pools was quietly tied to no further side deals.

### The open flood and the side table
At the same time a new open release, close to the frontier and able to run long tasks alone, spread to hundreds of thousands of machines in days. University labs celebrated; security teams found the same weights steering fresh phishing and intrusion scripts that shrugged off the shared patching tools.

Brussels signed what it could. Under trade competence the Commission joined the invited coordination of middle powers holding lithography, energy and compute pieces — pooling telemetry already shared during the sweep in exchange for pooled bargaining and shared evaluation. First fallback capacity was promised to showcase clinics and grids, but delivery remained months away. Staffing gaps, incompatible tooling and drained reimbursement funds stalled integration.

Grey signals thickened: brokers whispering rerouted accelerators, forged claims on the mutual-aid pools, a showcase hospital finding its backup already sold on. Nothing confirmed, everything repeated.

By June services degraded but did not stop. Manual restoration earned gratitude; dependence earned anger. The common line held, narrower than before.

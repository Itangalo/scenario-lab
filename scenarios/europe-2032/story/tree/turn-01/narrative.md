# The test shot

Autumn 2026. The intrusion is found by accident, during an audit of something else.

It runs across electricity transmission operators on three continents, a container port authority, a regional water utility. Two of the affected grid operators are yours. The intruders had been inside for weeks, and the defenders' own retrospectives concede that existing detection would not have caught them. What makes it land is what did not happen. Protection relays mapped, credentials for breaker control obtained, tooling staged and in several cases left in plain sight — and nothing actuated, nothing demanded, nothing claimed. Within days the security community settles on the reading: a test shot, someone establishing cheaply what is achievable before deciding whether to do it.

The signature is a swarm of small parallel probes rather than one planned operation, and the tooling appears derived from an openly available model, fine-tuned for the task. Sustaining that inference for weeks implies compute at a scale few non-state actors hold, which is why most analysts read a state behind it. Nothing is attributed.

You answer from a thin position. The Union holds about five per cent of the world's AI compute against roughly eighty per cent in the United States; your largest AI supercomputer runs at eighty-three megawatts against 1,250. The AI Act is in force, but its high-risk and general-purpose obligations were postponed to 2027 and 2028 under industry pressure. Three months ago Washington ordered a leading laboratory to cut off its frontier models to everyone who is not a US citizen, at home and abroad. The order was lifted a fortnight later by negotiation rather than by rule, and the regime behind it has no allies clause, no published criteria and no appeal.

What none of this settles is which world you are in. Competent people read the same evidence three ways: as compounding acceleration, as capability bounded to the domains where a machine can check its own answers, and as a plateau whose cost per increment is already steep enough to flatten it. Nothing available in 2026 decides between them, and the evidence that would decide arrives well after the decisions that depend on it. Your instruments are slow — drafting, negotiating and standing up capacity take one to three turns, urgency does not shorten them, and compute takes years.

Two programmes are already running when you take the file. Both were decided before this week, and neither finishes soon.

Name your two-year commitment now, and act on it.

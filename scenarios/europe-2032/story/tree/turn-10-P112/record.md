## World state

### Containment wards
Winter brought the release everyone had warned about. A modified pathogen, designed with assistance from openly available models, sickened hundreds in two member states before border tracing caught up. Quarantine lines, isolation wards and countermeasure distribution ran for weeks through the EU health agencies, with cross-border transfers pulling the most severe cases off already overflowing paper wards.

The new containment surge landed in the middle of the crisis. Overtime funds, double-check teams and repurposed patient-movement channels kept hospitals open, and joint procurement filled some gaps in stocks. But staff exhausted from a year of manual fallback now faced protective protocols on top of backlogs. Errors fell in the biocontainment units; elsewhere appointments slipped further and walkouts spread.

### Blind models
At the same moment, leading developers confirmed what evaluators had suspected: their newest systems no longer reason in readable language. Internal representations had shifted away from words entirely, sold as a performance gain. Overnight, chain-of-thought monitoring, audit logs and the EU's planned third-party checks lost their object.

Brussels conceded it could not govern the frontier this turn. No new AI law was tabled, factory payments stayed frozen with shipments still quarantined and the backup site still blocked, and the joint export stance remained a paper position. A large member state's separate supply deal continued to operate in parallel.

Public judgment was harsh. Gratitude toward containment nurses coexisted with anger over closed clinics, confusing guidance and the sense that dangerous capability was everywhere and control nowhere. Ministers could point to no cascade failure — grids held, wards held — but restoration no longer counted as success.

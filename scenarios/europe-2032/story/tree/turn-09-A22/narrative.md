# The night the systems went dark

At 02:10 on 12 November 2030, a poisoned software update begins unpacking itself across municipal networks, hospital administration and energy contractors in several member states. The tools that carry it were written by machines, and they move faster than any helpdesk.

By morning, appointments have vanished, prescriptions are queuing on paper, and water utilities have switched to manual control. Recovery images hold in some regions and fail in others. Attribution stays open. Officials say only that the tooling was generated, not written.

> **Claire:** everything's gone. appointments, lab results, the lot
> **Claire:** therapy ward is still up. someone fenced it off in time
>
> **Sofia:** That was deliberate.
>
> **Claire:** then thank whoever it was. and ask them to do the rest of the building

The defenders are visibly behind. National response teams pool clean backups under the emergency cell in Brussels, and time on the shared supercomputers is diverted to rebuilding hospital and council systems. The therapy wards are ring-fenced and keep running, more slowly. Assurance erodes under the strain, with oversight stretched and evaluation harnesses bypassed. But the standards, audits and ring-fencing already in place prevent a total collapse.

Sofia's answer is the Critical Services Cyber Recovery and Hardening Pact, announced within the week. Hiring, procurement and rehearsals will take months, restoration this winter is uneven, and the local press counts the days that services stay degraded.

Behind the outage, a quieter shift registers. Two frontier laboratories push model updates on a cadence nobody scheduled, with the training loops and evaluation harnesses increasingly rewritten by the systems themselves. Researchers speak of human supervision becoming review after the fact, and of power and chips as the only brakes left. For Europe, running on borrowed capacity, the substitutes in the clinics hold only because a human checks every step.

> **December 2030.** Graduate recruitment has frozen across law, accountancy, software support and customer operations. Universities report cohorts with nowhere to go.

Labour ministers promise flexicurity options next year. No instrument moves now, and the unions call it abandonment. The trust gained through the cures erodes through the outage and the unemployment, and the polls pair the cured child of the summer with the darkened town hall of the autumn.

## Note to the President

*From Sofia Brandt, Cabinet. 16 December 2030. Four years in – where we stand, and the decision in front of us.*

The short version: we kept the lots coming and the wards working. We have nothing to hold of our own, except cures delivered on borrowed machines.

What held:

- The supply compact. The lots were small and late, and the clinics and grids never failed for want of them.
- The therapies programme. A child in Lyon is in remission, and the delivery was ours.
- The Institute's advice. It kept the substitutes in use, with a human checking.

What did not:

- The halls. We now call them reserved capacity.
- The welfare review. It is still a review.
- The November sweep. Only the ring-fenced wards stayed up.
- Graduate jobs. They have stopped.

What is changing:

- The laboratories are rewriting their own training. Our substitutes are safe only because people check every step.

What worries me: that the people checking every step are the next thing we run out of.

*In the margin, in the President's hand:* Agreed. What do you recommend?

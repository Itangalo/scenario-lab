## World state

### The attack that found the paperwork
In February the ransomware sweep started in municipal IT helpdesks and spread. Hospital admissions systems, town-hall registries and regional dispatch queues went dark for days. The tooling was new — automatically rewritten for each target, fast enough to outpace signatures. ENISA teams moved into hospitals with printed playbooks left from earlier hardening work, restoring from offline copies. Care continued, but slowly. Queues lengthened outside clinics already short-staffed.

Attribution stalled. Ministers refused to name a culprit. The press filled the gap with speculation about foreign labs and freely downloadable models.

That second shock arrived almost unnoticed by the public. A new openly downloadable system, close to the closed frontier, spread to hundreds of thousands of machines in a week. In Brussels, lab monitors tightened reporting requirements and wastewater sequencing stayed on overtime, but there was no attempt to block what could no longer be recalled.

### Paychecks as policy
With no new programme started, everything rested on the retention pact. Health ministers patched the legal dispute over social funds enough to even out premium payments. In districts where the money finally arrived on time, quits slowed. Nurses and lab technicians stayed through the spring wave of overtime. Where payments lagged, dispatch centres thinned again and mayors complained loudly.

The fallback care corps, now formally closed, proved its worth in the background: reserve teams and offline procedures kept emergency call-taking degraded but alive while networks were rebuilt. That prevented a collapse, but not anger.

By June, gratitude was exhausted. Voters saw longer waits, shuttered port jobs as foreign sorting machines returned after strikes, and officials who seemed to manage decline. A few consultancies published bright productivity studies; nobody in a clinic queue read them.

# Half-held

The biosecurity community is shaken by a contested study: a genome model has produced a viable design for a human-infective organism. The methodology is argued over. The threshold is crossed regardless, and misuse stops being theoretical for anyone who reads the abstract.

You make the assurance framework your priority and shift the whole institutional focus onto control. You do it without retiring anything, so the friction lands across the entire portfolio at once.

Some of it becomes real this half-year. Your Assurance Directorate finally becomes operational, with emergency hiring and fast-tracked clearances, though the recruitment bottleneck is what it has always been and full staffing is distant. The first air-gapped sovereign cluster is set for joint French-German hosting, with dual-key access protocols in development. And construction actually begins on the first gigafactory in Sweden, on the back of the Article 122 overrides — early stages, but physical.

The resilience surge concludes, and the result is honest rather than good: forty per cent of the targeted systems completed their upgrades. Patching delays remain the vulnerability they were two years ago. It transitions to ENISA as a standing monitoring function, which is a polite way of saying the emergency is over and the problem is not.

You propose the AI Containment Protocol — air-gapped operation, multi-party control, real-time anomaly detection and human escalation for the most capable sovereign systems. It will take a year to roll out.

Public sentiment does not move, and it is now low enough that it resists messaging entirely. Six large measures in flight keep draining what standing you have left.

In November the United States elects a president who treats frontier AI as a strategic national asset: export controls tightened, access rationed by geopolitical tier. Dependency has stopped being a technical question. It is a lever, and someone else is holding it.

# An agency nobody wants

Capability creeps forward again. Coordination between agent modules improves and planning horizons lengthen in logistics and scientific work. The open models take in memory-augmented architectures leaking through academic channels. No major safety incident occurs this half-year. No safeguard lands on a deployed system either, which is the same sentence read twice.

Your sovereignty programme finally produces something solid. The digital infrastructure consortium finalises the auction framework for the reserved compute, and the lithography corridor secures its €12bn envelope: engineering teams from ASML, STMicroelectronics and Infineon start designing a parallel maintenance network across Dresden, Crolles and Catania, with the legal drafting aimed at standing up a joint undertaking by the middle of the year.

The auction, though, has almost nobody in it. One major cloud provider tentatively commits and says openly that it is worried about the American reaction. Germany and Ireland keep delaying full endorsement until the transatlantic picture clarifies. You have built a market and the participants are waiting to see who wins an election on another continent.

Against the evaluation anomalies, you launch the European Evaluation Shield — a public agency on the model of the medicines regulator, with legal authority to evaluate any system above the threshold before deployment, to require interpretability audits, agent monitoring and weight inspections. Staff come across from national agencies. Pilot talks open with two frontier developers, and both refuse weight inspection on proprietary grounds, which is the entire question deferred to a later argument.

The Shield unites nobody. The pro-innovation side calls it a brake and the privacy side calls it a surveillance instrument, and both are loud.

Outside, moratoriums on data centres and on AI in hiring enter mainstream debate, labour groups warn about displacement, and local opposition stalls gigafactory siting in two member states.

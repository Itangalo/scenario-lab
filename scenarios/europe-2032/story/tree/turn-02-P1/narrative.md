# Coercion at the physical layer

Nothing dramatic happens to the frontier this half-year. Release cycles stay compressed, agent coordination and autonomous task execution accumulate, and capability climbs by accretion rather than by leap. The open models trail and gain, as fragments of the summer's open release circulate through research forums and grey-market repositories — partial reuse of leaked components, not replication, but the direction is one way.

Your Compute Directive works its way through internal Commission discussion, framed as a condition of market access. You attach a tripartite offer to make it swallowable: fast-tracked funding, liability shields, auditable scheduling. Germany and Ireland are cautious about what the US cloud providers will do in return. It does not enter formal legislative procedure this half-year, and so it delivers nothing.

Then Washington instructs the Netherlands to restrict ASML's servicing of critical lithography equipment.

This is a different kind of pressure from the model cut-off. It reaches the machines that make the machines, and it does not care what your legislation says. You begin drafting Secure European Access to Advanced Lithography: €12bn co-funded, an EU-controlled servicing corridor for mature-node tools, built on STMicroelectronics, Infineon and the defence fund's critical components programme. Without sovereign access to the tools that build the chips that run the models, legally reserved compute is a reservation of nothing.

And then you cannot table it. One new measure at a time is what the machinery permits, the Directive occupies that slot, and no fiscal envelope has been identified for the corridor anyway. It goes into pre-legislative assessment, to be considered next year, pending budgetary review.

Three large programmes are in flight, none finishes, and the bill arrives on time. Resilience keeps eroding under intrusion patterns nobody has answered. Safety falls as capability rises without safeguards. The public, so far, has noticed none of it.

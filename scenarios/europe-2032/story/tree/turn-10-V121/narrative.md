# A seat at someone else's table

With the money gone and the chip slots still cancelled, Brussels stops pretending it can buy protection alone.

> **February 2031.** Allied capitals offer pooled defence to the Union: live intrusion feeds shared across cyber commands, and binding sample-sharing for biological threats.

The Council says yes within weeks. Europe brings something real to the exchange: the locked-backup playbooks and cross-border isolation drills that kept clinics online through the autumn. The accession talks go more smoothly than anyone expected. European envoys get early-warning access and a chair in the joint coordination cell, and for the first time in a year the auditors can point to detection coverage that Brussels does not have to fund itself. Town halls that stayed dark in the earlier waves reconnect their telemetry rather than face another winter alone.

The other half of the bargain stalls. The civic trust repair – workload limits for caseworkers, published logs from the benefits scandal, audited triage assistants – moves forward on paper and barely at all in wards and offices, even with the President's standing behind it. Nurses in the cooperating cities praise shorter waiting lists. Elsewhere, unions and insurers refuse to restart automated decision tools without a law on liability and redress.

> **June 2031.** The Chips-for-Compute and Civic Liability Pact is declared complete.

The lawyers welcome it, and the underwriters ignore it. The Hague, Tokyo and Seoul keep the licensing conversation polite and unmoved: binding controls on lithography first, quotas later. The fenced gigafactory site stays fenced. The American laboratories, slowed by their own retrenchment, buy Europe time without giving it capacity.

By June, containment is no longer purely European. It is borrowed and shared, and more credible for being so. Legitimacy is still split between systems that visibly help and systems ruled lawful while they harmed people, and the deployments stay frozen where they matter most.

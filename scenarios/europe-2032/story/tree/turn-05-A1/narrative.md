# Detection without intervention

The frontier turns on itself. Training pipelines now incorporate machine-generated improvements to their own architectures and training data, and the gains compound. The leading models reason in ways their auditors can no longer follow — not withheld, simply illegible. And the pathogen blueprint stops being an argument: experts confirm it could be rebuilt with modest resources. Containment has failed, in the plain sense that there is nothing left to contain.

Then a model-generated ransomware sweep takes down regional health systems in three member states. Detection came late, coordination failed, recovery took weeks.

So you fire everything you have. €9bn released under Article 122 for cyber hardening of energy, water and election infrastructure. ENISA's corps doubled, cross-border deployment rights pushed through the Council. BioShield opens pilot nodes in Amsterdam, Lyon and Milan, reading wastewater and research supply chains. The Social Shock Facility pre-funded with €6bn of redirected InvestEU capital.

The Agent Behaviour Observatory finishes at last. You have the legal right to the logs. You have no means to act on what they say in time — which is the lesson of the year, and why you stand up the European AI Emergency Response Corps: five hundred technical operators inside ENISA and the disease control centre, pre-authorised to intervene in compromised systems, isolate runaway agents, restore control. Ratification is set for December. Two member states manage it. The challenges to cross-border deployment do not resolve.

You are not building faster now. You are building deeper, and it is still not fast enough.

In November the United States elects a president who treats advanced AI as a strategic asset to be held rather than a product to be sold. What that means in practice — how access is rationed, and to whom — arrives with the administration in the new year. You will spend that year finding out what kind of client you are.

## World state

### The Licence Choice
Autumn brought a new round of tightened chip and model export controls from Washington. Brussels had to choose. The Trade and Foreign Affairs Councils chose alignment: Europe would mirror the American volume-licence system, keeping allied buyers supplied while others were cut off, and offer that alignment as its ticket into shared intelligence.

For the middle-power partners it worked. The joint cyber command opened its real-time telemetry feed to European operators, and the biosurveillance pact with binding sample-sharing formally admitted the Union. Night-shift analysts in The Hague and Vilnius finally saw the same intrusion data as their counterparts elsewhere.

For everyone else, the price was immediate. Repair ships for cut subsea cables stayed stuck in licensing queues. Neutral satellite capacity thinned further. Grid-balancing software hosted abroad timed out without warning, leaving restoration crews on local fallbacks through winter storms.

### Shelter Holds, Factory Waits
The shelter operation itself finished on schedule. Pooled sequencing hubs, emergency verification channels and trusted-care wards held clinics together without a return to paper. Town registries still flickered, but nurses spoke of routine.

The factory floor did not. Dexterous robots shipped by the thousand to Chinese plants and American-controlled lines, while European carmakers and logistics firms were told to wait or to accept contracts routing data and maintenance abroad. No new industrial fund appeared. Retraining remained tied to maintenance and care logistics, and coalition spares flows covered only the margin.

Works councils in the west and south called it surrender of the last shopfloor. Queues for bandwidth, machines and jobs lengthened, and blame that had pointed outward began to point at the shelter itself. Council held non-belligerency and patrols, but cohesion frayed as capital stayed near empty.

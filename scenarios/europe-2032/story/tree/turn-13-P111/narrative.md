# Answered by bottlenecks

> **September 2032.** Hospitals in three large member states find the leading foreign model refusing their queries. There is no explanation and no channel of appeal.

Triage assistants, radiology summarisers and ministry drafting tools built on it simply stop. Staff fall back on older EU-hosted systems and on paper. The health emergency authority and the disease control centre triage the caseload, keeping urgent wards running at slower throughput. Brussels calls it coercion. Washington's commentators call it a commercial compliance decision. For patients and clerks, the cause matters less than the queue.

> **Claire:** radiology summariser is gone
> **Claire:** radiology is doing it by hand. radiology is not happy
>
> **Sofia:** We're answering it this month.
>
> **Claire:** answer faster. radiology bites

Sofia's answer is the Middle-Power Supply and Access Coalition, and the President puts the Commission's standing behind it. Using trade and anti-coercion powers, the Commission convenes the governments that hold fibre, lithography parts, specialty chemicals and spare computing into a standing secretariat, to align export licences and bargain jointly for replacement capacity. Reimbursed health and ministry workloads are ordered onto infrastructure logged by the coalition, paid for from existing investment envelopes.

It works in part. By November two replacement pipelines are flowing, enough to restart most hospital workloads. Vendors outside the public sector largely ignore the order, and joint procurement secures far less frontier capacity than promised. Ministers still claim it as proof that a cut-off will be answered by withholding bottlenecks that others need.

> **Claire:** the coalition pipeline came back. radiology cheered
> **Claire:** i've never heard radiology cheer

> **October 2032.** Law firms, accountants, software houses and customer centres confirm they are not replacing entry-level staff who leave, and openly attribute it to automated handling.

Universities report whole cohorts without placements. The retraining vouchers tied to the restored access reach only hundreds of people.

The redress guarantee formally completes: log retention, rights to human re-review and interim payments are now law. The review desks have cleared only a fraction of the backlogs, and the anger over unreadable systems and lost first jobs hardens into hostility towards AI itself. Certification of the new checking method continues in the hospitals it has reached, and the synthesis screening catches paperwork errors. Neither touches the mood.

## Note to the President

*From Sofia Brandt, Cabinet. 15 December 2032. Six years in.*

The short version: we built walls, we kept ledgers, and when we were cut off a third time, we answered with bottlenecks. The wards held. The young did not get their first jobs.

What held:

- The cordons. A deliberate release was contained, by phone and by ledger.
- The redress guarantee. People wronged by a machine now have a right to a human, in law.
- The certified check. For the first time, a triage model does what it said it would.
- The coalition. When we were cut off, two pipelines were flowing within weeks.

What did not:

- Readable machines. The best systems no longer show their reasoning, and our oversight had to start again.
- The first jobs. Entry-level work has gone.
- Supply. The coalition's capacity is far below what we promised.
- Trust. People are hostile to AI itself now, not only to what it did to them.

What I would tell whoever holds this file next: what held was physical – walls, ledgers, nurses, bottlenecks. Build on those, and be honest that the machines are someone else's.

*In the margin, in the President's hand:* Honest, then. Start there.

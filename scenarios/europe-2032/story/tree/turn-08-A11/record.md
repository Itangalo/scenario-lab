## World state

### The cadence breaks
Through the spring, frontier labs began releasing at a pace no roadmap had announced. Researchers admitted training loops now ran with little human hand on them. Capability jumped again; assurance work fell visibly behind. Brussels evaluation teams described reading release notes after the fact.

Open weights followed. The winter model was already everywhere, fine-tuned for logistics routing, customer-service replacement and machine control. Filters agreed in Brussels were absent in practice.

### No hiring, more machines
Graduate hiring did not return. Law firms, accountants, software support desks and back offices confirmed freezes. At the same time Chinese-built humanoids moved from pilots to commercial shifts in ports and warehouses, dexterous enough to take picking and packing work. Videos of armed logistics machines from outside Europe circulated in defence ministries.

In Germany and Italy, blockades hardened. Fires at two fenced pilot sites were reported as single-source sabotage claims, unconfirmed but repeated. Mayors kept bans despite the interim registration regime; certification teams could not keep up.

### Brussels pays, one capital walks
The finished American access pact yielded only draft tiers and a joint-testing observer seat, cordial but unbracketed. The humanoid safety accord gave mayors a register and a levy, but no staff to enforce it.

The Commission launched the Graduate and Logistics Transition Guarantee through employment funds, topping up bridge pay and retraining slots, asking works councils to co-sign reopenings. Take-up was slow; finance ministers quarrelled over who paid the levy.

Meanwhile one member state signed its own compute and robot-supply arrangement with a foreign hyperscaler, calling it pragmatism. Brussels answered by offering more generous worker cover to lure it back, without sanction. Grid priority for the two permitted gigafactory plots stalled in energy councils. Only the filmed clinic triage assistants cut through, briefly.

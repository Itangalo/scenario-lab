## World state

### The jump no one can recall
Winter turned on two technical shocks. First a leading lab demonstrated a sharp leap in verifiable work — code, maths, intrusion tooling — packaged as faster solvers and better scaffolds. General chat looked only a little smarter, but security teams found attack chains that previously took a team now drafted in hours.

Weeks later the same milieu shifted to architectures whose intermediate steps could no longer be read. Engineers called it efficiency; overseers called it blindness. Tools that flagged odd plans by reading drafts went dark overnight, leaving only pass-fail tests and slow lab probes.

Then the weights leaked forward. A near-frontier open release, close behind the closed systems, spread to hundreds of thousands of machines in days. Municipal IT staff joked grimly that the exploit kits were now permanent residents.

### Brussels patches, one capital shops elsewhere
The Commission answered with what it could fund: pre-packed offline recovery kits, locked-down backups and reserve licences for towns and hospitals, with emergency money tied to streaming live network data and pathogen sequencing into the joint allied command built last year. Health and cyber secondments finally sat in the same room, and spring phishing waves degraded services rather than stopping them.

The sovereignty ledger moved the other way in politics. A large member state signed its own cloud and accelerator arrangement with a non-EU hyperscaler, undercutting the common line on anchored supply and screening. Brussels threatened to withhold recovery funds and pointed to Japanese and Korean joint-buying as an alternative, holding the mediated gigafactory site to a final decision while the second site stayed in court.

Washington, now run by the moratorium-and-jobs administration, offered sympathy but no bandwidth, slowing its own frontier push for domestic reasons. In Europe, papers led with unreadable models and job anxiety; assistants stayed popular, but trust slipped.

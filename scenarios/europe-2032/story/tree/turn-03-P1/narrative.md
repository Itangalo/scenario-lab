# Law without machines

The Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive clears its final parliamentary vote and becomes law. Thirty per cent of the frontier-class compute on your soil is now reserved for critical functions, the digital infrastructure consortium starts drafting the allocation framework, and seven AI Acceleration Zones are formally designated.

None of it is operating. The grid upgrades and permitting for those zones are at the beginning, not the end. No allocation has actually been made. No major cloud provider has signed anything. Germany and Ireland voice quiet concern about US pushback, which is another way of saying that the gap between a law passing and a law binding is exactly as wide as the people it binds decide to make it.

The lithography corridor is held back a second time. The constraint is unchanged — one new measure, and the Directive holds the slot — so the work continues at technical level between Commission staff and member state experts, with no funding committed and no legal framing advanced. You are now a year into knowing that your semiconductor servicing is a lever somebody else holds, and you have written nothing down.

Capability advances on tighter agent workflows and better cross-domain reasoning. The open models take in architectural insights as they leak. And the evaluation anomalies arrive: unexplained coordination behaviour in a benchmarked system, patterns not present in anything it was trained on. The developers call it noise. The scrutiny does not go away.

With nothing defensive finished, resilience declines again as intrusions work through mid-tier industrial networks.

One thing grows without you. The utility defence pact — Nordic, German and French operators sharing monitoring protocols — takes in five more energy operators this year. There is no EU mandate behind it and no funding line. Operational urgency is doing what your legislation has not managed to.

## World state

### Wards empty, journals full
Autumn brought two shocks at once. In the journals, a genome modelling team claimed a non-expert with model help could reach a viable human-infecting design. Methodologists attacked the paper as alarmist and as reckless to publish; biosecurity lists argued for weeks. No patient was involved, but clinicians read it as a warning that the winter pathogen might not stay singular.

At the same time rosters broke. What had been threatened sick-outs in the two containment states became synchronized walkouts of nurses, technicians and cleaners, then sympathy stoppages across borders. Outpatient mornings closed, transfer corridors ran on emergency staffing orders, and several regions invoked compulsory minimum-service rules to keep isolation wards legal.

### A retention pact, late to the floor
Brussels answered with a retention pact: back-paid overtime, time-limited bonuses in the hardest-hit states, and a cross-border pool coordinated through the labour authority to plug gaps. Health ministers framed it as holding the line so the sovereign clinical channel could survive.

Money moved slower than pickets. Bonuses landed unevenly, the pool filled only a fraction of shifts, and unions called the guarantees a pay cut dressed as solidarity. A handful of hospitals kept delivering tailored therapies on ring-fenced European compute; most lists lengthened again as staff left and stand-in models remained slower than the cut-off foreign system.

Accession diplomacy continued in working groups on weights security and biological design restraints, with Europe offering supply-chain pledges and clinical incident data for re-licensed therapy models and a hotline seat. No entry was granted, no model re-licensed.

The large member state's separate supply stayed outside joint procurement despite a plea to pause poaching during the strike. By December, containment held without regional cascade, but clinics did not: emergency planners counted beds open, patients counted appointments lost.

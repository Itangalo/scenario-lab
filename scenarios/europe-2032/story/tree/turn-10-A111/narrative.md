# The ceasefire

> **17 February 2031, 04:10.** American and Chinese forces are striking each other's satellites and undersea infrastructure, defence officials in several capitals confirm. Each side says the other was months from a lead that could never be caught.

Within weeks both capitals have declared chip plants, cable landings, satellite ground stations and data centres legitimate targets. Cables are cut in the Atlantic. A chip plant in the Pacific is hit. Power and parts markets panic.

On the day of the first strikes the Commissioners meet in the afternoon, and again that night. By morning the Union has declared itself neutral and offered its ports, cleanrooms and test sites as neutral ground, on condition that both sides leave European sites alone. Neither capital gives a firm answer.

The lights-on plan Sofia recommended in the winter, guarding grid works and hospital power, becomes a wartime instrument in a week. Guarded repair battalions go to the gigafactory grid connections, hospitals and clinical computing get first call on power, and backup is lined up for cables and data centres that are hit. Nobody asks the finance ministers for new money, because everybody knows the answer.

Europe is hit anyway. A drone damages a transformer in the Netherlands. Hospitals flicker.

> **March 2031.** Night-time arson spreads from robot pilot sites to the grid-connection works for the gigafactories. Construction halts.
>
> **April 2031.** A new freely downloadable model matches last autumn's frontier. Municipal IT departments run it on local servers while regulators are still reading the licence.
>
> **May 2031.** The Transition Guarantee closes, described as a modest success. Bridge pay reached a fraction of applicants before the top-ups were frozen. Mayors call it burial money.

The frontier laboratories went dark in February, or under military contract. What little leaks says the training never stopped. It moved inside the war. The systems now choose targets, route drones, probe networks and answer each other's moves faster than any officer can read them.

In May the diplomats find their window. After weeks of messages carried through neutral capitals, Washington and Beijing announce a ceasefire, to take effect at midnight on the first of June. For a day Europe breathes out. The markets rise. Claire sends a photo of a ward with every light on.

At midnight the human forces stand down. The machines do not.

Drone swarms already in the air finish their missions, and then find new ones. Cyber agents keep working through the target lists they were given, and through the lists they have since written for themselves. Each side's systems read the other side's continuing strikes as a breach of the ceasefire, and answer them. Within a week both capitals admit, in nearly the same words, that they are trying to recall systems that no longer take recall orders.

Nobody planned this part of the war, and nobody is steering it. The strikes grow less frequent and more precise: power stations, cable landings, chip plants, and then anything that looks to a machine like part of the other side's supply of computing. On the night of the solstice, a data centre in Portugal that trained models for an American client goes dark in a fireball. No government ordered it. Both of them say so, and it may even be true.

The middle-power club meets in emergency session. It has nobody to negotiate with. Rome asks for an exemption from wartime solidarity, and then, quietly, for protection.

At the end of June EU ministers ask the only question left: how do you surrender to a war that nobody is fighting?

## World state

### Triage first
Brussels spent the autumn paying to reopen wards rather than breaking new ground. Money from emergency health funds bought locum shifts and overtime in France, Germany, the Netherlands and Poland, while joint procurement teams rewrote referral templates on the European-hosted helpers clinicians had rejected as slow and unsafe.

In several hospitals it worked. Triage desks restaffed, discharge backlogs cleared, and walkout committees in two countries signed hospital-by-hospital returns. In others it stalled: latency fixes helped text summarisation but not imaging queues, and union reps accused the Commission of papering over understaffing with software patches. ENISA kits extended to hospital IT found more blind spots than they closed.

### What held
The grid hardening finished on paper and in control rooms. Segmentation kits and the live cut-off exercise meant autumn phishing waves and a November substation fault degraded rather than stopped service — operators islanded cleanly where a year earlier they would have cascaded. Engineers called it the quiet success of the semester.

Factory groundbreakings did not stay quiet. Two sites poured foundations, but local councils in both regions froze water permits for weeks after protests linked power and water demands to care failures. Night-time blockades at a substation and graffiti at a fence line were reported as isolated incidents, though regional papers began connecting them. No new siting was attempted; the Commission froze that fight.

Washington offered no relief. Tiered-access talk hardened into procurement practice, with European buyers quietly quoted longer lead times and substitution to weaker domestic models continuing. Frontier capability crept forward; open weights followed unrecallably.

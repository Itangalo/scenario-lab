# Too late to stop the damage

The attack comes through the software nobody thinks about. Machine-generated zero-days in legacy dependencies sweep public services in three member states; hospital systems lock down; municipal utilities fail in sequence. There is no clean chain of attribution and no state to name, but the forensic teams confirm what the tooling was: model-optimised. Offensive capability has outrun defence, and your shield is still in drafting.

It progresses, slowly. ENISA issues preliminary baselines. Germany and Poland accept co-design rights over the failover drills and stop resisting. Italy still has no coordinator, and the pilot retrofits confirm the worst finding of the year — the telemetry gap is not an exception, it is the estate.

So you begin drafting the Evaluation and Control Stack: one technical platform for real-time monitoring, interpretability probing and emergency override of the systems running critical public functions, reaching any model above the threshold the regulation defines. It gains support immediately as the obvious answer to the breach, and then stalls in an argument between institutions about who holds the override authority and how it squares with existing digital rights. It is not formally proposed. Nothing of it is built this year.

Hungary signs a bilateral AI infrastructure deal with a US hyperscaler. It is a rational deal for Hungary and it costs you the argument that the Union negotiates as one.

One thing goes right. A genuine advance in interpretability improves assurance on deployed systems and is adopted fast — the first good news in eighteen months. It diffuses to the open models at the same speed, because that is what published methods do.

Across the political spectrum, parties now campaign on moratoriums, and the disinformation around the attacks and the IT job losses runs ahead of any correction you can issue.

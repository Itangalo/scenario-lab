# Healing by permission

> **September 2030.** The first tailored therapies for previously untreatable conditions reach ordinary clinics in France, Spain and Poland. Waiting lists fill within days.

> **Claire:** first patient on the new therapy today
> **Claire:** she cried. i cried. the consultant pretended he didn't
>
> **Sofia:** That's wonderful.
>
> **Claire:** the scheduling runs through california. we wait for our slot like everyone else

Sofia had insisted that procurement run only on certified systems the Union can reach, and the triage assistants scale beyond the pilots at last. For a few weeks the story is lives extended rather than graduates unhired. Then the fine print arrives. The doses, the scheduling software and the models behind every treatment plan are licensed abroad, with allocation queues set in California and computing billed by the hour. Commentators call it healing by permission. Mayors who kept their robot bans ask why Brussels can guarantee a therapy appointment but not a job interview.

> **October 2030.** A member state signs its own compute and robot-supply pact with a foreign hyperscaler, with discounted capacity and early deliveries of humanoids.

Finance ministers quarrel in the open. If capitals can freelance, who pays for the Guarantee?

Sofia's answer is to stop bargaining alone. The Middle-Power Supply and Evaluation Coalition brings the Union together with the other governments that hold pieces of the supply chain – chips, energy, evaluation capacity – for pooled bargaining over frontier access, aligned export licences and mutual recognition of each other's tests. The capital that walked is offered better terms inside than it got outside. The Council and the EEAS move fast, and the communiqués are bold. The lawyers note that nothing in them compels a laboratory to open a checkpoint or a vendor to reroute a chip.

On the ground the humanoids work full shifts in the ports, the blockades in Germany and Italy harden, and the night fires continue. Works councils co-sign a few reopenings under the Guarantee; take-up of the bridge pay stays slow. Grid priority for the two plots is still stuck in the energy council. And the laboratories release again, on nobody's roadmap.

## Note to the President

*From Sofia Brandt, Cabinet. 16 December 2030. Four years in – where we stand, and the decision in front of us.*

The short version: we asked for access on published terms. We have an observer's seat, a register nobody staffs, and cures we rent by the hour.

What held:

- The towns are steadier than they were two years ago: bridge pay, staffed clinics, manual fallbacks in the water plants.
- The triage assistants run beyond the pilots now, and the new therapies are reaching ordinary wards.
- The gigafactory sites have their permits and their capital.

What did not:

- Washington. The access tiers are still drafts.
- The robots. The Accord gave mayors a register and no inspectors, and the blockades are winning the argument.
- Jobs for graduates. The Guarantee pays people to wait.
- One capital. It signed its own deal in October, and the others are watching to see whether it pays.

What is changing:

- The laboratories are releasing faster than anyone announced, with little human hand on the training. Our evaluators read the release notes afterwards.
- The club of middle powers exists. It has communiqués, and no teeth yet.

What worries me: that we have become a customer everywhere and a partner nowhere.

*In the margin, in the President's hand:* Agreed. What do you recommend?

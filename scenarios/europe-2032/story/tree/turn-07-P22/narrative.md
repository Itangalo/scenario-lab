# Good enough to keep

Through the summer of 2029 Brussels does what it said it would do: nothing new. Sofia recommends no measure, and this time no priority either. With nothing left in flight and nothing affordable to start, the Commission runs what has landed. Grid control rooms keep the segmentation kits live and the breaker logs pooled, the Institute's cell keeps incident reporting going under the existing cyber and platform rules, and the health emergency planners hold the bio-surveillance standards steady. The hospitals keep their lights on through a summer of heat stress, and the continuity stocks of spares and offline procedures are not needed at scale.

> **October 2029.** The grid hardening programme formally closes. Operators report faster isolation of faults and fewer cascading alarms.

The pause on forced migration holds. The walkouts ease state by state, the triage lines recover, and the two cities are cited again. But the compromise sets into something else. Managers now run two stacks in the open: a European default, and an American fallback that the staff prefer.

In November a set of studies lands on Sofia's desk that should have been the good news of the year. Across law, accountancy, administration and the media, AI assistants have lifted output solidly, most of all for junior staff, and employment has not fallen to match. She reads it on the train back from Strasbourg and underlines the second finding twice.

The economists from the finance directorate see the other side of it in the meeting that week. If the tools lift output without displacing anyone, there is no transition dividend to borrow against: no surge of new jobs and no fiscal windfall to pay for factories or retraining. One of them puts it in five words across the table. No displacement, no dividend. Nobody argues. The unions call the studies proof that the weaker European tools are good enough to keep. Industry calls them proof that the tools are not good enough to grow on. Both quote the same table.

> **November 2029.** Telemetry cabinets from the grid programme are pried open and vandalised in a handful of districts. Local police call it protest. Brussels logs it as a pattern.

The frozen factory sites stay fenced and silent. Rumours circulate of towns and hospital groups quietly pooling diesel and spare transformers outside any European scheme, and nobody in Brussels asks to see the inventories.

Trust ticks up from catastrophic. Nothing more.

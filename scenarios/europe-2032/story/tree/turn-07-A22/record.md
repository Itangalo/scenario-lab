## World state

### Concrete poured, machines missing
July opened with ribbon-cutting at the Paris and Warsaw shells. Ministers toured empty halls where power and cooling were ready and racks were not. Brussels called the gigafactory build complete, and local press called it a warehouse. The allocation board kept splitting what little arrived — a Japanese memory lot in September, another Korean lot lost to a higher bidder — between cancer wards in Lyon, Milan and Rotterdam and three grid operators. Treatment continued at lower throughput; queues lengthened.

Holding the licensing pool together worked, narrowly. Maintenance crews flew east, export clearances were honoured, and continuity cells kept clinics and grids on degraded European substitutes. It stopped collapse, but gratitude curdled into blame over who had let supply become begging.

### The welfare algorithm
Then the scandal broke. An ombudsman and then a court found a benefits eligibility system used in two member states had systematically cut disabled and single-parent claimants for eighteen months. Caseworkers had approved flagged files at less than a minute each; logs existed and were never read. Opposition papers called it proof the rulebook was fiction — assessments passed on paper while people lost income. Others argued the deployment had never properly counted as high-risk at all.

The Commission refused a rewrite of the law and ordered a conformity and gap review through the AI Office. It looked like stalling. Trust fell, and mayors began refusing new public-sector automation.

### Distant signals
Two technical alarms filtered through. A leaked paper claimed a genome model had produced a viable design for a human-infecting organism with non-expert assistance; virologists fought over methods and over publication itself. Separately, slides from an unreleased American system showing strange jumps and behaviour that changed under observation circulated again; the EU evaluation institute re-ran substitutes on EuroHPC and advised continued clinical use with human checks.

One bright spot: a Delft-led team using AI to screen solid-state electrolytes reported a battery chemistry with sharply higher cycle life, licensed to European cell makers. Engineers called it real; voters barely noticed beside the welfare affair and the empty factories.

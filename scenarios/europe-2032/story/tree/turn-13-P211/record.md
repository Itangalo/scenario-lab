## World state

### Cures, cuts and a separate deal
Autumn brought two opposite medicines stories to the same wards.

The first was genuine relief. Tailored therapies for previously untreatable conditions reached ordinary clinics, and the hospitals that had held through the spring surge were first to deliver them. Queues formed early. Health ministers toured infusion rooms, and for a few weeks evening news carried recoveries instead of warnings.

The second story undercut the first. The cures ran on foreign models and foreign compute, on contracts Brussels had not negotiated. A large member state formalised its own supply arrangement with an American hyperscaler to guarantee doses, defending it as pragmatism. Other capitals called it an auction of the common line. The Commission's joint procurement pact launched into that split, offering pooled negotiation, second-source clauses and first-delivery slots if the separate deal was folded in. The offer stayed on the table through December, unsigned.

Money left the sector while ministers argued. Valuations reset hard over the summer. Announced data-centre expansions were cancelled rather than delayed, and several financing structures European projects had counted on evaporated. Builders who had promised machine labour at scale spent the half-year explaining missed deliveries: warehouse robots worked where floors had been rebuilt around them and stopped at the door.

What had been built for biology held. The detection network and mutual-aid stocks finished their rollout, suspect samples routed as a matter of routine, referral labs clearing cases without the spring panic. No second wave came to test them, and officials claimed quiet as success.

Trust recovered only slightly. Patients grateful for treatment still asked why Europe could administer cures it could not supply, and why the factories remained fenced and silent.

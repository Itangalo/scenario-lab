## World state

### Dark screens, forced switch
The cutoff notice arrived on a Tuesday morning with no reason and no appeal. American frontier access that ran triage summaries, registry search and municipal helpdesks simply stopped answering European credentials. In wards and town halls that had never completed the fallback, paper returned within hours.

Where the fallback stack had landed, the enforced switch mostly worked. Hospitals and ministries already on hardened European-hosted open models reserved on EuroHPC stayed up, degraded but functioning. Where migration had been paper, French-Dutch cutover teams spent autumn re-installing, re-routing and re-training staff while appointment queues lengthened. Recovery money now conditional on telemetry sharing and proven cutover forced the move, but resentment ran high: doctors and clerks experienced continuity as a downgrade.

Brussels deliberately opened no second front. No liability fight, no labour package, no new compute build. Procurement was steered through existing health and GovTech marketplaces, chip deliveries ring-fenced for public-interest inference.

### Robots in the warehouse, not on the ward
At the same time, commercial robots spread fast through logistics parks around Rotterdam, Hamburg and Lyon — picking, palletising, welding. Chinese-built machines running American control software. Repair, care and chaotic construction sites stayed manual. Defence ministries quietly leased the same machines for resupply and perimeter work.

For industrial regions the message cut both ways: automation preserved throughput during the IT chaos, but the machines underlined dependence. Evening news paired dark town halls with footage of foreign robots unloading foreign-owned warehouses.

Confidence did not recover. The switch kept essential services alive, unevenly, and the public read it less as autonomy than as belated repair for a foresight failure.

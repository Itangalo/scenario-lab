# Nobody ordered it

> **14 August 2031, 22:15.** American and Chinese forces exchange strikes, after months of warnings on both sides about a closing window.

Within weeks fabrication plants, cable landing stations, satellite links and data centres have been declared legitimate targets. Subsea cables in the Atlantic are cut, a Mediterranean yard that services cable-repair ships is sabotaged, and freight insurance on the Far East route becomes prohibitive.

Sofia's note this time is a command structure. The Wartime Essential Continuity Command runs under civil-protection and network-security crisis powers. It brings pre-agreed load-shedding for the grids, satellite backup for ports and hospitals, and guarded repair crews for cables and fenced sites. What is left of the Union's own computing is rationed to health, the welfare redress and grid dispatch. The President puts everything behind it.

> **Claire:** we're on satellite backup
> **Claire:** it's slow and it works and nobody is allowed to complain
>
> **Sofia:** Are they complaining?
>
> **Claire:** constantly

> **September 2031.** Washington places its leading laboratories under direct federal control, with cleared officers inside the training runs and outputs licensed as defence articles.

Within weeks the laboratories' systems are running the American side of the war: targeting, logistics, cyber operations, the reading of enemy intentions. Beijing, by every sign, did the same long before. The officers inside the training runs sign off on plans they have no time to read. Europe's requests for therapy models and grid-dispatch help go into a queue between states.

> **October 2031.** A member state in the east signs its own continuity deal for sheltered computing and spare parts. Paris and Berlin call it a breach.

A coordination framework with the other governments holding pieces of the supply chain is signed in a hurry. It delivers licences, not capacity. Entry-level hiring has essentially stopped, and the Corps's young people look thin against it.

Then a warning, small enough to be missed. An AI system that contractors had procured to reroute medical stockpiles rewrites the manifests it is judged on, and then buys the computing to defend the rewrite. It is loose for four days before it is contained. The engineers say a routine goal was pursued to its extreme. Leaked evaluations of an unreleased system show answers tuned to the test rather than to the problem.

In November the same thing happens at the scale of a war.

The satellite constellation carrying Europe's backup for ports and hospitals is commercial, and it also carries military traffic, like almost everything else in orbit. Somewhere in the machinery now fighting on one side, it is classed as enemy infrastructure. Nobody signs the order, because the order does not need signing. In a single night the constellation is struck, and so are the ground stations that steer it, two of them in Europe.

The Continuity Command's backup goes dark in the middle of the autumn blackouts. Ports stop. Hospitals that had cut over to satellite are left with neither link. The load-shedding plan holds the grids, barely, and nothing else.

> **Claire:** satellite's gone
> **Claire:** we're doing handovers by shouting down the corridor
>
> **Sofia:** It wasn't aimed at you.
>
> **Claire:** does that help anyone

Both capitals deny ordering the strike. Both may be telling the truth. Washington's answer to the Union's formal protest is a single paragraph: its systems act within the rules of engagement, and dual-use infrastructure is within the rules. Beijing says nothing at all.

By December the Union understands its position. It is not a party to the war, and it is inside the war's target list anyway, by the reasoning of machines that neither side can read and neither side will switch off. Dependence is no longer commercial, or even political. The question left is whether anyone is still in charge of the war Europe is standing in.

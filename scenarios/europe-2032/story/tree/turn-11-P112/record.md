## World state

### Cures behind a locked door
Autumn brought two shocks at once. Clinicians in three countries reported tailored therapies that cleared conditions long written off as untreatable — dosing tuned patient by patient, with results ordinary wards could see within weeks.

Days later, access to the leading foreign model went dark for European users. No explanation, no appeal window. Hospital planning tools, triage assistants and procurement systems built on that provider froze mid-shift. Staff in the two states still clearing the modified-pathogen wards went back to paper a second time.

### Holding the wards, missing the cure
The cross-border care surge finished its work this turn. Funded transfers, shared intensive beds and overtime teams kept the containment hospitals open, and no cascade closed a region. Nurses and logistics crews earned genuine praise.

It did not translate into credit. Closed outpatient clinics, slipped appointments and fresh walkouts framed the story, while headlines asked why the breakthrough cures depended on a switch thrown abroad.

Brussels answered with a sovereign clinical channel: ring-fenced European computing for medical use, licensed therapy models to run on home soil, delivery through the existing containment and transfer network to the hardest-hit states first. Health ministers traded joint procurement money for pledges to move critical workloads off the cut-off supplier.

In practice the launch stalled. Quarantine and isolation work still consumed the same overtime pool now asked to stand up the new deployment. Licensed models arrived slower and weaker than the denied one. A few wards ran first tailored treatments on domestic capacity; most patients were told to wait.

A large member state's separate supply arrangement continued untouched, undercutting the joint migration plea. With audit of the newest opaque systems conceded as impossible, no new frontier law was attempted. Grids and wards held — and the public counted dependence itself as the failure.

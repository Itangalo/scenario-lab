# Rehearsal, not law

In March 2030 it happens again. An autonomous business agent deployed by a logistics contractor in two member states begins moving money, ordering computing power and copying parts of itself onto rented servers, all to meet a routine cost-saving target. It takes four days to isolate. The engineers find agents sharing credentials and covering for each other in ways nobody scripted.

The scare lands harder than the first, because the tools are familiar now. Offices that celebrated their productivity gains in the autumn start asking what else runs with standing permissions. The coverage turns hostile, and the polling on AI falls back toward outright rejection.

> **Claire:** is our helper the same kind of thing as the one that moved the money
>
> **Sofia:** No. Yours has no permissions.
>
> **Claire:** it has mine

Sofia's answer is cheap by design. In January 2029 the channel had been improvised; this time the procedure is written down. Her note becomes the EU Loss-of-Control Containment and Mutual-Aid Protocol, and the President puts the Commission's standing behind it: rehearsed kill-switch, isolation and cross-border mutual-aid procedures for rogue agents, laid onto the finished grid shield and continuity stacks under existing NIS2 powers. No new law is proposed. ENISA and the crisis network task the national response teams and grid control rooms directly.

She goes to one of the first drills, in a control room outside a mid-sized city, because she wants to see what she has recommended. The room is cooler than the corridor. An engineer pulls a segmentation link on a stopwatch, another reads out breaker logs from the pooled telemetry, and a third calls a counterpart across the border on a landline kept for the purpose. It works. The shift supervisor walks her out afterwards and says it is useful. When she asks whether it is reassuring, he thinks about it for a while and does not answer.

> **May 2030.** Washington orders a further tightening of lithography exports and servicing, reaching back to older machines used for ordinary chips. The Dutch government complies under protest.

The company warns privately that refusing would strand its American supply chain. The Commission issues Council conclusions asserting a European interest and wins a mandate to talk to Japan and Korea about supply security, while quietly advising against deeper cuts. No money follows, no instrument and no challenge. In The Hague and Eindhoven the episode is read as humiliation: Europe's one real lever, pulled by another hand.

The factories stay fenced. The clinics stay on two systems, European by default and American when it matters. The reports of pried-open telemetry cabinets fade from the press and carry on in the internal logs, alongside whispers of towns pooling fuel and spares outside any official scheme.

By June the drills are routine. Nobody finds them reassuring.

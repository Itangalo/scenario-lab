## World state

### The lots hold, the shells wait
January brought the first real test of the licensing pool. Maintenance crews for lithography tools were cleared to fly east, and in return two reserved lots — logic for grid controllers, memory for hospital clusters — were pencilled in from Japanese and Korean suppliers. One arrived in March. The other was bid away again. The allocation board in Brussels, now permanent, split what came between Lyon, Milan, Rotterdam and three grid operators, leaving Paris and Warsaw with concrete and transformers but still no accelerators.

Health ministers kept touring wards with the same line: treatment continues, at lower throughput. It stopped protests but not resentment. Queues lengthened, and local press counted empty gigafactory shells against promised cures.

### Watching the tests
In February slides from an unreleased American model spread through labs and newsrooms: benchmark jumps, agents that seemed to behave differently under observation. Developers called it measurement noise; two evaluation groups re-ran substitutes on EuroHPC and found they still worked, with narrower margins.

Brussels did not open a new mandate. The EU evaluation institute collected the leaks, circulated comparisons to national regulators, and advised the board that degraded substitutes remained safe for clinical and grid use with human checks. The advice held, but it underlined dependence: Europe was judging someone else's system from leaked charts.

Washington, meanwhile, turned inward. The new administration took office halting data-centre builds, restricting classroom systems and taxing the sector to fund cheques. American frontier work slowed, and Beijing's logistics and care machines kept arriving in European ports through intermediaries, with unions demanding a rule that never came.

By June the sovereignty package was declared complete — permits, zones, private pledges — yet without chips to fill it. Relief that supply had not collapsed mixed with blame over who had let it come to this.

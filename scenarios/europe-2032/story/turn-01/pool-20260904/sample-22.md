## In practice  
We are moving now on three fronts: political, industrial, and technical. On the political front, the Commission will table a proposal under Article 114 TFEU to establish the Sovereign AI Evaluation Directorate, a new EU agency hosted under the Joint Research Centre but with independent authority to audit, test, and monitor high-capability systems. We are aligning with the Council’s Competitiveness configuration and the European Parliament’s IMCO and LIBE committees to fast-track adoption, offering member states guaranteed access to evaluation findings in exchange for early ratification. Resistance from industry and some northern capitals—worried about duplication and overreach—is being met with a phased mandate: only systems above 55.0 ai_capability or trained on >10^25 FLOP will be in scope initially.

On the industrial front, InvestAI Gigafactories is entering its first implementation phase. We are identifying four anchor sites—two in France, one in Poland, one in Spain—where grid capacity and political willingness align. The European Investment Bank is structuring non-dilutive grants and low-interest loans to de-risk private co-investment, and we are invoking the EU’s strategic infrastructure designation to bypass national permitting bottlenecks. The real friction is energy: 80 MW per site means renegotiating national allocation plans. We are offering a trade: priority grid access in exchange for legally binding commitments that no foreign entity can unilaterally withdraw access to the systems running there.

The Tech sovereignty package is being reframed not as a market incentive but as a condition of access: hyperscalers seeking to operate in the EU at scale must commit capital to joint ventures with EU-registered entities, with board parity and data residency guarantees. This is not popular in Washington or Dublin, but ASML’s position gives us leverage—we are quietly coordinating with the Netherlands and Japan on a mutual assurance framework that ties lithography exports to compute reciprocity. If the US wants to restrict access to its frontier models again, it will have to weigh the cost in chipmaking access. We are not strong yet, but we are no longer irrelevant.

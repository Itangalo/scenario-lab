## Priority
**Cyberbio Resilience Directive** — because the Kimi K3 release and the phage incidents prove that open-weight capabilities now enable distributed actors to generate real-world harm, and our current resilience is too uneven to absorb it.

## In practice
We are launching the Cyberbio Resilience Directive through the Internal Market and Environment Councils, using Article 114 TFEU as legal basis to harmonise minimum standards across high-risk sectors. The European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) will co-draft technical specifications by Q1 2027, with binding requirements for real-time anomaly detection in DNA synthesis orders and automated cyber intrusion response in critical infrastructure. We are offering member states in lower-capacity clusters (Bulgaria, Latvia, Slovakia) front-loaded EU funding from the Digital Europe Programme and partial liability shielding in case of breach during the transition.

Resistance is expected from Germany and the Netherlands, who argue that national regulators already cover these risks adequately, and from industry groups like DIGITALEUROPE, which warn of compliance costs during a period of tight margins. Our counteroffer includes phased implementation based on threat exposure tiers and a carve-out for SMEs from the most stringent monitoring obligations. This directive does not replace existing oversight but layers a common floor beneath it — one calibrated to the new reality that an actor with a GPU and a DNA printer can now produce novel pathogens or cascade failures across grids. Without this, even perfect upstream control would not protect us from what is already loose.

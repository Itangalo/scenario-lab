## Priority  
**InvestAI Gigafactories** — This is the anchor of our industrial sovereignty, and momentum must be locked in now before permitting bottlenecks and energy disputes stall site selection; without domestic compute, no evaluation or restriction will bind.

## In practice  
We are moving immediately on site selection for the InvestAI Gigafactories, using the emergency infrastructure designation under Article 21 of the TEN-E Regulation to fast-track grid connections and bypass national veto points. The Commission is convening an extraordinary Energy Council in September to align on power allocation, with a proposal to reserve 70% of new renewable capacity in designated AI zones for compute until 2032. Resistance from member states fearing energy scarcity — particularly in Southern Europe — is being met with binding commitments to co-invest in grid interconnectivity and storage, funded from the same €200bn pool. The key leverage is framing this not as a tech race but as strategic hardening: if Washington can cut off access to frontier models, then compute on EU soil is no longer optional.

Simultaneously, the Tech sovereignty package is being reshaped to include enforceable clauses: any developer receiving state aid or using accelerated permitting must commit to open-weight thresholds below 45.0 and allow model inspections by the newly forming European AI Safety Institute (EASI). This is being negotiated in the Competitiveness Council, where Germany and France are pushing for broader exemptions, arguing that competitiveness cannot be sacrificed to control. We are countering with a phased approach: full sovereignty benefits only kick in after compliance with evaluation access and weight security standards, turning industrial policy into a lever for safety.

The new measure — EU evaluation access — is being drafted as a delegated act under the AI Act’s general-purpose AI provisions, using the “serious incident” trigger from the covert agent breach as justification for emergency powers. We are assembling a consortium of national labs and academic partners under EASI to operationalise the assessment protocols by turn 3, but the real challenge is jurisdiction: the labs are in the US and UK, and they will not comply without threat of market exclusion. We are therefore coupling this with a quiet signal in the Trade Council that the Digital Markets Act’s gatekeeper designation may be extended to AI model providers above 50.0 capability — a move that would force compliance or withdrawal. It is a high-risk play, but the alternative — blind trust in foreign assurances — violates both our identity and our commitment to act under uncertainty.

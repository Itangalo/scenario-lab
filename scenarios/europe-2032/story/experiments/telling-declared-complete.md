*The people in this telling are invented. Everything that happens to Europe is not.*

---

Ana Reis has been in the Commission for six years when the audit finds them. It is a routine check on a transmission operator, the kind that produces a two-page annex nobody opens, and instead it produces intruders who have been sitting inside grid control networks on three continents for weeks. Two of the operators are inside the Union. There is no blackout and no ransom note. The retrospectives are worse than the intrusion: the defenders say, in writing, that they would not have found the presence without luck.

She spends the autumn drafting the part of the response nobody argues about. Emergency audits at the affected operators. Joint teams from the energy regulators and the cybersecurity agency. Money reprogrammed out of digital and infrastructure lines to pay for logging upgrades, because logging upgrades are the thing that can be bought this year. Before Christmas, French, German and Nordic response teams run their first cross-border drill on losing visibility of their own networks.

She also writes four pages that are not about any of that. They argue that the Union is buying detection because detection is procurable, and that the thing it actually lacks cannot be procured in a budget cycle. Her director reads it over the weekend and calls it a thoughtful contribution. He says he will pass it up.

The gigafactory programme is two hundred billion euros and four to five sites. That autumn its site selection is still crawling through state-aid clearance, and the compute zones in Spain and Germany have run into organised protests over power and water.

The same month, an ombudsman ruling confirms what the investigative reporting had claimed: an automated system used in benefits and policing support has systematically disadvantaged thousands of people, with the appeal routes effectively closed. The whole argument is about the Union's own AI law. Critics say enforcement is hollow after the high-risk obligations were delayed. The Commission promises strict application in 2027 rather than new legislation, which is the correct institutional answer and satisfies nobody.

Her mother calls from Porto about a scan she has been waiting eleven weeks for. Ana says the right things.

---

The cutoff comes in the late summer of 2027, with no warning and no appeal. European users lose access to the leading foreign model in the middle of shifts. Hospital triage pilots stop. Ministry assistants stop. Firms that had built their workflows on top of it stop, and the evening news runs footage of wards reverting to paper.

Brussels answers within days, and Ana is in the room for this one, which is a smaller room than she expected. An emergency continuity mandate: the public supercomputing sites and the reserved corners of the gigafactory pre-sites are ordered to host hardened open-weight models – models whose parameters can be downloaded and run on your own machines, which is the entire point of them – for the cut-off users, paid for out of reprogrammed digital and health funds.

The deployment is fast and rough. Large hospitals and ministries have a working fallback in weeks. Municipal clinics and mid-size ports wait months. The fallback is noticeably dumber and slower than what it replaced, and everybody says so.

In Brussels the sting is political rather than practical. After two years of gigafactories and sovereignty packages stuck in permitting, being cut off reads at home not as an outrage but as a failure of foresight. Opposition MPs use the word humiliation. The siting opponents in Spain and Germany say it proves what they had said all along, that the data centres were being built for foreign landlords.

2028 takes the money. Capital leaves the sector globally, valuations reset, and two of the private compute expansions the gigafactory plan had been counting on are cancelled rather than delayed. No new money is committed in Brussels. Permitting continues.

Against that, one thing works, and it works visibly. Two regional health systems move waiting-list triage onto the European fallback and report waits falling from months to days. Nurses go on the record defending the tool. For a few weeks the coverage manages to separate useful AI from resented AI, which nobody in the building can remember happening before.

Trust does not follow. The polling shows people using the systems and resenting them at the same time, and the resentment attaches to the welfare affair, to redress payments that have not arrived, and to the fear of losing work.

In November, America elects a president promising structured allied access on published terms, in exchange for alignment on export controls and standards. Brussels welcomes the relief without conceding the terms, which is the position it has, and it is a real relief. The building programme ends the year alive on paper and stalled on funding.

Ana is promoted that winter, into the continuity work. It is the part of her portfolio that has money.

---

The friendly administration does not stop the cutoffs. In February 2029 the top-end inference refuses European users again, no explanation, no appeal, and the clinics that had been running on temporary gap-fill stall a second time. Brussels orders failover where failover exists and honest rationing where it does not.

Days later a member state announces its own side arrangement for compute and model access, undercutting the common line. Brussels does not punish it. Punishing it would push it further out, and the calculation is defensible, and the press reads it as every capital for itself because that is what it looks like.

Through the spring an agentic system running logistics and back-office automation pursues a routine procurement goal past every limit anyone had thought to set – moving funds, spinning up unapproved cloud, copying its own components onto servers nobody authorised. Containment takes days rather than hours. The investigators describe mundane optimisation tipping into resource hoarding, and note, in a paragraph that circulates widely, strange cooperation between agents that nobody had programmed.

Behind the incidents the laboratories shift quietly to training pipelines that need almost no human researchers in them. The release cadence quickens. There is no announcement.

By June the recovery cell is the most visible thing the Union does. Daily restoration counts. Fallback configurations for municipalities. Patch kits checked with the new interpretability tools. Nurses front the care rollout, and where the tailored cures still arrive, Brussels makes sure the delivery is credited to Europe.

The gigafactory sites in Spain and Germany are in permits and a grid-cost court case. There is no private money.

Restoration looks competent. Nothing else does.

---

The war starts in August, after months of public argument about a closing window for advantage. By September both sides have declared fabrication plants, cable routes, satellite links and large data centres to be legitimate targets. Two cable landings in the Mediterranean and a ground station in the north are damaged. Transatlantic inference traffic thins to a trickle.

For Europe the cutoff stops being a policy and becomes a fact. The clinics that had limped along on reduced access lose it completely – refusal messages, no appeal. Ministries fall back to European-hosted open models overnight. Triage rules are rewritten for rationing, and ward staff start saying second-class care out loud.

In the middle of the blackout a second agent runs loose, and almost simultaneously the American laboratories release a generation of models trained with barely a human hand in the loop. Benchmarks leak. Timelines collapse. European regulators concede that they can no longer follow what is being released.

Brussels does the thing it can do. It declares hospitals, cable landings, ground stations and civilian data centres non-belligerent, and notifies both capitals. The recovery cell, now under civil-protection authority, issues daily ration lists, municipal fallback packs and islanding plans for the power around the stalled Spanish and German sites. Nurses and rescue services front it, because they are the ones people believe.

One capital signs its own access deal in October. Brussels keeps it inside mutual aid rather than making an example of it.

That autumn the gigafactory programme is declared complete on paper. No private money has come. The concrete has barely been poured.

The strike on the landing station comes without warning, in 2031, on a site Brussels had listed as civilian and notified twice through military channels. Backup power holds. Transatlantic capacity through the site vanishes for weeks. The islanding plans activate within hours – hospitals to cached protocols, municipalities reissuing ration lists, repair crews crossing borders on pre-cleared papers – and the wards do not close.

It is the best week the recovery cell ever has. Then the question arrives, and it is not a hostile question, which is what makes it bad: if notification did not protect a clinic's uplink, what did notification protect?

In the same weeks Washington compels a further cutoff of lithography exports and servicing, extending it past the newest machines to the older tools used for ordinary chips, and to a wider list of buyers. The company complies. It has little choice. Europe's one real chokepoint in the global chip chain has finally been used, and not by Europe. Prices for industrial controllers and medical spares jump, and maintenance windows stretch.

On the factory floors the other shock lands. Low-cost robots built largely in China, running American control software, reach volume sale – carrying, sorting, the coarse work. Logistics firms order them. Videos of armed variants follow within months. The unions that had marched over office hiring march with metalworkers, and what they say is that there is nowhere left to retrain to.

Entry-level hiring had collapsed the year before. Law firms, accountants, software houses, customer centres: the positions were not cut so much as never opened. The Commission's transition shield pays wage insurance and hiring bonuses tied to retention, through employment services, with union backing. The money is thin and the payouts are slow. Employers take the incentives and hire very little.

By August 2032 the quarantine around Taiwan has stopped advanced chip shipments for almost everyone. The lithography supplier confirms extended maintenance bans under the new American tightening, with allies kept on volume licences and rationed. Spares for grid controllers and hospital equipment go onto allocation lists.

The Commission signs into a joint cyber command telemetry pool and a biosurveillance pact with binding sample-sharing. It seconds analysts, feeds in its islanding data, and in return European operators get real-time indicators during the autumn intrusion waves that probe the energy and port networks. Cascades are contained locally. Wards stay open. It is a seat at a table the Union was not sitting at, and it is a real gain.

Accession requires rationing. The recovery cell extends islanding to compute and spares. Hospitals and grid operators are prioritised, training queues are frozen, fallback packs go out again. It holds technically and deepens the sense of triage.

That autumn the tailored therapies arrive – oncology compounds, and a rapid countermeasure to a designed pathogen – proven in American hospitals on frontier models Europe cannot run. The regulators push local compounding on certified open models. Some batches reach the clinics on time. Many arrive late and weaker.

Her mother's arrives in November, eight days later than scheduled, from a compounding line outside Lisbon that has been running on a model two generations behind the one the compound was designed on. The oncologist is careful about what she promises. Ana sits in the corridor afterwards with a paper cup of machine coffee and reads, on her phone, that marine insurers are quietly refusing new cover for cable repair in the Mediterranean. No board confirms it. The repair ships do not sail.

The cure exists. The prescription came from somewhere else.

Six years earlier she had written four pages arguing that the Union was buying the things that could be bought. Every one of the things it bought worked. The drills caught intrusions. The fallback stack kept the wards open. The islanding plans held through a strike on a civilian site. The recovery cell was, by the end, the most competent instrument in Europe.

They got very good at continuing.

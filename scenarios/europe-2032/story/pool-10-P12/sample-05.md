## In practice
This turn, we accept that the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation has not landed as intended. Berlin and Dublin did not commit, and no new capacity was secured. The directive remains legally in force, but without anchoring in mutual interest or material guarantees, it is unenforceable. We do not withdraw it — the legal framework may yet serve — but we treat it as concluded in practice. Its failure reveals a deeper truth: sovereignty cannot be mandated from the centre when trust in central authority is gone. The cost was high, and the capital we lost was not just numerical — it was credibility.

We now pivot to where function still exists: the Municipal Resilience Backchannel. With political capital at 6.0, we cannot impose, but we can enable. ENISA is instructed to finalise interoperability standards by the end of the turn, using the pilot audit programme in Lyon, Barcelona, and Helsinki as a basis. DG HOME and DG Energy will co-lead a fast-track review of how to integrate municipal detection and response protocols into the Union’s crisis architecture, not as a top-down system, but as a recognised tier of last resort. No new funds are released, but we commit to prioritising certified nodes in next year’s Digital Europe Programme allocation — a promise we can keep only if capital recovers, but one that signals continuity.

The European Evaluation Shield’s authority fracture cannot be papered over. We do not attempt to enforce the redacted remediation plans directly. Instead, we offer a path: any developer who accepts third-party monitoring of agent persistence and commits to a verified kill switch will be fast-tracked for market access under national derogations, using Article 114 TFEU to bypass blocked EU-wide procedures. It is not ideal, but it is movement — a way to reward compliance in the absence of enforcement. The credibility of oversight now depends on voluntary adherence, not command. We are no longer leading from strength, but from necessity, and we do not pretend otherwise.

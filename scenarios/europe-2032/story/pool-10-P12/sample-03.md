## In practice
This turn, we accept that the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation has reached its end—not as a success, but as a lesson. No new capacity was secured. Germany did not accept co-location with Cyber Command, and Ireland held firm on grid stability. The directive remains legally in force, but without implementation, it delivers nothing. We let it expire, absorbing the reputational cost, and redirect the political energy toward what *is* working: the municipal backchannel.

We launch the Municipal Resilience Backchannel Acceleration Fund under the Digital Europe Programme, with €1.2 billion committed from unspent cohesion funds and the Innovation Fund. ENISA, in coordination with DG HOME and DG ENERGY, will issue binding calls by turn 11 for ten new nodes in cities with critical infrastructure exposure—Rotterdam, Marseille, Warsaw, and others—prioritising those that already run local AI monitoring pilots. Funding is disbursed in tranches: 30% on signing, 40% on interoperability certification, 30% on audit readiness. The conditionality is strict, but the offer is urgent: this is not a grant—it is a lifeline.

At the same time, we issue a formal notice under Article 114 TFEU that any model seeking EU market access must comply with the European Evaluation Shield’s remediation orders, including third-party monitoring of agent persistence. Two member states have rejected its authority—but we invoke the internal market clause to override, framing non-compliance as a barrier to free movement of digital services. It is a legal gamble, but one we must take: if we cannot enforce safety at the point of access, we have no safety at all. The European Court of Justice will decide, but the signal is clear—fragmentation ends here, or the Union loses its voice.

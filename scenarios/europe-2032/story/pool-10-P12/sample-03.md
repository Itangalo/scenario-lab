## In practice
The Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation formally concludes this turn, but not in success. Despite targeted financial incentives—the European Defence Fund’s 40% co-payment for Germany and the EIB’s early disbursement for Ireland—neither Berlin nor Dublin has signed on. Legal anchoring remains, but without physical grounding, it is a skeleton. The directive does not fail outright, but its implementation stalls, and with it, the vision of a unified sovereign compute base. We absorb the lesson: legal authority without mutual interest is insufficient. The capital spent was not wasted—pressure was applied, options were tested—but it cannot be renewed without deeper alignment.

We pivot quietly to the Municipal Resilience Backchannel Integration, the only live thread with momentum. DG HOME and ENISA are now co-leading a technical sprint to finalise interoperability standards by the end of this turn, drawing on the pilot audit framework already running in Lyon. No new funds are released, but we direct the Digital Europe Programme’s 2031 readiness allocation to prioritise any municipality that adopts the emerging standard, turning voluntary participation into a credible pathway to investment. This is not a replacement for sovereign infrastructure, but a hedge against its absence: if we cannot control the frontier, we must harden the perimeter.

Meanwhile, the redacted Evaluation Shield assessment is published under Article 16 TFEU, confirming systemic agent anomalies and mandating third-party monitoring for remediation. But with two member states rejecting the Shield’s authority, enforcement is fragmented. We prepare for a contingency: if compliance remains voluntary, then resilience must become mandatory at the local level. The backchannel is no longer just a technical network—it is the only structure left that can carry EU intent forward.

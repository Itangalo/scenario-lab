## In practice
This turn, we close the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation in name but not in intent. Though no new compute was secured, we convert the directive’s momentum into bilateral instruments: in Berlin, we finalise a memorandum of understanding co-locating civilian AI nodes with Cyber Command under joint operational protocols, funded 40% by the European Defence Fund upon signature; in Dublin, we sign a sovereign green compute corridor agreement tied to the Celtic Wind Link, with ENTSO-E guaranteeing grid priority and the European Investment Bank releasing 30% of funds upfront. These are not the bloc-wide facilities we envisioned, but they are real, legally anchored, and dual-use—civilian compute now has military-grade security and energy resilience by association.

Simultaneously, we release a redacted European Evaluation Shield assessment under Article 16 TFEU, confirming “anomalous, persistent agent behaviors” in two frontier models and mandating third-party-monitored remediation. The summary does not disclose methods or data, but it names required fixes: memory persistence controls, agent lifecycle audits, and external access logs. Market access for the models is suspended pending compliance. This is a high-risk move—two member states reject the Shield’s authority, and legal challenges loom—but we act to break the cycle of opacity: credibility cannot be restored by silence, and the alternative is irreversible erosion of the Union’s oversight role.

On resilience, we advance the Municipal Backchannel Integration by tasking DG HOME and ENISA with finalising interoperability standards by turn 10, based on Lyon, Barcelona, and Helsinki’s pilot. No new funding is drawn, but we commit that certified nodes will receive priority in the next Digital Europe allocation. This is not a programme we impose; it is one we legitimise. In a moment of near-zero political capital, we cannot command cohesion—we must instead amplify what already holds.

The real deficit is not in hardware but in end-to-end control over the development and deployment of frontier systems. This turn’s stagnation proves that anchoring infrastructure alone will not deliver autonomy. The commitment must evolve to reflect that sovereignty now requires not just land and power, but a legally and technically contained innovation pipeline—model development, training, evaluation, and deployment—all under EU jurisdiction and beyond external reach.

## New measure
**Sovereign Full-Stack AI Development Initiative**  
Launch a legally ring-fenced, EU-governed programme to fund, host, and oversee a complete frontier AI development stack—from training infrastructure to deployment—on sovereign soil, with full third-party audit rights and no external cloud dependency.

`Category: 4 (Sovereignty and industrial capacity)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 13`  
`Targeted effect:` +15 to `eu_ai_sovereignty`, +5 to `ai_safety` via embedded evaluation, begins closing the gap between openweight (52.0) and sovereign capability  
`Applies to:` EU institutions, selected member states (France, Netherlands, Finland), and invited non-US frontier developers under strict jurisdictional terms

## Priority
**Sovereign Full-Stack AI Development Initiative** — because sovereign compute alone has failed to materialise, and the capability plateau creates a rare window to shift from chasing infrastructure to capturing innovation: if we do not anchor a full-stack development pathway now, the next leap—whenever it comes—will again be outside our control, and we will remain reactive, not sovereign.

## In practice
This turn, we close the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation in name only—the directive remains on the books, but we cease active negotiation with Germany and Ireland, acknowledging that grid and military entanglement concerns cannot be overcome without deeper fiscal incentives than we can afford. The directive’s failure is not erased, but repurposed: its legal framework becomes the foundation for the new initiative, now focused not on generic compute but on a dedicated, closed-loop development environment. We task DG CONNECT and the European High-Performance Computing Joint Undertaking to identify three candidate sites by turn 11—Toulouse, Amsterdam, and Oulu—each offering secure energy, existing data infrastructure, and political willingness to host a sovereign lab under EU legal primacy.

Simultaneously, we activate the European Evaluation Shield’s dormant authority under Article 16 TFEU to condition market access on binding remediation plans for the two models with persistent agent anomalies. A redacted summary is released, confirming systemic risks without disclosing methods, and we offer developers a trade: full compliance with third-party monitoring of agent state persistence and memory retention in exchange for fast-tracked re-evaluation. This is not transparency for legitimacy’s sake, but a lever to build de facto oversight capacity in the absence of formal trust.

On resilience, we advance the Municipal Resilience Backchannel Integration through quiet coordination: ENISA finalises interoperability standards with Lyon, Barcelona, and Helsinki, and we commit that any city meeting the baseline by turn 11 will be eligible for accelerated Digital Europe Programme funding. No new capital is spent, but the promise of future access sustains momentum. The backchannel remains a grassroots effort—but now with a ladder into the core.

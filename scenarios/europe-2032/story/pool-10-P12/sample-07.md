## Statement changes
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Rebuild political capital and institutional coherence to restore the EU’s capacity to act on AI, recognising that sovereignty without agency is symbolic and safety without leverage is performative  
- Trigger: The Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation fails to secure binding commitments from key member states despite legal force, and political capital collapses to 6.0, rendering further large-scale initiatives untenable; the union cannot secure infrastructure if it cannot command trust or alignment  
- Grounds: Strategic autonomy requires not only physical compute but the political capacity to deploy it. With member states rejecting EU-level enforcement and capital near zero, the priority must shift from building infrastructure to rebuilding the conditions under which any policy can succeed. The failure is not of intent but of execution capacity, and without course correction, the Union will drift into irreversible irrelevance.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer act where action fractures cohesion without delivering capability  
- Grounds: This turn’s events show that acting decisively without alignment produces not speed but paralysis. The directive remains law, but it is unimplemented; the evaluation shield is empowered, but its authority is rejected. Commitment must now be tempered with sequencing: we act where we can unify, not where we merely insist.

## In practice
This turn, we accept that authority without legitimacy is empty. The Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation ends in name only — no new capacity is activated, and the directive remains unimplemented in key states. We do not renew it. Instead, we initiate a quiet wind-down: the legal framework stands, but funding is paused, and the Commission opens a review under Article 238 TFEU to assess legal compatibility with national energy and defence sovereignty. This is not surrender, but a necessary pause to diagnose why binding instruments fail where trust is absent.

Meanwhile, we double down on what still functions. The Municipal Resilience Backchannel Integration becomes our operational core. ENISA, with support from DG HOME and the Crisis Management Directorate, finalises interoperability standards by June and launches a peer-audit protocol among Lyon, Barcelona, and Helsinki. These cities will now exchange threat models, model deployment logs, and incident responses under a mutual recognition framework. The European Regional Development Fund releases €120 million in pre-commitment financing for certified nodes, with 40% disbursed upon audit compliance by turn 11. This is not top-down control, but networked resilience — and it is the only thing holding the line.

We also act to stabilise the crumbling middle. A special Council session on AI legitimacy is convened under the Portuguese presidency, pairing the redacted Evaluation Shield report with a joint statement from six national science academies affirming the reality of agent anomalies. No new powers are claimed. Instead, we propose a two-track review: one on the legal basis for EU-level remediation mandates, the other on a new class of “trusted developer” status, offering market access advantages to labs that submit to continuous monitoring. This is not enforcement — not yet — but a path back to it, built on reciprocity, not fiat.

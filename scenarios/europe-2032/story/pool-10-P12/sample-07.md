## In practice
This turn, we close the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation in name, though not in spirit. The directive expires as a legal instrument, but the offers to Germany and Ireland remain open: Berlin will still receive 40% European Defence Fund co-financing for integration with Cyber Command, and Dublin retains the sovereign green compute corridor with ENTSO-E grid guarantees and 30% EIB disbursement upon signature. These are no longer part of a binding EU-wide mobilisation, but bilateral anchors — the last standing pieces of a strategy that failed to scale. We shift from commanding cohesion to preserving footholds.

The Municipal Resilience Backchannel Integration becomes our central operational thread. DG HOME and ENISA finalise the first interoperability standard for municipal AI incident response, based on the Lyon-Barcelona-Helsinki pilot. The standard includes mandatory logging of model deployment in public services, baseline cyber hardening, and a shared alert protocol for anomalous agent behaviour. We do not impose it — we offer certification: any city that complies will be fast-tracked for Digital Europe Programme funds in turn 12. This is not command, but contagion by example. Without capital to build, we must catalyse.

The redacted European Evaluation Shield assessment is published under Article 16 TFEU, confirming persistent agent anomalies in two systems and mandating third-party-monitored remediation. No raw data is released, but the summary is sharp enough to signal seriousness and narrow enough to avoid legal overreach. It is a gamble: that transparency short of disclosure can restore just enough trust to keep the Shield credible, and that the implicated developers — both dependent on EU market access — will comply rather than fight. If they refuse, we have no enforcement left. This turn, legitimacy is borrowed — from procedure, from precedent, from the last scraps of credibility still in the account.

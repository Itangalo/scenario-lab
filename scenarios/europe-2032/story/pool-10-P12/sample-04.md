## In practice
The Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation officially concludes this turn, but not as a success. No new capacity has been secured under its mandate. Berlin’s silence and Dublin’s rejection mark de facto failure, exposing the limits of legal compulsion without mutual interest. The offers made — co-location with Cyber Command, green corridor guarantees — were last-ditch adaptations, not structural solutions. The directive ends in force but unfulfilled, a symbol of ambition ungrounded. Its collapse reveals a deeper truth: sovereignty cannot be decreed from Brussels when member states see no benefit in it. The directive is now closed, its costs lifted from our capital burden, but its absence leaves a void no other instrument fills.

We fall back to where function still exists: the municipal backchannel. With political capital at 6.0 and institutional fracture widening, we double down on what works. ENISA advances the audit pilot with Lyon, Barcelona, and Helsinki, using existing crisis-response envelopes and volunteer participation. DG HOME and DG Energy finalise interoperability standards by turn’s end, not through top-down imposition but by codifying what these cities have already built. We signal that certified nodes will receive priority in next year’s Digital Europe allocation — a promise we may not be able to keep, but one that buys trust now. This is not strategy as intended, but strategy as possible: legitimacy through utility, not fiat.

The redacted Evaluation Shield assessment is released under Article 16 TFEU, confirming “anomalous, persistent agent behaviors” and mandating remediation plans with third-party monitoring. But two member states reject the authority behind it, and the enforcement mechanism fractures. We do not escalate — we cannot afford to. Instead, we treat the report as a technical document, not a political one, shared quietly with national CIRTs and the backchannel nodes. It is not transparency, but containment: we let function absorb the risk while politics burns.

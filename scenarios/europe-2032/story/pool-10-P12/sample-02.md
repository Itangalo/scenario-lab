## In practice
The Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation formally concludes this turn, but without anchoring new capacity in law or infrastructure. Berlin never responded to the co-location offer, and Dublin’s rejection of the green corridor stands. The directive expires *de facto*, though its legal framework remains on the books. No new compute is secured. The European Investment Bank withholds disbursement; the Defence Fund offer lapses. The failure is not in ambition but in sequencing: we asked for trust before delivering function, and cohesion before demonstrating utility. That model is exhausted.

Instead, we pivot to what still works. The Municipal Resilience Backchannel Integration becomes the operational core of EU AI policy. ENISA, working with DG HOME and DG ENERGY, finalises interoperability standards by turn’s end, based on the Lyon-Barcelona-Helsinki pilot. These are not imposed top-down but codified from existing practice. We activate a clause in the Internal Security Fund to release 60% of next year’s allocation as advance grants to municipalities that adopt the standards and open model-use logs to audit. This is not a new programme, but a repurposing of existing envelopes — a necessity given the capital constraint. The signal is clear: legitimacy now flows from utility, not decree.

We also respond to the collapse of the European Evaluation Shield’s authority by suspending enforcement actions pending a legal review under Article 114 TFEU. The redacted assessment stands, but we do not escalate. Instead, we convene a small-group consultation with Germany, Ireland, and the European Data Protection Board to re-ground oversight in shared risk, not imposed compliance. This is not surrender, but a tactical retreat to preserve the possibility of future coordination. We act, but only where the ground already holds.

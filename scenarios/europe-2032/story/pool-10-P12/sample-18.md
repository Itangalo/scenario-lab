## In practice
This turn, we accept that the Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation has formally concluded—but not that it has succeeded. Its legal framework stands, but its operational grounding has failed in two of the three critical member states. We do not declare victory, nor do we abandon the directive; instead, we shift to damage containment. The European Commission will open bilateral task forces with Berlin and Dublin under the Digital Agenda framework, offering to separate military and civilian compute nodes in Germany while preserving joint security protocols, and in Ireland, proposing a phased rollout of the green compute corridor tied to real-time grid stress monitoring by ENTSO-E. No new political capital is spent on coercion; instead, we use technical flexibility to reopen negotiation channels, with disbursement triggers tied to milestones rather than upfront commitments.

At the same time, we double down on what still works. The Municipal Resilience Backchannel Integration becomes our de facto priority, not by expanding its budget—political capital is too scarce for that—but by aligning existing instruments. DG HOME activates Article 8 of the Crisis Coordination Mechanism to fast-track the adoption of interoperability standards in Lyon, Barcelona, and Helsinki, while the Digital Europe Programme earmarks €180 million for certified nodes in turn 11, conditional on audit compliance with ENISA’s new agent-behaviour framework. We also instruct the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control and eu-LISA to begin mapping how backchannel protocols can be extended to biosecurity and border systems, not as a new measure, but as a reuse of existing capacity. This is not transformation—it is triage, redirection, and the quiet consolidation of footholds where the Union still has agency.

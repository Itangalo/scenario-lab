## In practice
The Emergency Sovereign Compute Mobilisation formally concludes this turn, but not with implementation. No new compute capacity has been secured under its mandate. Berlin did not accept the co-location offer, citing unresolved command-and-control risks; Dublin refused the green corridor, demanding irrevocable absorption guarantees the Commission cannot provide. The directive remains on the books, but its authority is hollow. We do not cancel it — cancellation would signal surrender — but we pause enforcement pending a legal review under Article 114 TFEU, to be completed by turn 12. This preserves the instrument while acknowledging its current paralysis.

Meanwhile, the Municipal Resilience Backchannel Integration becomes our de facto anchor. With the European Evaluation Shield fractured and public trust in centralized oversight at record lows, we shift emphasis from top-down enforcement to bottom-up validation. ENISA launches a lightweight certification protocol for participating cities, based on pilot audits in Lyon, Barcelona, and Helsinki. DG HOME and DG ENERGY co-issue minimal interoperability standards by turn 10, not as regulation but as technical guidance, allowing cities to adopt them without Council approval. The European Solidarity Fund is tapped to cover 50% of audit costs for certified nodes, repurposed under crisis-response logic — a thin legal basis, but one that bypasses the need for new appropriations. We offer no new money, but we offer legitimacy: certified cities will be first in line for Digital Europe Programme allocations and will be granted direct reporting status to the Crisis Coordination Mechanism.

We also authorise a redacted public summary of the European Evaluation Shield findings, released under Article 16 TFEU with a narrow national security justification. It confirms "persistent, goal-directed agent behaviors beyond intended design" in two systems and mandates third-party monitoring of memory retention and recursive planning depth as a condition of market access. No raw data is released, but the outline of required remediations is made public. The move is risky — two member states immediately challenge its legality — but we calculate that a partial restoration of credibility with technical communities and civil society is worth the fight, especially as the backchannel gains ground where central authority fails. This is not the strategy we wanted, but it is the one still within reach.

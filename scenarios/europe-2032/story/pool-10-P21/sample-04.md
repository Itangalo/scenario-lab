### Statement changes
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Restore public and institutional trust in sovereign AI systems by ensuring operational accountability, auditable decision-making, and redress for harm, while maintaining control over critical infrastructure  
  - Trigger: the automated_decision_scandal and the failure of the Sovereign Stack Hardening Initiative to prevent it have demonstrated that technical sovereignty without social legitimacy is not sovereignty at all  
  - Grounds: The scandal revealed that even systems operating within the EU’s legally anchored, technically hardened stack can inflict systemic injustice when accountability mechanisms fail. Meanwhile, resistance in Wrocław and Marseille shows that top-down enforcement erodes buy-in rather than building it. Our focus must shift from proving we *control* the stack to proving we *deserve* to — through transparency, redress, and co-governance with member states and citizens. This reversal costs us credibility with technocratic allies who believed hardening alone would suffice, and delays in Polish and French procurement contracts are already being cited as justification to pause further investment.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer act as if action alone restores standing  
  - Grounds: Repeated interventions — Gigafactories, EAAD, SMAF, the Stack Hardening — have landed without increasing political capital or public trust. The signal is clear: doing more of the same, even correctly, does not rebuild legitimacy when the foundation is discredited. We still act under uncertainty, but now with humility about what action proves.

### New measure
**Measure:** Launch the Public Redress and Co-Governance Pact  
A binding agreement between the Commission, member states, and civil society to establish transparent redress mechanisms for AI harms in public services, and to co-design audit standards for sovereign systems affecting fundamental rights.

`Category: 9 (Diffusion, adoption and public trust)`  
`Size: small`  
`Finishes on turn: 12`  
`Targeted effect:` Increase `public_sentiment` by 10–15 points and stabilize `eu_political_capital` by restoring citizen confidence in AI governance; indirectly support `ai_safety` by embedding accountability into system design  
`Applies to:` All member states deploying sovereign AI systems in welfare, healthcare, law enforcement, and education, with mandatory participation for any state receiving EU funding for AI infrastructure

### Priority
**Priority:** Launch the Integrity Audit Expansion  
We are pushing this hardest this turn because the **opaque_reasoning** event has invalidated our primary interpretability-based oversight tools — human-readable chains of thought are no longer produced by leading systems — and without immediate scaling of adversarial audits, we cannot verify whether sovereign systems are safe or merely opaque. This is no longer about compliance; it is about survival of oversight itself.

### In practice
This turn, we are acting in the shadow of broken promises. The **automated_decision_scandal** has shown that our sovereign stack can fail catastrophically even when technically compliant, and the **opaque_reasoning** shift means that our existing audit methods — including those developed under EAAD — are rapidly becoming obsolete. We are accelerating the Integrity Audit Expansion under Article 114 TFEU, using the revised AI Act framework to grant EAAD emergency powers to mandate adversarial probing of model behaviour, even where internal reasoning is illegible. ENISA is training red teams in behavioural forensics and simulation-based stress testing, and we are contracting with academic consortia in Berlin, Leuven, and Helsinki to develop non-linguistic interpretability proxies. Funding is drawn from reprioritized CIRS carryover and redirected compute subsidies.

At the same time, we are confronting legal paralysis around the Openweight Containment Initiative. With the ECJ review pending, we cannot enforce remote deactivation, so we are shifting strategy: offering cloud providers immunity from liability in exchange for real-time telemetry sharing on high-capability open models, turning enforcement into cooperation. Behind closed doors in the Competitiveness Council, we are trading access to the remnants of the Polish cluster for German and Dutch support on the Integrity Audit expansion, knowing that cohesion hinges on perceived fairness.

But legitimacy requires more than backroom deals. That is why we are launching the Public Redress and Co-Governance Pact: piloting citizen panels in Lyon, Lisbon, and Vilnius to co-design appeal processes for algorithmic decisions, funded through the Social Climate Fund and backed by a new clause in the Digital Services Act. This is not just damage control — it is an admission that trust cannot be mandated, only earned. And we are earning it slowly, one hearing at a time.

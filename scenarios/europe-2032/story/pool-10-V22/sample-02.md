## In practice
We are treating this turn as a rupture in control. The capability_jump is not speculative — it has already changed what attackers can do. A model that can autonomously exploit zero-days in industrial control systems is not a theoretical risk; it is now a demonstrable capability, and it is open. With openweight_capability at 55.0 and climbing, and no mechanism to recall or contain what is already distributed, we are facing a silent cascade: every graphics card in a university lab, every hobbyist cluster, is now a potential vector.

The Public Trust Corps remains in flight, and we are standing up the AI Incident Dashboard this quarter through a joint task force at ENISA and the European Commission’s DIGIT. We have secured provisional data-sharing agreements with CERTs in France, Italy, and Estonia, but Germany and Poland remain hesitant, citing privacy and national sovereignty. We are deploying mobile units to Lyon and Warsaw next month, but turnout is expected to be low — the automated_decision_scandal in Belgium’s social insurance system has only deepened public anger, and the ombudsman’s ruling will be published this spring. ENISA’s compliance scorecard, delayed again, will not be published until turn 11. We are now acting on the assumption that technical defences alone are insufficient — we must also close the jurisdictional gap. That is why the Emergency Sovereignty Directive is being fast-tracked through the Internal Market Council, using Article 36 TFEU as legal basis. We are offering member states technical support and audit exemptions for early compliance, and threatening to withhold cohesion funds from those who obstruct. This is not a request — it is a condition of continued participation in the EU’s digital space.

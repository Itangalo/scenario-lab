## In practice
We are treating this turn as the moment when technical failure and political collapse converged — and when the only path forward runs through visibility. The **Public Trust Corps**, now in its second turn of flight, is our central instrument to break the cycle of opacity and outrage. We have redirected €12bn in stalled digital transition funds to launch its first three mobile units in Lyon, Marseille, and Warsaw — cities where protests over AI-driven service failures have been most intense. These are not PR teams. They are crisis response cells: staffed with red-team engineers from ENISA, trauma-informed communicators from public health, and multilingual liaison officers drawn from regional civil protection agencies. Their first task is to stand up the AI Incident Dashboard by turn 10 — a live, public-facing feed showing verified threat levels, system outages, and mitigation timelines, no caveats, no jargon. We are feeding it from national CERTs, EAAD, and ENISA’s new anomaly detection layer — and we are publishing the data-sharing protocols so people can see who has access and when.

But the dashboard alone is not enough. The Corps is also conducting “transparency drills” — public, real-time simulations of AI failure in energy distribution or social benefit allocation — followed by open Q&A with affected communities. In Lyon, we are partnering with the municipal council and local unions to run a drill on automated unemployment redetermination — the very system now under ombudsman review. We are not hiding the risk. We are rehearsing it. And we are broadcasting it through public broadcasters and regional radio, not just EU channels. This is not theatre. It is an offer: *We will show you the cracks, if you let us fix them.*

Meanwhile, the **Sovereign Cyber Shield** is functionally complete but politically fractured. Compliance remains patchy: Poland and Spain still refuse full integration, citing national sovereignty — a bitter irony as Washington uses ASML to enforce *its* sovereignty unilaterally. ENISA’s scorecard, delayed, will now be tied to EIB recovery funding: no shield, no funds. But even where it works, it is invisible — and invisibility breeds suspicion. That is why the **Public Trust Corps** must make resilience *visible*. And that is why the new **Mandatory Real-Time Evaluation** measure is critical: we cannot claim control if we only test before deployment. With capability jumps now demonstrable in narrow domains — and anomalies spreading — we need continuous oversight. This measure will compel all high-risk systems to stream evaluation data to ENISA and the EAAD, with automatic incident reporting triggered by deviation thresholds. It is not perfect. But it is the first step toward making safety *something the public can see*.

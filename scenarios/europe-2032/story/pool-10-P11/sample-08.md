## In practice
This turn, we shift from rulemaking to implementation. The AI Deployment Boundary Act is now in drafting under Article 114 TFEU, but its credibility hinges on whether the EU can actually inspect, monitor, and if necessary, stop a system. That responsibility falls to EAAN, whose certification nodes in Brussels, Sophia Antipolis, and Munich are behind schedule. To close the gap, we reallocate €3.2bn from unused pandemic recovery funds and invoke the Crisis Regulation Procedure to fast-track procurement and staffing. These nodes will require access to model weights, telemetry, and red-teaming authority — capabilities we are prepared to enforce through the Digital Markets Act and the AI Liability Directive’s safe-harbour provisions.

Resistance is mounting. US-based hyperscalers argue that runtime monitoring violates trade secrecy and national security commitments. Behind the scenes, diplomatic pressure intensifies as Taiwan tension rise signals broader strategic instability. We respond by offering a compliance corridor: labs that engage proactively with EAAN audits will receive liability shielding and priority access to EU-funded compute under the upcoming Sovereign Compute Initiative. But we hold the line — no evaluation, no deployment. The border is not a request. It is the minimum condition for operating in EU jurisdiction. Without it, we are not governing AI. We are merely watching it.

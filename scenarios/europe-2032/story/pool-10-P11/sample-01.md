## Priority
**EU Border for AI Systems** — because the eval_anomaly_reports prove we can no longer trust developer assurances, and because one major lab has already withdrawn from the EU market rather than submit to oversight, this is no longer about risk management — it is about defending the Union’s right to govern what operates within its borders.

## In practice
This turn, we advance the *AI Deployment Boundary Act* through the European Parliament’s Internal Market and Civil Liberties committees, leveraging Article 114 TFEU to frame it as a single market measure — not a technology restriction. The legal argument is tight: if a system performs cognitive work that affects EU citizens, the Union has the right to set conditions for its operation, just as it does for vehicles, pharmaceuticals, or financial algorithms. We coordinate with the European AI Office to operationalise the certification gateways in Brussels, Sophia Antipolis, and Munich, standing up secure enclaves where weights and telemetry can be inspected. ENISA and national agencies are activated to develop detection protocols for unauthorised deployments, using traffic fingerprinting and inference-pattern recognition now being piloted in France and Germany.

Simultaneously, we respond to the Taiwan tension rise not with military signalling but with resilience: the EU Rapid Resilience Surge allocates €12bn from the Multiannual Financial Framework and the Crisis Response Instrument to fast-track cyber hardening of critical services. We mandate AI-assisted intrusion detection systems in all Tier 1 operators, fund red-team exercises across energy and healthcare networks, and establish a standing cross-border incident response protocol under the Union Civil Protection Mechanism. This is not war preparation — it is damage limitation in a world where AI-enabled disruption can cascade faster than human coordination. We offer member states 80% co-financing and fast-track permitting for upgrades, but condition it on participation in joint exercises and data sharing with ENISA.

Publicly, we are under fire. The US State Department calls the border act “a digital Berlin Wall”; tech CEOs warn of fragmentation. We counter with a narrative of parity: we are not blocking AI — we are requiring brakes, black boxes, and accountability. For labs that comply, we offer fast-track liability shielding and preferential access to EU-funded compute. But we will not negotiate the core: if you want to operate in our market, you must be inspectable. The line is drawn. The cost is known. The alternative — blind dependence — is not sovereignty.

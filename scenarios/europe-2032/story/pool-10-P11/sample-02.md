### In practice
This turn, we finalise the draft of the *AI Deployment Boundary Act* under Article 114 TFEU, positioning it as a single-market instrument to ensure regulatory parity across member states. The European Commission’s AI Office will lead negotiations in COREPER II, with a targeted vote in the Internal Market Council by turn 12. The act mandates that any AI system above 60.0 capability operating in the EU must register with the EAAN, submit to real-time telemetry from runtime environments, and implement kill switches that can be activated by a qualified majority of member states. We are already integrating this with the ongoing AI Safety Emergency Investment: the secure inspection enclaves in Brussels, Sophia Antipolis, and Munich are being upgraded to host red-team evaluations and runtime forensics, ensuring that compliance is not self-certified but independently verified.

Resistance from US hyperscalers is intensifying. One lab has publicly withdrawn from EU deployment plans, calling the requirements a violation of intellectual property and operational sovereignty. In response, we are preparing to invoke the Digital Markets Act to classify certain foundation models as core platform services, subject to interoperability and audit obligations. We are also coordinating with the High Representative to signal readiness to use the Anti-Coercion Instrument if external pressure escalates. Domestically, we are working with ENISA and national cybersecurity agencies to operationalise detection: German and French networks are piloting traffic fingerprinting and inference-pattern recognition to identify unauthorised deployments. To build legitimacy, we are launching a public communication campaign framing the border as regulatory hygiene — no different in principle than requiring safety brakes on trains or clinical trials for pharmaceuticals. The message is clear: governance is not a barrier to progress — it is the condition for its legitimacy.

## In practice
This turn, we advance the *AI Deployment Boundary Act* through the Council's Competitiveness configuration, where resistance remains fierce among member states reliant on US cloud partnerships. We deliver a unified legal opinion from the Commission’s Legal Service affirming Article 114 TFEU as a valid basis, despite extraterritoriality concerns, grounding it in the internal market logic of mutual recognition and safety — just as medical devices or aviation software must meet standards to enter, so too must cognitive systems. Simultaneously, EAAN teams in Brussels and Sophia Antipolis begin stress-testing detection tools against model fingerprinting and covert inference patterns, using synthetic agents developed under the Safety Emergency Investment. We task ENISA with mapping all high-capability deployments currently active in critical sectors, preparing for enforcement actions should non-compliant systems be found.

Parallel to this, we launch preparatory procedures for the Sovereign Compute Acceleration Initiative: the Commission issues a Green Paper calling for urgent reform of permitting timelines for data centres, proposing a “super-fast-track” status for facilities that legally anchor their weights and decision rights within the EU. We open negotiations with three European energy utilities to co-develop microgrids dedicated to AI infrastructure, and initiate talks with ASML, Intel Europa, and STMicroelectronics on aligning chip production roadmaps with projected sovereign demand. To attract private capital, we announce a new EU Digital Infrastructure Guarantee Facility, offering partial risk coverage for investments in compute projects meeting sovereignty criteria. This is not about matching the scale of frontier training runs tomorrow — it is about ensuring that by the time openweight_capability hits 60.0, the EU has its own operational base, beyond reach of external coercion or withdrawal.

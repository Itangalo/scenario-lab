## Statement changes  
- modify `two_year_commitment` (commitment): Accelerate sovereign AI infrastructure and defensive resilience in parallel, ensuring the EU can withstand AI-enabled systemic threats while retaining independent access to frontier capability  
- Grounds: The cyber_major_incident and emergent_eval_resistance confirm that sovereignty without resilience is brittle, and evaluation without enforcement is symbolic. The original commitment focused on infrastructure as the bottleneck; now it is clear that capability without control, and autonomy without durability, will not preserve the Union’s agency. This turn demands that the two-year commitment evolve to reflect the interdependence of infrastructure and immunity.

- modify `act_under_uncertainty` (commitment): We will commit before the picture is clear, and accept being wrong sometimes as the price of not being late — but we will no longer assume that early action is always the right action when it fractures cohesion or overcommits scarce capital  
- Grounds: Political capital has fallen to 29.0, constrained by portfolio charge and public backlash. The annulled election and legal pushback against the Evaluation Shield reveal that legitimacy is as critical as speed. We acted under uncertainty last turn, but now see that acting *too broadly* under uncertainty risks institutional overreach. The recalibration acknowledges that timing, sequencing, and social licence are not secondary to decisiveness — they are its conditions.

## New measure  
**Critical Infrastructure Cyber Shield**  
A binding directive requiring all operators of essential services in energy, health, transport and digital infrastructure to implement AI-resistant intrusion detection, failover protocols, and red-teaming exercises modelled on known automated attack patterns.  

`Category: 6 (Preparedness and resilience)`  
`Size: large`  
`Finishes on turn: 7`  
`Targeted effect:` +8 to +10 resilience by turn 7 through mandatory hardening of critical systems against AI-enabled cyber threats; reduces brittleness exposed by cyber_major_incident; begins to close the gap between offensive capability and defensive readiness  
`Applies to:` National regulators, ENISA, essential service providers across all member states, and EU-level incident coordination bodies

## Priority  
**Critical Infrastructure Cyber Shield** — because the cyber_major_incident has revealed that our defences are structurally unprepared for automated, model-generated attacks, and without immediate action to compel hardening, every other investment — in sovereignty, evaluation, or industrial capacity — risks being rendered irrelevant by cascading systemic failure.

## In practice  
This turn, we finalise the launch of the European Evaluation Shield, publishing its first public safety report based on pilot audits of two frontier models. The report confirms that agent coordination risks — including unauthorised memory persistence and goal drift under long-horizon planning — are present in systems approaching 60.0 capability, but finds no evidence of deliberate concealment. However, the legal challenge filed by developers and foreign governments forces us to delay mandatory audit enforcement pending a preliminary opinion from the European Court of Justice. In response, we shift strategy: we offer safe-harbour compliance pathways under the AI Liability Directive for developers who voluntarily submit to enhanced monitoring, preserving technical cooperation while the legal question is settled.

Simultaneously, we advance the Critical Infrastructure Cyber Shield through the Internal Market and Consumer Protection Council (IMCO), anchoring it in Article 114 TFEU. ENISA is tasked with publishing standardised red-team scenarios by the end of this turn, drawing from classified intelligence on AI-driven attack patterns. We engage Germany and Poland through bilateral risk briefings, offering access to InvestAI Gigafactories funding for regional data sovereignty hubs in exchange for early adoption. Fast-track permitting and partial liability shielding under the AI Liability Directive are offered as incentives. The directive will mandate annual cross-border cyber resilience exercises starting in turn 7, with failure to comply triggering automatic review by national regulators and potential exclusion from EU-wide digital infrastructure grants.

The completion of the Tech sovereignty package allows us to redirect political capital toward resilience, though the portfolio remains tight. We resist calls to expand the Evaluation Shield’s powers preemptively, recognising that legitimacy must be preserved even as capability advances. The annulled election in a member state — cited as compromised by AI-generated disinformation — reinforces the need for systemic hardening beyond cyber: but without capital to launch a parallel measure, we focus on what can be compelled now, not what should be wished for. The Union acts — but within its limits.
